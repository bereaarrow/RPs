Abstract
Purpose–Thepurposeofthisstudyistofindthesusceptibilityofconfirmationbiasanditsrelationshipsamong intolerance of uncertainty (IU), perceived stress, and emotional-personality-related career decision-making difficulties (EPCD), through a serial mediation analysis.
Design/methodology/approach – The study employed a structural equation modelling (SEM) approach using R Studio (version 4.4.3). The study employed a cross-sectional design, involving 378 final-year undergraduate students from rural regions of three southern Indian states, who were selected through purposive sampling. Findings– The findingsrevealed thatstudentsexhibit a tendency towardsconfirmation bias, and intolerance of uncertainty (IU) positively influences emotional and personality-related career decision-making difficulties (EPCD), as well as confirmation bias through perceived stress. Additionally, the results support the proposed serial mediation model, indicating that IU increases susceptibility to confirmation bias via its impact on perceived stress and, subsequently, EPCD.
Researchlimitations/implications–Thisstudymakestheoreticalcontributionstothefieldsofcareerdecision- makingandcognitivepsychology,aligningwithkeySustainableDevelopmentGoals(SDGs),particularlySDG 4 (quality education) and SDG 8 (decent work and economic growth). The findings offeractionable insights for researchers, career counsellors, and policymakers to design targeted interventions that reduce career decision- making difficulties and mitigate the tendency toward confirmation bias, thereby promoting more informed and quality decision-making.
Originality/value – This study extends existing models by introducing perceived stress and EPCD as serial mediators between intolerance of uncertainty and confirmation bias. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first study to explore this specific mediation pathway in the context of career decision-making among students from rural communities, providing valuable insights to the fields of cognitive psychology and career development.

2. Literature review
2.1 Theoretical background TheCognitive-AffectiveConativemodel(KimandChen,2021)servesasthetheoreticalbasis for this investigation. It describes how cognitive and emotional factors are activated sequentially, involving the psychological process of decision-making that influences an individual’s behaviour. When choosing a specialisation, selecting relevant courses, and exploring alternative career paths, students often encounter uncertain situations. Their intolerance for uncertainty frequently becomes overwhelming, leading to elevated stress and psychological discomfort (Buhr and Dugas, 2002; Carleton et al., 2007, 2016). It also intensifies anxiety, low self-efficacy, and career indecision (Saka and Gati, 2007). According to research, people are more likely to engage in motivated reasoning and biased information processing as coping mechanisms when they are faced with stress, anxiety, and uncertainty (Proulx et al., 2012).
2.2 Hypothesis development
2.2.1 Intolerance of uncertainty (IU) and Confirmation bias. The rapid market changes have increased career instability, which is reflected in the growing diversity of career paths, specialised professions, and employment opportunities (Schulz et al., 2015; Gati and Levin, 2014). Intolerance of uncertainty (IU) refers to a trait of an individual (Rosen et al., 2014) and isacognitiveprocessthatencodesandinterpretsinformationandrespondstotheworldaround them, characterised by a future-oriented element marked by unpredictability (Grenier et al., 2005). Prior research studies associate IU with impulsive, affect-driven decisions that favour immediate, suboptimal options, indicating impaired judgement under uncertainty, which may exacerbate emotional discomfort and cognitive strain (Buhr and Dugas, 2002; Dugas et al., 2005; Luhmann et al., 2011). The studies show that IU significantly contributes to increased interpretation bias (Tyson et al., 2025), particularly in a social context (Lauriola et al., 2024). This study particularly focuses on confirmation bias, which is one of the behavioural biases that significantly contributes to the development of overconfidence, especially when people face uncertainty regarding a conclusion. It is the propensity to give more weight to data that
K confirms one’s preexisting opinions while ignoring data to the contrary (Rassin, 2008). For
instance, a previous study found that medical students are susceptible to confirmation bias during the rapid diagnostic process under time constraints (Kojima et al., 2025). Individuals are especially vulnerable to this bias under such conditions, leading them to be strongly inflexibleintheirinitialbeliefsevenwhenpresentedwithnew,conflictinginformation,which can result in an overstated sense of confidence in their judgements and decisions (Rabin and Schrag, 1999). With this argument, we propose:
 H1. IU is positively associated with confirmation bias.

3. Methods
3.1 Participants
The participants of thisstudy comprise 55.5% females and 44.5%males. In terms of age, 89% of the respondents fall within the 20–22 age group, 9% are between 23–26 years, and 2% are above 26 years. The participants are final year students enrolled in undergraduate programs across various disciplines: 12% in arts, 30% in science, 37% in commerce, and 21% in management. In socio-economic research, annual family incomes are broadly defined and interpreted in relation to the locally established socio-economic status scales (Masthi and Kulkarni, 2013). In this study, the participants belong to lower–to middle–income households located in rural areas of two Indian states. Rural communities are referred to as students enrolled in institutions located outside metropolitan and major urban regions. This population predominantly belongs to agriculture and related occupations (Tilak and Choudhury, 2021). Regarding annual family income, 89% of the respondents come from families earning below ₹8 lakh (approximately US$9,400), while 11% come from families earning above ₹8 lakh. Additionally, 77% of the participants reported not having attended any career guidance program, whereas 23% indicated they had attended at least one program during their course.
3.2 Procedure
In this study, we collected data using a mixed approach that combines both paper-and-pencil surveys and online questionnaires employing purposive sampling techniques. Students who belong to undergraduate courses in non-urban, rural areas were recruited for the study. Weapproachedacademicinstitutionslocatedinruralregionsandgotpermissiontocollectdata fromstudents.Thepapersurveyswereadministeredinaclassroomsetting,whereparticipants were first briefed on the purpose of the study. They were assured that their responses would remain confidential and were encouraged to ask questions or clarify any doubts before filling out the questionnaire. This process helped create a comfortable environment and encouraged betterparticipation.Studentswhowerenotphysicallyaccessiblewerereachedusinganonline survey through LinkedIn and emails. This increases the diversity of the sample and improves participation. Both modes used identical questionnaires to ensure measurement equivalence.
3.3 Ethical considerations
Before initiating the research, formal approval was obtained from the local institutional authorities. The approval process involved a thorough review of the research proposal to ensure compliance with ethical principles, including respect for participants, informed consent, confidentiality, and transparency. Participation was entirely voluntary, and students were selected accordingly.

3.6 Common method bias
For data collection, this study employed a self-report method, necessitating an assessment of the potential threatto validity posed bycommon method bias, also known as commonmethod variance. The authors followed the procedures and statistical methods proposed by Podsakoff et al. (2012) to eliminate this problem. Harman’s one-factor test was used to assess the common method bias. The results show that a single factor accounted for 37.9% of the total variance,whichisbelowtherecommendedthresholdof50%,suggestingthatcommonmethod bias is not a threat to this study. Additionally, we included the common latent factor (CLF) in theconfirmatoryfactoranalysismodel,withallobservedindicatorsloadingontheirrespective theoreticalconstructs andthelatent methodfactor.TheintroductionoftheCLFindicated only slight changesin modelfitcompared to the baseline measurement model (CFI5 0.954 versus 0.941;RMSEA5 0.043versus0.047).Particularly,themainfactorloadingsremainedlargely consistent after the inclusion of the CLF. Overall, these results suggest that common method variance is not significantly biasing the study’s findings.

5.2 Practical implications
The study has provided meaningful practical implications, particularly for university career guidance centres, counselling cells, and mentors, who play a pivotal role in addressing the
cognitive and emotional factors underlying students’ career indecision (Santos et al., 2018). Kybernetes Gatlin et al. (2018) emphasised that the universities, educators, and policymakers of the institutesshouldprioritisestudent-centredcareerdevelopmentprogramsforstudentswholack

A previous study highlighted that students from low-income, underserved communities who areallstudyinginruralschoolslackaccesstocareer-relatedinformation,trainingoptions,and adequate hands-on learning (Lindstrom et al., 2022). The study’s findings offer valuable direction for educators, policymakers, and community stakeholders seeking to foster more resilient, reflective, and empowered decision-makers in under-resourced settings.
6. Limitations and future research
Although the study contributes theoretical and practical implications, it has its limitations as well, which can guide future research. The study explored the susceptibility of confirmation bias among students during their career decision-making process, particularly when they face challenges in making decisions. It provided suggestions to implement career interventions, bias mitigation strategies, and mindfulness training programs, but it did not provide practical methodstosuggest.Theparticipants forthestudy werechosenfromruralpartsofthree Indian
states,whichisnotarepresentationoftheentirepopulation.Also,thisisacross-sectional,self- reported questionnaire that was used for data collection, which limits the ability to infer causality. More experimental and longitudinal studies are needed to develop mitigation or intervention strategies to improve the students’ decision-making quality in career-related
K concerns, which could reveal whether confirmation bias diminishes with increased exposure
to decision-making scenarios.
For instance, structured reflection tasks, premortem analysis, or training in metacognitive strategies may help reduce biased reasoning. Testing such interventions in real-world educational and counselling settings can offer practical implications for improving student decision quality. Future studies should investigate how these cognitive and emotional factors vary across different socio-cultural and geographic settings. For example, rural versus urban backgrounds and collectivist versus individualist cultures may help to better understand how cultural context amplifies or mitigates decision difficulties and confirmation bias. Along with that,otherbehaviouralandcognitivebiasescanbeexplored,andhowthesebiasesinteractwith careerdecision-makingdifficulties.UpcomingstudiescanexplorehowotherSDGgoals,such aspovertyandinequalities,interactwiththeseelements.Withstudentsincreasinglyrelyingon digital platforms and algorithm-driven content, future research should explore how such environmentsreinforcebiasedcareerbeliefs.Finally,morepotentialvariablescanbeincluded, suchaspeersupportandsocialsupport,whichcanbetreatedasamoderatingvariableinfuture studies. The findings are especially valuable for career counsellors, educators, and policymakers, as they point to specific psychological barriers that hinder optimal decision- making. By addressing these barriers, interventions can bedeveloped to foster more reflective and rational decision-making processes, thus improving career outcomes and individual well-being.
