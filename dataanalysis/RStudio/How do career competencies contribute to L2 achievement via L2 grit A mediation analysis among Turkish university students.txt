   ABSTRACT  
   Considering  the  intriguing  link  between  career  growth  and  second
   language  (L2),  this  research  aims  to  promote  cross-disciplinary
   understanding  by  linking  career  development  with  language  learning
   outcomes  that  are  relevant  to  educational  guidance  and  university
   support  services. In this regard,  the  influence of career competencies  on
   L2 achievement  was explored.  In this study, the  mediating  effects of the
   dimensions  of  L2  grit  — speci cally,  perseverance  of  effort  and
   consistency  of interest  — were  examined  between  career  competencies 
   and  L2 achievement  scores.  The  research  involved  290  tertiary-level
   Turkish EFL students  who  participated  voluntarily.  Structural  equation

   consistency  of interest  did  not  serve  as a mediator  in this relationship.
   These  ndings  provide  career  practitioners  and  language  educators

Introduction
In an era characterised by globalisation and increasing interconnectedness, pro ciency in additional languages,  even extending  beyond  just a second  language  (L2), has emerged  as a critical asset  for university students  career development across various industries and professions. This is particularly prominent  in developing  countries,  including  T rkiye,  especially  given  the  upward  trajectory  of youth  unemployment  rates  (Taş et al., 2018), coupled  with the  growing  annual  influx of graduate students  (Adıg zel,  2021). In this scenario,  the  English language,  recognised  as the  global  lingua franca (Crystal, 2003) and the international language for business (Bloch, 1995), carries considerable signi cance  in T rkiye,  where  it is taught  as a foreign  language  (EFL). Acknowledging  this, edu- cational policies in T rkiye have long mandated  the  inclusion of English in the  centralised  curricu- lum,  starting  from  the  second  grade  in  primary  schools  through  the  rst  year  of  tertiary education.  Currently, this asset,  that  is, L2 pro ciency,  eases  university enrolment  and  graduation for particular programmes, serves as a requirement for pursuing academic studies and international education, and, predictably, guarantees quali cation  for job opportunities  and career advancement 
in both  public and private sectors. In this respect,  the following statement  would likely be elucidat- ing and still relevant:  A foreign language  is not an open  ticket to the position  of one s dreams, but

the  message  from  the  growing  number  of  businesses  looking  abroad  is that  a  graduate  with language  skills, particularly one with a measure  of professional  training, is at a de nite advantage  (Financial Times, 1990, p. 43)
The signi cance of pro ciency in L2, a fundamental requirement for entering the workforce (B re-
šov , 2009), has been furthered in the European Commission s multilingual policy aimed at fostering 
a functional  multilingual  economy  (Hrehova, 2010). Moreover, several  studies  elaborated  on  this matter  of  concern.  For  example,  Sindik and  Božinović (2013) reported  the  importance  of  the mastery  of foreign  languages  for a career  in the  tourism  sector  in Croatia, while Shrestha  et  al. (2015) emphasised the merit of L2 English pro ciency for the career prospects of Nepalese engineer -
ing students.  Additionally, Peltokorpi  (2023) presented  evidence  supporting  the  direct  correlation between  L2 English pro ciency  and  job  promotions,  salary increases,  and  career  satisfaction  in the  Japanese  context.  Along similar lines, Pudelko  and  Tenzer (2019) uncovered  both  direct  and indirect effects of language barriers as an impediment to global career opportunities in international business  schools across four countries.  Finally, Miranda and  Umhoefer  (1998) identi ed  L2 English pro ciency  and  acculturation  as predictors  of career  self-efficacy among  Latino individuals in the United States.
L2 pro ciency,  therefore,  can be regarded  as a necessary  attribute  that  expands  both  domestic and  international  career opportunities.  It provides  individuals with access to global work contexts, enhancing  professional  mobility, which is notable  for building  sustainable  careers. Hartung  and Di Fabio  (2024) argue  that  career  development  has  entered  a  fourth  paradigm,  emphasising  that careers should  be considered  not only in terms of individual success but also in relation to societal well-being and the promotion of decent work. Within this framework, L2 pro ciency can function as 
a connective  resource  that  links societies, enhances  individuals  flexibility in the  face of precarious working  conditions,  and  strengthens  collective  awareness,  thereby  supporting  the  design  of active  lives (Guichard, 2022). For the  purposes  of the  current  study,  however,  our  focus shifts to approaching  the  nexus  of career  development  and  L2 asset  from  the  opposite  perspective.  The premise  is that  as  individuals  feel competent  in pursuing  their  career  and  professional  growth, they  recognise  the  signi cance  of  succeeding  in  L2 to  enhance  their  competitiveness  in  the global  job market  by deriving  potential  bene ts  from particular  personality  traits. In other  words, self-perceived  career competencies,  as a professional  driving force, could be motivating  individuals to attain L2 achievement through the mediating effect of personality attributes, alongside numerous factors and social, psychological, and instructional motives. From such a perspective, we view career competencies  as a valuable  asset  that  could influence  L2 achievement  through  a personality  trait, namely grit, a social psychological  construct  linked to academic  and  career success (Steuber  et al., 2019). The existing research  has not yet directly examined  the integration  of the career competen - cies framework with L2 learning  within a model.  Although  career  competencies  have been  exten- sively studied  in relation  to  a  range  of academic  and  career-related  outcomes  (Jo et  al., 2024), their  role  in  L2 learning  remains  largely  underexplored.  In  this  study,  we  intend  to  examine whether  university  students   career  competencies  would  predict  their  L2 English  achievement through  the  mediating  mechanism  of language-domain-speci c grit.
Grit and achievement
Grit, characterised  as  perseverance  and  passion  for long-term  goals   (Duckworth  et  al., 2007, p. 1087), encompasses  the  continuous  pursuit  of objectives,  sustaining  relentless  determination  and enthusiasm for an extended duration, despite facing failures, setbacks, or periods of stagnation. Fre- drickson s (2004) broaden-and-build theory  suggests  that  positive traits, with grit being  no excep- tion,  contribute  to  academic  success  by  expanding  an  individual s  awareness  and  encouraging exploration,  and  thus  activate  cognitive  and  behavioural  processes.  Extending  the  seminal  work by Duckworth  et  al. (2007), which demonstrated  the  signi cance  of grit in different  life domains, the  number  of studies  mushroomed  to investigate  grit in relation  to success  in academic  as well 
as non-academic settings. The main impetus behind these studies was the conviction that grit plays 
a pivotal  role in determining  success  (Duckworth, 2017). In the  context  of educational  research, however,  the  examination  of grit has led to diverse results. Carried out across various instructional settings and academic disciplines, some of the studies indicated a relationship between grit and aca- demic outcomes (Bliss & Jacobson, 2020; Calo et al., 2022; Montas et al., 2021), whereas others have presented  conflicting results (Dixson, 2021; Steinmayr et al., 2018).
With respect  to research  on grit within the  L2 domain,  a recent  surge  of interest  has emerged among  scholars. The scholarly attention  has  primarily focused  on  exploring  the  potential  associ- ations  between  L2 learners   perseverance  and  passion,  termed  as  L2 grit,  which  is a  domain- speci c conceptualisation (Teimouri et al., 2020), and various L2 outcomes. While doing so, research- ers utilised both domain-general and language domain-speci c grit scales, with the former showing 
a decreased  likelihood of correlating  with L2 outcomes  (Li & Yang, 2023). Still, whether  assessed through  domain-general  or  domain-speci c  instruments,  a  notable  collection  of  studies  have demonstrated  signi cant  associations  between  L2 grit  as  a  unitary  construct  and  L2 outcomes. These investigations,  for instance, have identi ed signi cant  pathways  from grit to L2 achievement (Dehkordi, 2023; Li & Yang, 2023; Liu et al., 2022; Mikami, 2024; Teimouri et al., 2022), L2 pro ciency (Alrabai, 2022; Hu et al., 2022), and L2 performance  (Liu & Wang, 2021; Wei et al., 2019) across diverse contexts.  However, within the  existing literature,  discrepancies  emerged  when  the  components  of grit, namely perseverance  of effort (PoE) and consistency  of interest  (CoI), were treated  as separate factors in predicting  L2 outcomes.  These inconsistencies  manifested  in both  PoE and  CoI showing positive correlations with L2 achievement (Dehkordi et al., 2021), while neither component individu- ally predicted  L2 achievement  in Khajavy et al. (2020), coupled  with a reversal of roles in predictive capacity (Shirvan & Alamer, 2022; Sudina & Plonsky, 2021).
Career competencies  and grit
Career competencies  (CCs), the  ability to shape  one s own career (Akkermans et al., 2013), encom - pass a broad  range  of skills and attributes  essential  for effective career management and advance - ment.  Akkermans et al. (2013) identi ed  six career  competencies,  that  is, reflection on motivation, reflection  on  qualities,  networking,  self-pro ling,  work exploration,  and  career  control.  Likewise, the  career  development  framework  by  Hillage and  Pollard  (1998) identi es  key  competencies such  as  self-awareness,  decision-making,  goal  setting,  and  networking  as  crucial for career  pro- gression.  Career competencies  serve as valuable  resources  in the  journey  of career  development, providing  individuals with essential  tools and  skills necessary  for navigating  through  the  complex- ities of their professional  paths  (G rg l  & Bozgeyikli, 2024).
Traditionally, research  suggests  a signi cant  overlap  between  grit, primarily manifesting  in the antecedent  role, and  CCs and  career-related  outcomes,  wherein  grit  influences  career  decision- making  processes  and  chosen  career  paths  and  goals. Thus, grittier  individuals are more  likely to engage  in long-term  objectives  and career-related  behaviours  and functioning  (Lee & Sohn, 2017). For example,  previous  research  revealed  grits  relations  to career  decision  self-efficacy (Vela et al., 2018), career success and engagement (Lechner et al., 2019; Walker et al., 2016), career adaptability (Li et  al., 2021), career  stability (Duckworth & Quinn, 2009), career  exploration  (Datu et  al., 2017), career goals, goal setting and career maturity (Lytle & Shin, 2023; Ting & Datu, 2020), career construc - tion (Zhai et al., 2023), and career satisfaction (Popoola & Karadas, 2022). Moreover, grit served as an effective variable to mediate  the  links between  person-environment  t  and career success (Koeke- moer  et  al., 2023). Additionally, grit  stood  as  a signi cant  mediating  mechanism  to  consolidate the  association  between  career-related  behaviours,  that  is, employability  attributes  such as career agility, resilience  and  employability  competency  expectations  such  as  autonomy  and  personal employability  qualities (Ismail et al., 2023).
That being  said, in accordance  with our study s framework, prior research  also underscores  the predictive  nature  of  career-related  behaviours,  beyond  their  recognised  role  as  an  established outcome  variable. To exemplify, in Akkermans et al. (2018), career  resources  such as CCs and  career adaptability predicted students  life satisfaction, study engagement, and academic performance. Like- wise, in Lapan et al. (2016) which involved urban  primary school students,  engagement with an inte- grated English Language Arts (ELA) and career development curriculum highlighted the signi cance of career  agency  for students,  demonstrating  a correlation  with essential  indicators  of ELA pro ciency such as grades  and  standardised  test  scores. In another  targeted  investigation  (Sasaki et al., 2017), it was found that exposure to peers with ambitious career aspirations could elevate students  motivation 
to study  L2, even  in cases  where  their  initial motivation  levels are not  notably  high. Therefore, the present study examines the antecedent role of CCs in a speci c academic domain, namely L2 learning, 
by testing  the mediating  role of L2 grit. Building on previous research, it is expected  that career com- petencies  predict  L2 achievement  through  their positive association  with L2 grit.
Purpose of the study
Dedicated  to  expanding  on  the  empirical  base  established  in  earlier  works,  through  a  novel, uncharted  model  of parallel mediation,  this study investigates  the  predictive  capacity of CCs in L2 achievement  among  EFL learners,  and  the  differential mediating  functions  of L2 grit components (PoE and  CoI) in the  relationship  between  CCs and  L2 achievement.  To achieve  these  objectives, the  following hypotheses  were formulated: 
H1: CCs positively predict  PoE.
H2: CCs positively predict  CoI.
H3: PoE positively predicts  L2 achievement.
H4: CoI positively predicts  L2 achievement.
H5: CCs indirectly and positively predict  L2 achievement  through  PoE. H6: CCs indirectly and positively predict  L2 achievement  through  CoI.
Method
The present  study  utilised Structural  Equation  Modeling (SEM) (Kline, 2011; Schumacker  & Lomax, 2015). SEM was  composed  of  two  models:  the  Measurement  Model  and  the  Structural  Model. Measurement  models  assess  the  relationships  between  observed  variables  and  latent  constructs, while structural  models  evaluate  the  paths  among  latent  constructs.  This study  employed  SEM to explore  the  parallel  mediation  roles  of PoE and  CoI between  career  competencies  (CCs) and  L2 achievement.
The participants
The  research  sample  consisted  of  290  Turkish university  students  (206  females  and  84  males) enrolled  in English preparatory  classes of different  state  universities. In Turkish universities, under - graduate  programmes  where  the  language  of instruction  is English require  a  year-long  English language study, while those conducted with Turkish as the language of instruction offer it as a volun- tary practice before the entry into the programme. A convenience sampling was employed, with the criterion that participants should have spent at least one semester in the English preparatory classes. This made  the  participants  eligible for collecting L2 achievement  and L2 grit scores
Both a priori and post-hoc power analysis were conducted  to determine  the appropriate  sample size. The a priori analysis suggests  that  a sample  size of 164 participants  is adequate  to achieve  a statistical power  of approximately  80% at an alpha  level of 0.05, with an RMSEA value of 0.05. Measurements
The study  variables  were  measured  through  two  quantitative  instruments  and  a personal  infor- mation  form. We obtained  demographic  information  from the  participants  including  their gender, socio-economic  status  (SES), and age, as well as their L2 achievement  scores.
L2 achievement
In line with Brown et al. (2018) review, we asked the participants to provide the last two English exam scores to measure L2 achievement. All scores were standardised using a 100-point grading system as different  scoring  systems  were  used  across  the  participants   institutions.  Then we  calculated  the mean  scores to obtain  one particular score reflecting their L2 achievement.
Career competencies
The Career Competencies  Questionnaire,  originally developed  by Akkermans et al. (2013) and later validated  for a Turkish sample  by G rg l  and  Bozgeyikli (2024), was employed  in this study. This questionnaire  assesses  six dimensions  of career  competencies:  reflection  on  motivation  (3 items, e.g.:  I know what  I like in my work ), reflection on qualities (4 items, e.g.:  I know my strengths  in my work ), networking  (4 items,  e.g.:  I know  a lot of people  within  my work who  can  help  me with  my career ),  self-pro ling  (3 items,  e.g.:  I can  clearly show  others  what  my strengthsare  in my work ), work exploration (3 items, e.g.:  I know how to  nd out what my options are for becoming further educated ), and career control (4 items, e.g.:  I can make clear career plans ). It comprises 21 items, rated on a 5-point Likert scale. Higher scores indicate greater career competencies. The Dutch sample  demonstrated  good  internal  consistency  coefficients  (Cronbach s  Alpha = .77—.90 across dimensions)  and  construct  validity (Akkermans et al., 2013). Similarly, the  Turkish sample  showed good internal consistency coefficients (Cronbach s Alpha = .86—.94 across dimensions) and construct validity (G rg l  & Bozgeyikli, 2024). The present  study found  high internal  consistency  across the dimensions,  with omega  coefficients ranging  from .82 to .89.
L2 grit
This study  utilised the  language  domain-speci c  Grit Scale (L2-Grit Scale), developed  by Teimouri et al. (2022) and  validated  for a Turkish sample  by Uştuk and  Erarslan (2023). The scale measures two dimensions of grit: perseverance of effort (PoE) and consistency of interest (CoI). The scale com- prises  9 items,  with  ve  items  for PoE (e.g.  I am  a diligent  English language  learner )  and  four reversed-coded  items  for CoI (e.g.  My interests  in learning  English change  from  year  to  year ), each  rated  on  a  ve-point  Likert scale.  Higher  scores  in  each  dimension  indicate  greater  L2- speci c  grit.  The  scale  demonstrated  sufficient  internal  consistency  (Cronbach s  Alpha = .66—.80 across dimensions and the overall scale) and con rmed construct validity in the original sample (Tei- mouri  et  al., 2022). On the  other  hand,  the  Turkish sample  exhibited  very good  reliability scores (Cronbach s Alpha = .84—.90 across dimensions  and  the  overall scale) and  construct  validity (Uştuk & Erarslan, 2023). In this study, the  Omega  internal  consistency  coefficients  were  .87 for PoE and .83 for CoI.
Data analysis
We used  the  R programming  language  with  RStudio (version  2023.06.1 + 524) for data  analysis (RSTUDIO, 2020). Initially, the  variables were examined  for outliers using z-values. An outlier in L2 achievement  (z-value = −3.35) was  excluded  from  the  analysis.  Additionally, there  were  only  8 missing  values  in L2 achievement,  which were  addressed  using  the  listwise deletion  method.  To describe the data, we utilised the  psych  (Revelle, 2024) and  dplyr  (Wickham et al., 2024) packages. 
In this study, Herman s one factor test was applied to assess whether the results were affected using 
a single  data  collection  method  (e.g. self-reported  measures),  commonly  referred  to  as common method bias (Fuller et al., 2015; Podsakoff et al., 2003). This test calculates the proportion of variance 
in the model s variables that can be attributed to a single factor. If the variance explained by a single factor exceeds  50%, it indicates  a common  factor influenced  the analysis. The analysis showed  that the  rst  unrotated  factor explained  37.76% of the  variance. These  ndings  provide  evidence  that common method bias is not a signi cant concern in this study. For the main analysis, which involved Structural  Equation  Modeling  (SEM), we  employed  the  lavaan   package  (Rosseel,  2024)  and  semPlot   package  (Epskamp, 2015).
In the  SEM analysis, latent  variables included  CCs, PoE, and  CoI, while observed  variables con- sisted  of  the  items  for  PoE and  CoI, the  total  scores  for  the  dimensions  of  CCs (reflection  on motivation,  reflection  on  qualities,  networking,  self-pro ling,  work  exploration,  and  career control), and  L2 achievement.  In this study, parcelling  was employed  to represent  the  latent  vari- able  CCs through  aggregated  scores  of  its  dimensions.  Item  parcelling,  commonly  utilised  in factor  analysis and  structural  equation  modelling  (SEM), entails  aggregating  multiple  items  into a single composite  indicator.  This method  has both  advantages  (such as reduce  model  complex- ity) and  disadvantages  (such as bias), therefore  it should  be  used  carefully (Little et  al., 2013). In this  study,  this  decision  was  based  on  several  methodological  and  theoretical  considerations. Firstly, the  dimensions  of CCs can  theoretically  be  argued  to  demonstrate  homogeneity  in their content.  Methodologically,  the  second-order  CFA analysis supports  construct  validity and  demon - strates  the  holistic structure  of CCs. A second-order  CFA was conducted  to verify the  strength  of the  relationships  between  the  dimensions  and  the  higher-order  factor  (CCs). The second-order CFA model  demonstrated  an  acceptable  t  to  the  data  (CFI = 0.93,  TLI = 0.92,  RMSEA = 0.079, SRMR = 0.048). The  factor  loadings  for  both  rst-order  factors  (dimensions)  and  especially  the second-order  factor  (CCs) were  signi cant  (p < 0.001)  and  generally  high  (>0.7  standardised). These  ndings  suggest  that  the  CCs  dimensions  are well-represented  and  theoretically  coherent. Given these  considerations,  item  parcelling  can  be  a justi ed  approach  to  reduce  measurement error,  enhance  model  stability,  and  improve  the  t  of  our  complex  model  with  a  relatively small sample  size (Little et  al., 2013).
SEM was  conducted  through  the  following  steps:  model  speci cation,  model  identi cation, model  estimation,  model  testing,  and  model  modi cation  (Schumacker  & Lomax,  2015). The model  was speci ed  and  identi ed  based  on the  conceptual  framework  outlined  in the  literature section. A bootstrapping method  with robust  maximum  likelihood estimation  was used  for model 
estimation. Initially, the measurement model estimations con rmed that all factor loadings were sig-
ni cant.  Subsequently,  model  t  indices were examined.  We used  Chi-square, CFI, TLI, RMSEA, and SRMR as  t indices. Although Chi-square is ideally expected to be insigni cant, it tends to be signi  - cant with larger sample  sizes. Therefore, we relied on other  indices to complement  the Chi-square. For a good  model  t,  CFI and  TLI values should  be  .90 or above,  while RMSEA and  SRMR values should  be  below  .08. (Schumacker  & Lomax, 2015). After evaluating  the  path  coefficients,  the results were reported.

Discussion
In the  globalised  business  world, pro ciency  in L2 can  be  a  critical requirement.  Consequently, careers  serve as a motivator  for the  development  of L2 skills among  individuals. In line with this, our study  demonstrates  that  career  competencies  (CCs) were  indirectly linked to L2 achievement through perseverance of effort (PoE). Firstly, CCs positively predicted PoE. These competencies com- prise components  such as awareness  of one s own skills and  goals, the  capability to set and  plan career  goals, and  these  components  can motivate  individuals to persist  in their efforts to achieve L2 pro ciency.  Therefore, career  competencies  may function  as motivational  factors that  facilitate sustained  effort in L2 learning, similar to career aspirations  (Sasaki et al., 2017) and career-oriented curricula (Lapan et al., 2016). According to Morris  (2011) study, determining  career  goals, a com- ponent of career control, was a signi cant motivator for language learning. Furthermore, Akkermans 
et  al. (2018) suggested  that  career  competencies  can  initiate  motivational  processes  as valuable resources.  Additionally, the  relationship  between  competencies  and  grit is not  con ned  to career contexts.  Wei et  al. (2020) found  that  self-related  L2 competencies  predicted  L2 grit, indicating that as individuals  con dence in their own competencies increases, they may exhibit greater deter - mination  to succeed.
In this study, PoE also positively predicted L2 achievement. The relationship between PoE and L2       achievement  has  been  observed  in numerous  studies  in the  literature  (Alamer, 2021; Datu et  al., 2017;  Shirvan  & Alamer,  2022;  Teimouri  et  al.,  2020).  These  studies  suggested  that  PoE, as opposed  to  CoI, can  predict  academic  outcomes  regardless  of cultural  differences.  Cred   et  al. (2017) also emphasises the importance of maintaining consistent effort and diligence to successfully 
complete  the  tasks necessary  for achieving  one s  goals. This suggests  that  perseverance  of effort

On the other  hand, CoI was not found  to be associated  with either  career competencies  or L2 achievement.  Demir (2024) stated  that  the  relationship  between  CoI and  L2-related  variables varied  across  studies,  making  it a contentious  dimension.  There were  several  explanations  for this  discrepancy.  Some  researchers  suggest  revisiting  the  de nition  of the  construct  (Cred   et al., 2017; Shirvan & Alamer, 2022). Another  rationale  leaned  on cultural differences  (Demir, 2024; Datu et al., 2017). Datu et al. (2017) noted that CoI might not have signi cant meaning for achiev-
ing academic  goals  in collectivist cultures.  In such  cultures,  meeting  the  expectations  of others might be more important than maintaining one s own passion. Therefore, gritty individuals in col- lectivist societies  may not  necessarily  need  to  sustain  their  interest  in L2 to  achieve  academic success. Supporting  this inference,  while CoI was found  to be  a signi cant  predictor  of success in a study  conducted  in the  United  States  setting  (Sudina & Plonsky, 2021), it did  not  predict achievement  in studies  conducted  among  Saudi and  Iranian samples  (Alamer, 2021; Shirvan & Alamer, 2022; Teimouri et al., 2020). The speci c  result of the  present  study  can also be  viewed in this light, considering  the  Turkish context,  which, while being  a rich and  diverse  mosaic that resists easy classi cation  on the collectivist/individualist  scale, generally exhibits collectivist incli- nations  (G regenli,  1997).
This insight becomes  more meaningful  in relation to career situations.  Career competencies  did not predict CoI. This could be because, for the Turkish youth, career competencies and university life are linked to extrinsic goals, such as obtaining  a profession. A study investigating  the  reasons  why Turkish youth  attend  university found  that  vocational  goals were prominent  (G rg l  et al., 2025). Additionally, T rkiye  also implements  an  examination-based  selection  and  placement  system  for the  transition  from  middle  school  to  high  school,  through  university.  This system  encourages young  people  to study regularly starting  from early ages, with hard  work being  highly valued. For example,  students  must  cover and  solve problems  and  questions  in various  elds  such  as maths, physics, and  geography,  even  if they  are  not  interested,  to  achieve  higher  scores. Therefore, the CoI dimension might be irrelevant and have limited functionality in certain cultures and conditions.
This study found a relationship between career competencies and L2 achievement, albeit an indir-
ect one.  We interpret  this result  within the  context  of the  Turkish labour  market,  suggesting  that Turkish students  might  strive for L2 achievement  due  to  employment  pressures,  rather  than  out of a genuine passion for learning L2. Students with higher career competencies might be more cog- nizant of the relationship  between  career choice and L2 achievement.  They might  feel the need  to work hard to promote  and increase their career opportunities,  which drives them  to persist in their efforts. As a result, this persistence  ultimately  leads to higher  L2 achievement  scores. In this view, passion  for L2, that  is, consistency  of interest,  may not  have  a game-changing  role for CCs or L2 achievement.  We can observe  that  this is also probably  due  to the  labour  market  in T rkiye  with high unemployment rates  (Turkish Statistical Institute, 2024). With high unemployment in T rkiye, young  individuals may feel compelled  to view L2 learning  as a practical requirement  for securing employment  rather  than  as an intrinsic interest  or personal  enthusiasm.
This pattern  can also be interpreted  within the  expectancy—value framework (Wig eld  & Eccles, 2000). From this perspective,  the  psychological  mechanism  underlying  these  relationships  may be associated  with the  task value students  attribute  to L2 learning.  As career  competencies  increase, students  may  assign  greater  importance  to  L2 learning,  particularly  in terms  of attainment  and utility values, for the  reasons  discussed  above.  This increase  in perceived  task value may, in turn, lead to greater  effort investment  and  higher  achievement.  Although  the  other  core component  of the  framework  — students   expectancy  for  success  — was  not  examined  in  the  present  study, future  research  could extend  the  model  by incorporating  perceived  competence  or self-efficacy.
Implications
Given that career competencies indirectly enhance L2 achievement through perseverance, there are potential  implications. In line with United Nations Sustainable  Development  Goal (SDG) 4 access to inclusive and  equitable  quality education  is essential  for sustainable  human  development  (United Nations, 2015). From a sustainability  science perspective  (Komiyama & Takeuchi, 2006; Sahle et al., 2025; Takeuchi et al., 2017), education also plays an important role in in promoting this perspective. 
In the context of the present study, higher education institutions should consider L2 learning as part 
of sustainable human development, and academic units should collaborate for these broader goals. Speci cally, schools of foreign languages may develop joint programmes with career centres. In this way, students  can understand the importance  of L2 learning for career sustainability by developing career competencies  early on. Introducing  career competency  training from the  rst  year of univer- sity, especially in preparatory  language  programmes,  may positively influence academic  success in L2 learning. Therefore, it would be bene cial for higher educational institutions to consider integrat -
ing brief, group-based career counselling programmes into their preparatory language programmes. Career counsellors in career centres can work with students in preparatory language programmes to raise awareness  of the  importance  of L2 for their future  career trajectories.
In addition  to  integrating  career  counselling  into  L2 learning,  career  counselling  interventions themselves  could  be  conducted  in the  L2, thereby  simultaneously  supporting  students   language development and enhancing their career competencies. This could entail incorporating career plan-
ning workshops, goal-setting exercises, and skill awareness sessions conducted in the L2 used within 
the language curriculum. It is also recommended to increase efforts both within and outside the cur- riculum  to  facilitate  the  acquisition  of career  skills. Although  this  research  focuses  solely on  L2 achievement,  other  studies  (e.g. Akkermans  et  al., 2018) have  shown  that  career  competencies can be a signi cant  predictor  across various academic  outcomes.
The  ndings of the study indicate that the role of consistency of interest and persistence of inter-
est in L2 achievement  may differ across different  cultural contexts.  Consequently,  it is essential  for educators  and  policymakers  to  consider  cultural  factors  when  designing  language  programmes with career-related  content.  For instance,  in collectivist cultures  where  extrinsic goals and  societal expectations  may be  salient,  language  programmes  may bene t  from emphasising  the  practical and career-related  bene ts of L2 pro ciency.
Limitations and future research
Considering  the  scarcity  of research  that  simultaneously  explores  L2 achievement  and  career development,  this study represents  a novel and  valuable contribution  to the  eld.  However, this study  also has  certain  limitations. Since this study  adopts  a cross-sectional  design,  it is difficult to  draw  de nitive  conclusions  about  causality.  Longitudinal  studies  would  better  capture  the development  of these  relationships  over time. Additionally, longitudinal  studies  could contribute to understanding the reciprocal and recursive relationships between career competencies, L2 grit, 
and achievement.  Secondly, other  potential  individual difference variables that  could impact  the relationships  of PoE, CoI, and  L2 achievement  were  not  included.  For instance,  factors  such  as individuals   socio-economic  status,  family backgrounds,  or  educational  history  should  also  be taken  into  account.  Furthermore,  the  sample  in this study  consisted  of preparatory  English stu- dents  at  universities,  neglecting  L2 learners  in  different  contexts  (e.g.  elective  courses,  high school  level,  private  courses,  etc.).  Given  that  many  preparatory  classes  are  mandatory,  the results should  be interpreted  speci cally  within this context.
Additionally, future research  should  explore grit in English language  learning across various cul- tures.  Academic and  career-related  variables  are  thought  to  be  important  for understanding  the factors that  drive L2 learning.  These variables are susceptible  to the  influences  of culture  and  the present  labour  market.  Therefore, comparative  studies  between  Western  and  Eastern settings,  as well  as  countries  with  varying  economic  conditions,  could  provide  a  better  understanding  of these  relationships.  Given the  mixed  ndings  on CoI in the  literature,  it is also recommended that future  research  should  address  alternative  de nitions  and  measures  of CoI. This could  help  clarify its role and improve  the  consistency  of  ndings  across studies.
