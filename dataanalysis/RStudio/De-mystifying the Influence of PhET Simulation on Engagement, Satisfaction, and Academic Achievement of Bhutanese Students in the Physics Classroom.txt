Abstract
This study employed anon-equivalent quasi-experimental pre-test/post-test control-group design to study the effect of thePhET simulation intervention on students’ engagement, satisfaction, and academic achievement in the learning of direct current electric circuit concepts among Bhutanese students. We analysed thepre- and post-test scores and perceptions of 57 ninth-grade students, divided into experimental group (EG, n = 29) and control group (CG, n = 28), from one high school in Paro District, Bhutan. The EG students were taught with the PhET simulation intervention, while the CG students were taught with the traditional chalk- talk method. The pre- and post-test scores were collected with the Electric Circuits Conceptual Evaluation (ECEE) inventory. Mean, standard deviation, a two-sample t-test, and multiple linear regression (MLR) were computed using R and RStudio. The t-test revealed a statistically significant difference in the mean post-test scores of CG and EG students. MLR analysis further confirmed that this difference was due to the PhET simulation intervention, ruling out the influence of other confounding vari- ables. Additionally, an instrument called thePhET Engagement-Satisfaction Questionnaire was developed to assess EG students’ engagement level and satisfaction with the PhET simulation intervention. Confirmatory factor analysis and Cronbach’s alpha calculation confirmed its validity and reliability. Data from thePhET Engagement-Satisfaction Questionnaire unveiled significant impact of thePhET simulation intervention on students’ engagement level and their overall satisfaction, reinforcing prior research. However, further research witha larger sample size, incorporating lesson observations, interviews, and our measurement tool, is necessary to ascertain whether the findings it yields align with the present study’s findings.

Given these contexts and the imperative to prepare learners for the challenges of the twenty-first century, there is a need to evaluate the efficacy of current pedagogical approaches and identify strategies for enhancing science education in schools across Bhutan.
The advent of educational technology, or “EdTech” (Rodriguez-Segura, 2022), has given both teachers and students a myriad of educational tools, strategies, and resources (Ross et al., 2010) to enhance and facilitate teaching and learning processes. EdTech interventions in the teaching and learning process have provided students the opportunities to engage in more interactive, hands-on, and collaborative learning experiences rather than simply being passive recipients of information from textbooks or lectures (Banda & Nzabahimana, 2021; Bo et al., 2018; Perkins, 2020). One often touted technology is Physics Education Technology (PhET) simulations, created by the University of Colorado. PhET simulations, available for free download for off-line use or which can be run online from the PhET website (https://phet.colorado.edu/), are a collection of research-based interactive, animated, game-like, and easy-to-use computer simulations (Banda & Nzabahimana, 2022; Ruwiyah et al., 2021) for the teaching and learning of topics in science and mathematics. A PhET simulation holds the potential to revolutionise science education for several reasons.
PhET simulations possess numerous hallmark features that render them an effective tool for educators to incorporate into their teaching processes (Ruwiyah etal., 2021). Firstly, the simulations have “extraordinary potential to facilitate authentic  inquiry  practices  that  include  articulating questions, hypothesis formulation, exploring the effect of data in a situation, and theory revision” (Rutten etal., 2012, as cited in Almasri, 2022, p. 7164). This type of inquiry- based exploration shifts the focus from a teacher-centred classroom to a more student-centred one, where students can explore, experiment, discover, and develop a deeper understanding of scientific concepts and phenomena by enabling them to form their own hypotheses and test them through virtual simulations. “Just as scientists construct and 
expand their knowledge through experimentation, students can simultaneously be constructing and expanding their knowledge through exploration and discovery in interactive simulations” (Perkins, 2020, p. 43). Indeed, when evaluated based on Dale’s Cone of Experience, this EdTech tool fell into the category of concrete experience, which is effective in engaging 90% of students in educational tasks such as observation, experimentation, and data analysis (Haryadi 
& Pujiastuti, 2020). PhET simulations provide students with a more inclusive, effective, interactive, and engaging virtual learning environment to explore and experiment with abstract concepts and phenomena they may not otherwise have access to in real classroom settings (Lindgren et al., 2016). For example, the “Build an Atom” simulation allows students to manipulate subatomic particles to build different types of atoms and observe their behaviour, providing a hands-on learning experience that is difficult to replicate in a traditional classroom setting. Secondly, PhET simulations offer a challenging and game-like environment (Perkins etal., 2012), where students learn through inquiry-based authentic explorations (Ruwiyah et al., 2021), making the learning experience more dynamic and exciting. For example, the Circuit Construction Kit activity challenges students to apply their knowledge and problem-solving skills to make working circuits with the minimal electronics kit at their disposal in the simulation itself (Perkins et al., 2012). Thirdly, the instant dynamic feedback provided by the simulations allows students to identify and correct their misconceptions (Ganasen & Shamuganathan, 2017), explore cause-and- effect relationships, and answer scientific questions through simulated exploration (Ruwiyah et al., 2021), fostering a deeper understanding of the scientific concepts. Fourthly, they are designed to help students develop a mental picture of the scientific phenomenon, enabling them to retain the information for longer periods of time (Ruwiyah etal., 2021). Fifthly, PhET simulations incorporate diverse representations of scientific concepts, including object movements, graphs, and numerical data. This caters to diverse learning styles and enables students to interact with the simulations in a way that best suits them (Almasri, 2022). By providing visual and interactive representations of scientific concepts, PhET simulations allow students to understand complex ideas without relying on text-based explanations, thus transcending language barriers. Lastly, these simulations circumvent the need for expensive equipment, making science instruction more accessible and efficient in classrooms.
Recent international studies have used PhET simula- tions in teaching subjects like physics (Almasri, 2022; Banda & Nzabahimana, 2022; Chinaka, 2021; Haryadi 
& Pujiastuti, 2020), chemistry (Almasri, 2022; Ganasen 
& Shamuganathan, 2017), biology (Almasri, 2022), and other disciplines (Liu & Lin, 2010). Despite the differ- ences in methodology, the foregoing studies have found a

Theoretical Framework andHypothesis Development
This study is guided by several educational theories and models such as constructivist pedagogy, experiential learning theory, and technology acceptance model (TAM). Constructivist pedagogy and experiential learning theory, rooted in the constructivist learning theory, pioneered by Jean Piaget and Lev Vygotsky, emphasise active, contex- tual, and social learning, where learners construct knowl- edge through experiences and interactions with their envi- ronment (Richardson, 2003). Piaget’s theory emphasises the active role of learner in the learning process, suggest- ing that knowledge is best acquired through active interac- tion and hands-on activities rather than passively receiving and memorising information (Zajda, 2021). PhET simula- tion-assisted teaching supports this by providing interac- tive, hands-on experiences that engage students in active learning. For example, in the “Circuit Construction Kit” simulation used in this study, students can build their own electrical circuits by dragging and dropping components like batteries, wires, and light bulbs. They can change the configuration and immediately see the effects, such as how altering the resistance impacts the brightness of the light bulb. This hands-on activity requires students to actively engage with the concepts of electricity and electric cir- cuits. Additionally, the feedback provided by the PhET simulations to students while exploring the simulations resembles the concept of scaffolding proposed by Lev Vygotsky. Similar to how scaffolding, typically provided by a more knowledgeable other such as teacher or peer, assists learners in mastering tasks beyond their current level of competence, feedback mechanisms within PhET simulations serve to guide students through the learning process. For example, dragging a slider might change a variable, and the immediate feedback (in the form of vis- ual changes, numerical updates, or other indicators) helps users understand the relationship between their actions and the outcomes. PhET simulations also facilitate social interaction, another critical aspect of Vygotsky’s social constructivist theory, by encouraging collaborative prob- lem-solving and discussions among students. Addition- ally, to maximise the effectiveness of simulation-based teaching, Kolb’s (2014) experiential learning theory was also used. While direct research linking PhET simulations to Kolb’s experiential learning theory is lacking, Meyer et al.’s (2021) study in the clinical field suggests that inte- grating four stages of Kolb’s experiential learning theory into simulation-based education can significantly enhance 
students’ skills and performance across various subjects. Kolb proposed a four-stage learning cycle, which includes concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract con- ceptualisation, and active experimentation (Babin et al., 2019; Lehane, 2020). According to Kolb, concrete expe- rience involves engaging in hands-on, discovery-based learning experiences like simulation, reflective obser- vation involves reflecting on their concrete experiences, abstract conceptualisation involves conceptualising their observations to formulate hypotheses or mental models to explain the observed phenomena, and active experimen- tation involves testing their hypotheses through further experimentation (Babin et al., 2019; Lehane, 2020). Even- tually, optimal learning takes place when learners experi- ence all four stages (Poore et al., 2014). Simulation-based teaching aligns well with this theory, as it offers opportu- nities for students to engage in immersive hands-on activi- ties, receive immediate feedback encouraging reflection, develop theories, and test those theories through further experimentation within the simulations. Furthermore, this study integrates technology acceptance model (TAM; Davis, 1987), into its theoretical framework. TAM pro- vides a framework for understanding whether users will accept or refuse a technology (Fussell & Truong, 2022). According to TAM, perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness are critical factors that influence users’ atti- tudes towards and acceptance of a new technology (Fussell 
& Truong, 2022). If users perceive a technology as easy to use, they are more likely to accept and adopt it, whereas perceived complexity or difficulty may deter it. Users are more likely to accept and use a technology if they believe it will be useful to them in achieving their goals. In the context of this study, TAM is used to explore how Bhuta- nese students perceive PhET simulations. We hypothesised that if students perceive the simulations as easy to use and useful for understanding scientific concepts, they are more likely to engage with the PhET simulations. This height- ened engagement, in turn, was anticipated to positively influence their learning outcomes.
Method
Research Design
This study used a mixed method approach. The study employed a non-equivalent quasi-experimental pre-test/ post-test control-group design to compare the academic per- formance of experimental group (EG) students, who were taught with the PhET simulation intervention, with those in the control group (CG), who were taught with the traditional chalk-talk method. The concepts covered in the study was direct current (DC) circuit concepts, including topics such 



Journal of Science Education and Technology (2024) 33:892–909
as electric current, resistance, potential difference, electric power, series, and parallel DC circuits. The Electric Circuits Conceptual Evaluation (ECEE) inventory was used as both pre- and post-test measures. To assess EG students’ engage- ment level and satisfaction with the PhET simulation inter- vention, data were collected through a self-created measure called the PhET Engagement-Satisfaction Questionnaire and a qualitative, open-ended reflective survey.
Setting, Sample Size, andS ampling Method
This study employed a two-sample approach. Initially, data was collected from 302 tenth–twelfth grade students from two higher secondary schools in western Bhutan to assess the questionnaire’s psychometric properties. This sample was 59.9% male and 40.1% female, with an average age of 16.2 years. Subsequently, for the pri- mary research investigation, akin to a second pilot study due to the small sample size, 57 ninth-grade students enrolled in three different sections at our school were used. This sample, with an average age of 15.6, was used to address the research questions. This smaller sample consisted of 57.9% males and 42.1% females. These students were divided into two groups: CG (n = 28) 
and EG (n = 29). To ensure impartial group labelling, a flip of the coin was conducted. Both sets of samples were selected via the convenience sampling method. This approach was chosen for its practicality and ease of access to the student population under investigation (Andrade, 2021).

PhET Engagement‑Satisfaction Questionnaire
A tool named PhET Engagement-Satisfaction Questionnaire (Table 2) was developed to estimate students’ engagement level and satisfaction with the PhET simulation intervention during their study of DC electric circuit concepts. A total of 21 observed variables (items) were adapted from existing sur- vey questionnaires (Jefferies & Rizzolo, 2006; Lindgren etal., 2016) and the TAM model. The questionnaire consisted of three sections. Section A collected participants’ demographic information, such as their current grade level, age, and other confounding variables (discussed below). Sections B and C with, 11 and 10 items, respectively, measured EG students’ engagement level and satisfaction with the PhET simulation intervention. Each item was measured on a five-point Likert- type scale (1 = Strongly Disagree, 2 = Disagree, 3 = Neutral, 4 = Agree, and 5 = Strongly Agree). Negatively keyed items were reverse-scored before conducting any analysis.
Before administering the PhET Engagement-Satisfaction Questionnaire to the actual sample, a pilot study was con- ducted to assess its psychometric properties via CFA and Cronbach’s alpha analysis. The details are mentioned under the “Statistical Analysis” section.
Confounding Variables
Researchers (e.g. Pourhoseingholi et al., 2012; Theobald 
& Freeman, 2014) have asserted that failure to control the impacts of confounding variables in a non-equivalent quasi- experimental pre-test/post-test control-group research design study can potentially mislead researchers and lead to valid- ity issues. Confounding variables refer to the extraneous variables that have the potential to exert an influence on the response variable (Pourhoseingholi et al., 2012). There- fore, to confirm whether the difference in the mean post- test scores of the CG and EG students is due to the PhET simulation intervention, we collected data on students’ prior exposure to PhET simulation, hours spent preparing for the post-test, prior knowledge of taught concepts, and level of motivation to learn physics.
Qualitative Reflective Survey
To elicit a comprehensive understanding of students’ percep- tions regarding the usefulness of PhET simulation interven- tion, we also collected qualitative reflective responses from a few EG students using open-ended questions. Students responded to the reflective survey in English.
Data Collection Procedures andIn tervention
This study was conducted during the academic year 2023. Ethical approval for conducting the study was sought from 
the school’s principal. The study’ purpose and objectives were thoroughly explained to the school principal and participants. Informed consent was obtained from all parents and partici- pants, given the students’ minor status. Prior to the instruc- tional intervention, a pre-test was administered to diagnose and evaluate students’ prior knowledge of DC electric circuits. This pre-test also served to determine whether the academic achievement levels of the CG and EG students were similar before the intervention (Banda & Nzabahimana, 2022). Subse- quently, as mentioned earlier, the CG students were then taught in a traditional way with the chalk-talk method, while the EG students were taught with a free-ware PhET simulation titled “Circuit Construction Kit: DC” (available at https://phet.color ado.edu). To minimise any teacher-related effects, both groups were taught by the same teacher in English, who was one of the researchers. The CG students did not use both real circuits or PhET simulations during the study. They only engaged in theoretical learning and traditional problem-solving exercises (network problem-solving) without hands-on practice, either with simulations or physical components. As for the EG stu- dents, they participated in both hands-on practice with real circuit and PhET simulation activities (see Fig. 8). Initially, they engaged in PhET simulation activities. After mastering the concepts through simulation, they transitioned to practical work with real circuits. They used battery eliminators (device that provide a stable DC power supply by converting AC from the mains), wires, resistors, ammeters, one-way keys, voltme- ters, and multimeters (see Fig. 8), to recreate the circuits they had built virtually. This practical work involved constructing series and parallel circuits, measurement of actual current and voltage, and observation of any disparities between simulated and real circuit behaviour. Both activities were conducted in English, as it serves as the medium of instruction in Bhutan’s modern educational system (Dendup, 2023).
To develop the PhET simulation activities for EG students, we used six heuristics—a research-based strategies devel- oped by Rehn etal. (2013)—to facilitate the productive use of the PhET simulations. Table 1 depicts these six heuristics and describes their implementation in the present study.
After the interventions, a post-intervention post-test was administered to both classes. Then, EG students’ engagement and satisfaction levels with the PhET simu- lation intervention were assessed using the PhET Engage- ment-Satisfaction Questionnaire and a qualitative, open- ended reflective survey. Students completed their ECEE pre- and post-tests using the English version, the only version available, and also responded to the two survey questionnaires in English.

PhET Engagement‑Satisfaction Questionnaire’s Psychometric Evaluation
CFA was conducted with initial data collected from 302 students to assess its structural or factorial validity (i.e. whether 21 items are related to the two hypothesised latent variables (factors), engagement and satisfaction, as expected) of the PhET Engagement-Satisfaction Ques- tionnaire. CFA was chosen because of its unique ability to explore the causal relationships among latent and observed variables in pre-defined, theory-derived models (Tavakol 
& Wetzel, 2020). CFA was conducted using the cfa () func- tion from the lavaan package in R (Rosseel, 2012). Before conducting CFA, data screening procedures were con- ducted, including the examination of missing data, outli- ers, and univariate and multivariate normality. Fortunately, there were no missing data or outliers in our datasets. The univariate and multivariate normalities of the data were checked using the mardia () function from the mvnormal- Test package (Zhou & Shao, 2014). Since our data did not meet both the univariate and multivariate normality assumptions (Li, 2016), we chose Diagonally Weighted Least Square (DWLS) for estimating CFA models (Ross 
& Bauldry, 2022). We set the variance of the latent vari- able to one. To assess the CFA model’s goodness-of-fit, various goodness-of-fit indices including chi-square test (χ2) and χ2/df, Root Mean Square Error of Approxima- tion (RMSEA) with 95% confidence interval (CI), Com- parative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), and Standardised Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) were used. The CFA model was deemed to be a good fit for the data when the χ2 result was non-significant (Marsh 
& Hocevar, 1985) and the χ2/df < 2 (Hu & Bentler, 1999; Schreiber et al., 2006). The adequacy of the other fit indi- ces was assessed by comparing them to the threshold val- ues recommended in prior research studies (Hu & Bentler, 1999; Ross & Bauldry, 2022; Schreiber et al., 2006); RMSEA ≤ 0.05, CFI ≥ 0.95, and SRMR < 0.08 indicate 
the model’s good fit to the observed data. Due to the χ2 statistic’s sensitivity to the sample size (Marsh & Hocevar, 1985) and departures from multivariate normality in the data (Ross & Bauldry, 2022), more importance is placed 
on these fit indices, because the χ2 statistic tends to reject the null hypothesis in samples larger than 100 (Lebowitz et al., 2019; Ross & Bauldry, 2022). Finally, we used the semPlot () function from the semPlot package to visualise the ultimate CFA model (Fig. 1). Furthermore, to estimate the questionnaire’s construct reliability, Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was calculated and assessed following George and Mallery’s (2019) guidelines.
Main Data Analysis
To address research question 1, tables, graphs, mean (M), median, standard deviations (SD), and interquartile range (IQR) were used to describe and summarise the pre- and post-test scores collected from EG and CG students. Addi- tionally, an independent two-sample t-test (hereafter t-test) was computed to examine the statistical significance of the difference in means of the pre- and post-test scores of CG and EG students. To quantify the magnitude of the dif- ference, if any, Cohen’s d effects size was calculated. Its magnitude was assessed using the thresholds outlined in Cohen (1992), that is, |d|< 0.2 “negligible”, |d|< 0.5 “small”, |d|< 0.8 “medium”, otherwise “large”. Assumptions of the t-test were checked, including the normality of the data and Levene’s test of homogeneity of variances. No violations were observed. Specifically, the normality of the data was checked visually by plotting diagnostic plots such as histo- grams and Quantile–Quantile plots (Q-Q plot). Additionally, the Shapiro–Wilk test, skewness, and kurtosis values were also computed.
Parametric tests, like t-tests, assume the data follows a normal distribution and that the variances of two groups (EG and CG) are equal. The Shapiro–Wilk test assesses if the data are normally distributed, while Levene’s test checks whether the variances of two groups are equal. If both tests’ p values are greater than the chosen significance level (  = 0.05), it indicates the data follows a normal distribution and variances are similar between groups. Skewness and kurtosis also help assess the normality of the data. Skewness measures the asymmetry of a distribution, with a skewness of zero indicating symmetry (Kallner, 2018). Kurtosis measures the peakedness or tailedness of the distribution, with a kurtosis of 3 indicating a normal distribution (Kallner, 2018). Deviations from these values result in the non-normality of the data. In such cases, non-parametric tests may be more suitable (Mishra et al., 2019). Histograms and Q-Q plots provide a graphical representation of data distributions.
Additionally, means and SD were calculated for each item under the engagement and satisfaction constructs. The means were categorised into three levels: low (1.7 or lower), medium (1.8–3.5), and high (3.6–5.0) to describe EG students’ level of engagement and satisfaction with the PhET simulation intervention. Furthermore, Pearson’s correlation coefficient was calculated to examine the potential association between engagement, satisfaction, and the post-test scores achieved by EG students. Finally, qualitative analysis was performed on the data obtained from the post-intervention reflective survey to gain comprehensive understanding of students’ percep- tions about the PhET simulation intervention.

Discussion
This study investigated the experiences of Bhutanese stu- dents as they engaged with the free-ware “Circuit Construc- tion Kit: DC” PhET simulation to learn DC electric circuit concepts (Fig. 8). Following our initial pilot study involv- ing 302 students, this research can be considered a second pilot study due to the smaller sample size of 57 participants. Despite this limitation, the results offer additional empirical validation for existing research, affirming the PhET simula- tions as a potent educational tool that enhances students’ engagement, satisfaction, and academic performance.
From the post-test score analysis, we found that students in EG, who received PhET simulation intervention, per- formed better on average than their counterparts in the CG, who did not receive PhET simulation intervention. This is evident from the higher mean and median post-test scores 
of the EG students compared to the CG students (Table 4). Moreover, a t-test confirmed that the observed difference was not due to random chance but rather a consequence of the PhET simulation intervention (p < 0.05). The MLR analysis provided further confirmation that the gain in the post-test scores among the EG students was due to the PhET simulation intervention and students’ prior knowledge of the subject matter and not due to some other confounding vari- ables, including prior exposure to PhET, hours spent prepar- ing for the post-test, prior knowledge of taught concepts, and level of motivation to learn physics. Since students’ prior knowledge of the subject matter also contributed to the improvement in the post-test scores, participants, on aver- age, did not strongly agree with item S16 under the satisfac- tion factor. Nevertheless, the PhET simulation has positively influenced EG students’ scores in the post-intervention assessment. These results align with the findings of Banda and Nzabahimana (2022). Banda and Nzabahimana (2022) investigated the impact of PhET simulation on students’ motivation and academic achievement in learning oscilla- tions and waves among Malawian secondary students. The t-test showed a statistically significant difference between the EG and CG students’ mean post-test scores. Also, the results from linear regression indicated that the difference between the two groups in the post-test was due to the inter- vention and not owing to other students’ characteristics (Banda & Nzabahimana, 2022).
Our study also unveiled the substantial influence of the PhET simulation on students’ levels of engagement and overall satisfaction with the learning experience. The quantitative results obtained from PhET Engagement- Satisfaction Questionnaire provided compelling evidence of the high level of engagement among students exposed to the PhET simulation intervention. As depicted in Table 5, the mean engagement score, measured on a five-point Likert scale, surpassed 3.8, categorising the students’ engagement level as “High”. This is also evident from the low mean score (M = 1.7) on item E6 (I found the PhET simulation-assisted learning boring and uninteresting) under engagement construct. This data strongly supports the notion that PhET simulation is a great pedagogical tool to effectively captivate and involve students in the learning process. Additionally, qualitative feedback obtained from the reflective survey at the end of the instruction provided robust support for this quantitative finding. EG students expressed overwhelmingly positive feedback regarding their experience with the PhET simulation. They found the PhET simulation to be highly engaging and effective, providing an interactive, hands-on approach to experimenting with different circuit designs, observing the outcomes, and making quick modifications. Additionally, students appreciated the challenging yet engaging nature of the PhET simulation activities, which not only piqued their interest but also facilitated their active 
participation. This, in turn, boosted their understanding and retention of the DC electric circuit concepts, a fact clearly evident in their improved performance in the post-test (Table 4). Moreover, a high level of satisfaction expressed by students provided additional convincing evidence supporting PhET simulations as a powerful teaching tool. As depicted in Table6, the mean satisfaction score, measured on a five- point Likert scale, surpassed 4.1, categorising the students’ satisfaction level as “High”. Additionally, qualitative feedback obtained from a reflective survey further reaffirmed this finding. For instance, EG students acknowledged the PhET simulation as an invaluable tool for enhancing their learning experiences, while also highlighting its efficiency in saving time and energy, and promoting the development of critical thinking and ICT skills. Moreover, a resounding satisfaction rating of 9 out of 10 clearly underscored a strong contentment with the PhET simulation intervention in the learning process. Additionally, students’ high level of satisfaction with the PhET simulation intervention is also evident from the notably high mean score (M = 4.4) on item S21 (Overall, I am satisfied with my experience in PhET simulation-assisted learning of taught physics concepts) under the satisfaction construct. We hypothesised that if students perceive the simulations as easy to use and useful for understanding scientific concepts, they are more likely to engage with the PhET simulations. This heightened engagement, in turn, was anticipated to positively influence their learning outcomes. Our result affirmed this hypothesis. Students who perceived the simulation as user- friendly and beneficial for their learning were more inclined to use them. The mean response for students’ willingness to use PhET simulations in future classes was 4.1 and 4.2 for recommending them to peers. This demonstrates enhanced student engagement with the PhET simulations, aligning with the TAM model (Davis, 1987). Moreover, our study’s significant positive correlation between students’ academic achievement and their level of engagement with PhET simulations, as well as between students’ academic achievement and their overall satisfaction with the PhET simulation intervention, strongly supports our hypothesis. Overall, our study’s findings provide strong empirical support for the TAM, demonstrating how users’ perceptions of technology’s ease of use and usefulness influence their attitudes, engagement, intention to use, and ultimately their outcomes. This emphasises the importance of educational institutions in Bhutan and similar contexts to prioritise tools that align with TAM to maximise learning effectiveness.
Our foregoing results supplement the results of Almas- ri’s (2022) and Alhadlaq’s (2023) studies. Almasri (2022) used computer simulations like PhET interactive simulations, SimPop, and OLABS for teaching science subjects such as physics, chemistry, and biology to explore tertiary students’ learning experiences. This study demonstrated that computer 
simulations are an effective pedagogical tool for increasing stu- dents’ engagement and satisfaction with the learning process. Alhadlaq utilised PhET simulations to explore university stu- dents’ attitudes and experiences of using PhET simulations as a tool for unsupervised science learning without teacher support. The participants of the study showed a high level of engage- ment and satisfaction with the use of simulations in unsuper- vised science learning. Our findings also complement the quali- tative results of Penjor etal.’s (2022). Penjor and his colleagues identified several benefits of using PhET simulations, including the ability to assist teachers in keeping students’ interest and enthusiasm levels high throughout the lesson, simplify abstract concepts and enhance knowledge of the subject, enhance con- cept retention over the long term, and foster independent learn- ing through self-exploration and discovery.
Our study not only empirically validates the PhET simula- tions as a powerful teaching tool for enhancing student engage- ment, satisfaction, and academic performance, but it also vali- dates the efficacy of the six heuristics (Table 1), which are research-based strategies developed by Rehn et al. (2013) to facilitate the productive use of the PhET simulations. Rehn and colleagues have already demonstrated the utility of these six heuristics in facilitating the productive usage of the PhET sim- ulations. Our study adds to their utility by showing that using these heuristics with PhET simulations has the potential to:
1. Promote genuine inquiry-based learning by encourag- ing students to pose questions, formulate hypotheses, experiment with data’s effect in various situations, and revise their theories (Rutten et al., 2012).
2. Shift the educational focus from a teacher-centred class- room to a more student-centred one, where students can freely explore, experiment, discover, and gain a deeper understanding of scientific concepts and phenomena (Lindgren et al., 2016).
3. Provide students with instant, dynamic feedback to identify and correct their misconceptions, leading to a more pro- found grasp of the concepts (Ganasen & Shamuganathan, 2017; Ruwiyah etal., 2021).
4. Help students construct a mental model of the scientific phenomenon, enabling them to retain the information for longer of period (Ruwiyah et al., 2021).
Consequently, we urge educators to implement these six heuristics to develop assignments that facilitate productive use of a PhET simulation in the teaching and learning process (Rehn etal., 2013). If we use these heuristics as our guide, we can develop PhET simulation assignments or activities that can facilitate productive use of PhET simulation to enhance student engagement, satisfaction, and academic performance. We also implore teacher educators, teacher training institu- tions, and school administrators within Bhutan, as well as from any part of the world, to provide workshops and training for 
both pre- and in-service teachers to enhance their efficacy in implementing these six heuristics effectively. This will facili- tate the successful integration of PhET simulations into their teaching and learning process.
Conclusion
Overall, this study’s results highlight that transitioning from conventional teaching methods and incorporating interactive and immersive PhET simulations in the teaching and learning process can create more engaging and effective educational experiences for students (Banda & Nzabahimana, 2021; Bo et al., 2018; Perkins, 2020). This approach not only fosters a deeper interest in the subject matter but also enhances students’ overall satisfaction with the content elucidated by the simulations (Rehn et al., 2013). Our study also validates the utility of the six heuristics (Table 1), which are research-based strategies developed by Rehn et al. (2013) for facilitating the productive use of the PhET simulations. Our study also underscores the importance of incorporating the principles of TAM when introducing new tools for educational purposes.
Limitations andFuture Recommendation
While the study yielded promising positive outcomes, any generalisation beyond the study’s participants should be made with care due to the following reasons. First, the dependence on a small sample size may hinder the generalis- ability of its findings to a larger population of interest (Faber 
& Fonseca, 2014). The small sample used in this study may not represent the diverse range of students found in a larger population. Second, another limitation of the present study is its reliance on the self-report measure called the “PhET Engagement-Satisfaction Questionnaire” to assess students’ engagement and satisfaction with the PhET simulation inter- vention. While this instrument has showcased its validity and reliability via methods such as CFA and Cronbach’s alpha analysis, the response obtained through it is vulner- able to various issues (Demetriou et al., 2015). According to Demetriou et al. (2015), responses obtained through self- report measures suffer from various issues such as social desirability bias, response bias (both acquiescent and non- acquiescent bias), the inherent subjectivity of participants when responding to open-ended questions, potential recall bias, and the limited flexibility associated with fixed-choice questions (Demetriou et al., 2015). Consequently, further research with a larger sample size, incorporating lesson observations, interviews, and our measurement tool, is nec- essary to ascertain whether the findings it yields align with the present study’s findings.
