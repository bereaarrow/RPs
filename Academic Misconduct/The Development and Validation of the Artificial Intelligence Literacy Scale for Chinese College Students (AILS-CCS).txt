The Development and Validation of the Artificial Intelligence Literacy Scale for Chinese College Students (AILS-CCS)

ABSTRACT In the context of rapid advancements in artificial intelligence (AI), enhancing AI literacy among college students is crucial for promoting the sustainable development of higher education. It is imperative to strengthen the assessment and training of AI literacy among college students. however, there is currently a lack of effective tools for evaluating AI literacy in students from developing countries. Therefore, based on previous literature and the Chinese context, this study developed and validated an Artificial Intelligence Literacy Scale for Chinese College Students (AILS-CCS). After developing the initial framework, this study collected survey data (N=546) through random sampling. Following the execution of Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) and Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA), the final validated AILS-CCS emerged as a theoretically and empirically consistent tool, consisting of 15 items across four dimensions: Awareness, Usage, Evaluation, and Ethics. This tool holds significant importance for the assessment and training of AI literacy among college students in China and other developing countries globally.

I. INTRODUCTION 
With the rising popularity of tools like ChatGPT and Sora, artificial intelligence (AI) has become a global focal point. AI is significantly impacting various aspects of daily life, including education[1] and healthcare[2]. For example, AI can  assist  in  improving  medical  practices  by  providing intelligent  algorithms  and  machine  learning  (ML) techniques to aid in accurate diagnosis and the development of effective treatment plans[3]. Similarly, the impact of AI appears to be growing increasingly significant in the field of education.A survey conducted among Chinese university students revealed that 84.88% of respondents had used AI tools, with 16.30% using them frequently[4]. Clearly, using these AI applications or tools requires a certain level of AI literacy, which includes not only the proficiency in using AI tools but also the ethical use of these tools and the ability to  critically  evaluate  their  capabilities  and  limitations. Meanwhile,  these  so-called  digital  natives,  the  college students, show differences in their ability to use digital technologies  like  AI  and  have  fundamentally  different understandings  and  uses  of  these  technologies[5].  The Chinese  government  has  consistently  promoted  the 
integration of digital technologies, such as big data and AI, with higher education, as reflected in policy documents like "China  Education  Modernization  2035"[6].  As  college students are the main body of higher education, enhancing their  digital  technology  literacy  is  of  great  importance. Given  the  current  popularity  of  AI,  developing  tools  to measure  AI  literacy  among  Chinese  college  students  is crucial, as it helps relevant departments conduct targeted research and training. 
Previous researchers have conducted extensive studies on digital literacy, which appears similar to AI literacy on the  surface[7]-[10].  However,  digital  literacy  cannot substitute  for  AI  literacy[11].  AI  literacy  is  an interdisciplinary  field  involving  computer  science, psychology, sociology, philosophy, and other disciplines [12]. These intersections highlight the differences between digital literacy and AI literacy, indicating the necessity of conducting  independent  studies  on  AI  literacy.  Some scholars[11],[13]-[15] have explored this field but have yet to reach a unified conclusion. For instance, Wang et al. (2022) proposed an AI literacy framework encompassing awareness, usage, evaluation and ethics, and developed and

II. Literature Review 
A. The Connotation of Artificial Intelligence Literacy
The term "literacy" initially referred to the basic skills and knowledge related to books and printing[17]. However, with the rapid development of computers and digital technologies, many  attempts  have  been  made  to  extend  the  concept  of literacy  beyond  its  original  meaning  of  reading  and writing[18].  Consequently,  discussions  on  literacy  have expanded  to  include  television  literacy[19],  information literacy[20], digital literacy[21], game literacy[18], and media literacy[22]. 
Artificial intelligence literacy is not a new concept; it is often referred to as AI competency in the literature. Currently, there is no unified definition of AI literacy among scholars, and different scholars have provided various interpretations. Long and Magerko (2020) listed 16 core AI literacy competencies and defined AI literacy as "a set of competencies that enable 
individuals  to  critically  evaluate  AI  technologies, communicate and collaborate effectively with AI, and use AI as a tool in online, home, and workplace settings"[23]. Ng et al. (2021) pointed out that "learners should not only know how to  use  AI  applications  but  should  also  be  instilled  with fundamental AI concepts for their future careers, as well as the ethical  issues  of  AI  applications,  to  become  responsible citizens"[24]. Wang et al. (2022) proposed that AI literacy refers to the ability to correctly identify, use, and evaluate AI- related  products  in  accordance  with  ethical  standards[11]. Carolus  et  al.  (2023)  stated  that  AI  literacy  covers "competencies needed to interact with AI technology in a self- determined  and  rational  manner"[25].  Pinski  and  Benlian (2023) provided a definition of General AI literacy, describing it  as  "humans'  socio-technical  competence  consisting  of knowledge  regarding  human  and  AI  actors  in  human-AI interaction, knowledge of the AI process steps, that is input, processing, and output, and experience in AI interaction"[26]. Despite these and other attempts to define AI literacy, there is still no clear consensus on which specific skills fall under the umbrella term of AI literacy. However, researchers seem to agree  that  AI  literacy  is  aimed  at  non-experts,  that  is, laypersons who have not received specific training in AI or computer  science.  These  individuals  may  be  classified  as consumers of AI or individuals who interact with AI in a professional  manner[27].  Given  the  ambiguity  of  the definitions, this paper posits that AI literacy for ordinary users without  professional  AI  training  refers  to  the  ability  to correctly understand the basic principles of AI, use AI tools or applications  ethically,  and  critically  evaluate  the  AI  tools themselves or the content generated by these tools. 
B. Measurement of Artificial Intelligence Literacy 
Compared  to  the  conceptual  research  on  AI  literacy, measurement research is somewhat lagging and has produced fewer overall results until recent years when AI technology began to be widely applied in people's lives and work. As mentioned above, developing AI literacy measurement tools is crucial for assessing individuals' AI literacy levels, which is essential for conducting digital literacy initiatives and bridging the digital divide, especially as differences in AI literacy levels become a new source of the digital divide. Wang et al. (2022) developed an AI literacy scale that has gained widespread attention  and  citation,  including  four  dimensions: "Awareness" "Usage" "Evaluation" and "Ethics". This scale was developed to measure ordinary users' AI literacy[11]. Laupichler et al. (2023) developed an AI literacy scale for non- experts  (SNAIL),  which  comprises  three  dimensions: "Technical Understanding" "Critical Appraisal" and "Practical Application" with a total of 31 items[14]. Due to the increasing focus on AI literacy and AI education as research topics in recent years[28]-[29], some scholars have begun studying AI literacy among students, but the primary focus has been on secondary school students[15],[30]-[31]. III. Method 
Based on the classic scale development procedures initially proposed by Churchill (1979)[32] and the subsequent practical applications  in  AI  literacy  scale  development,  this  study followed several fundamental steps for the development and validation of the scale: (1) initial scale construction; (2) formal data collection; (3) exploratory factor analysis (EFA); and (4) confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). 
The initial scale construction phase primarily included the construction  of  the  AI  literacy  framework,  collection  and organization of the item pool, initial item purification, and pilot  testing.  The  framework  construction  and  item  pool organization  were  based  on  extensive  literature  reviews. Subsequently, we purified the initial items mainly through expert  evaluations.  Following  this,  we  invited  25  Chinese college students to participate in a pilot test and conducted in- depth  interviews  to  assess  the  acceptability  and comprehensibility of the initial questionnaire. Based on the feedback, we further refined the scale, resulting in the initial version of the scale. 
After constructing the initial scale, formal data collection commenced.  We  conducted  the  survey  through  random sampling, ensuring anonymity and promising respondents that their information would be kept strictly confidential and not used for any illegal purposes. Completing the questionnaire took approximately 3-5 minutes. Ultimately, we collected 493 questionnaires from five universities in Zhejiang Province, China, of which 448 were valid. Following previous research practices, we divided the sample into two parts: sample A and sample  B,  used  for  exploratory  factor  analysis  and confirmatory factor analysis, respectively. 
During  the  exploratory  factor  analysis  phase,  we  used sample A and applied principal component analysis in SPSS to determine the factor structure, deleting non-compliant items based on certain criteria. In the confirmatory factor analysis phase,  we  used  sample  B  to  validate  the  factor  structure identified in the exploratory factor analysis phase. We utilized AMOS software for the analysis and tested the model fit. Finally, we examined the model's convergent validity and discriminant validity, further demonstrating the rationality of the factor structure. 
IV. Scale Development Process
A.Initial Scale Construction
Based on the AI literacy framework established by Wang et al.[11], we conceptualized an original AI literacy framework comprising four dimensions: Awareness, Usage, Evaluation, and Ethics. Awareness refers to the understanding of AI concepts and basic principles. Usage denotes the ability to use AI in daily life and learning. Evaluation signifies the ability to critically assess AI tools and AI-generated content. Ethics involves the ethical and safe use of AI. 
We  incorporated  relevant  items  from  previous studies[11],[13]-[15],[23], and adapted some items to fit the local internet environment in China, thereby determining the initial  items  for  the  four  dimensions  of  the  AI  literacy framework. Subsequently, we employed an expert validation approach to purify the initial items, making the following key modifications: first, we removed items that were not suitable for the living and learning characteristics of Chinese college students. Second, we revised items that were inaccurately phrased or prone to causing misunderstandings. 
Additionally, to further refine the questionnaire design, the authors  conducted  in-depth  interviews  with  a  sample  of college students. Initially, respondents were asked to complete the original version of the questionnaire and were then asked if they could understand all the questions and complete the questionnaire smoothly. The respondents indicated that they could understand all the questions well, but some items needed adjustments to facilitate better responses. For example, the respondents mentioned that they did not adapt well to reverse- worded  items.  Therefore,  we  changed  all  reverse-worded items to positive ones to maintain consistency with the style of other items. For instance, the original item "I am never vigilant about  privacy  and  information  security  issues  when  using artificial intelligence applications or products." was revised to "I am always vigilant about privacy and information security issues  when  using  artificial  intelligence  applications  or products."Next, we randomly selected some tasks from these items and required respondents to perform them on-site during the  interviews  (for  example,  logging  into  AI  tools  and interacting with them) to test the consistency between their self-assessed AI skills and their actual AI skills. We found that the participants' self-reported answers were consistent with their actual behaviors and abilities. C. Exploratory Factor Analysis
We conducted exploratory factor analysis (EFA) using SPSS 24 to test the proposed factor structure. The Kaiser-Meyer- Olkin (KMO) measure and Bartlett's test of sphericity were used to examine the suitability of the data for factor analysis. A KMO value greater than 0.5 and a significant Bartlett's test indicate that the sample is suitable for principal component analysis[33]. The results showed that the KMO value was 0.855 and Bartlett's test of sphericity was significant (Sig. = 0.000, p < 0.01), indicating that the data were appropriate for EFA. 
Following  previous  research,  we  adopted  the  principal component  analysis  method  with  maximum  orthogonal rotation and extracted factors with eigenvalues greater than 1. According to previous studies[34], items were deleted based on the following criteria: 
1. Items with factor loadings less than 0.5 after rotation were 
deleted. 
2. Items  that  were  clearly  inconsistent  with  the  factor's 
meaning were deleted. 
3. Items  with  high  cross-loadings  (greater  than  0.5)  on 
multiple factors were deleted. 
4. Factors with fewer than three items were deleted.

D. Confirmatory Factor Analysis
To validate the factor structure identified in the exploratory factor  analysis,  confirmatory  factor  analysis  (CFA)  was conducted using AMOS 24.0 software with the maximum likelihood estimation method. The model fit was evaluated using model fit indices. The model and fit results are shown in Figure 1.

Based  on  the  suggestions  by  Hair  et  al.[35],  this  study selected relevant fit indices to assess the model's fit, focusing on absolute fit indices (CMIN/df, RMSEA) and incremental fit indices (NFI, CFI)[36]. The chi-square value (CMIN) is an indicator for judging the overall model fit, used to assess the difference  between  the  sample  covariance  matrix  and  the theoretical  model  covariance  matrix. V. Discussion 
A .Major Findings 
In recent years, research in the field of artificial intelligence has rapidly advanced. More and more scholars are beginning to consider the prerequisites and influencing factors for using AI  technologies,  with  AI  literacy  being  a  critical  aspect. However, there is a need for more theoretical knowledge and reliable, effective tools to measure developments in this area. In our current contribution, we propose a thoroughly tested scale to measure AI literacy among Chinese college students, 
which is considered a key component in promoting digital inclusion. 
The development of the scale began with a critical review of existing literature, ensuring that all proposed items were well-established  in  previous  research.  Additionally,  we avoided  items  related  to  specific  platforms  or  activities, allowing these items to be used for a considerable amount of time, as they do not depend on the type of activity or platform. Meanwhile, we optimized the initial item pool through expert validation  and  focus  group  interviews,  discussing  the acceptability  of  the  questionnaire. It also holds substantial value for bridging the digital divide, ensuring that every student can benefit from AI technologies. This scale can provide  universities  with  an  effective  tool  to  understand students'  AI  literacy  levels  and  aid  in  evaluating  the effectiveness of AI education. Universities can use the specific scores from the four dimensions of this scale to implement differentiated training, thereby improving training efficiency. 
B.Practical Implications 
To  comprehensively  enhance  the  AI  literacy  of  university students,  institutions  of  higher  education  can  implement  a series of systematic and comprehensive measures across four dimensions of AI literacy. Firstly, enhancing AI awareness is crucial.  Universities  should  offer  courses  such  as "Introduction to Artificial Intelligence" that cover basic AI concepts, principles, and application scenarios to effectively strengthen  students'  foundational  knowledge  of  AI. Additionally, regularly organizing lectures and seminars that introduce cutting-edge AI technologies and applications can help students stay abreast of technological advancements and broaden their horizons. In terms of improving AI usage skills, universities  should  encourage  students  to  use  AI  tools reasonably and provide corresponding training and guidance. However, it is also essential to establish regulatory systems to ensure students use AI technology within reasonable limits, maintaining academic integrity, and avoiding over-reliance on AI, which could affect their autonomous learning capabilities. To strengthen AI evaluation skills, universities can use case studies and specialized assessment projects to teach students how to evaluate the effectiveness and potential risks of AI applications, thereby enhancing their evaluation capabilities. In  the  realm  of  AI  ethics  education,  universities  should emphasize the ethical and safety education related to artificial intelligence, ensuring that students adhere to relevant ethical standards and legal regulations while using AI technologies, and  increasing  their  awareness  of  privacy  protection  and ethical standards. 
While  enhancing  the  overall  AI  literacy  of  university students, special attention should be given to particular groups such as female students, implementing targeted measures to help them improve their AI literacy. As the research results indicate,  female  students  exhibit  disadvantages  in  AI awareness, usage, and evaluation. This may be due to their insufficient grasp of AI principles or a lack of confidence in information technologies such as AI. Therefore, universities should provide personalized support, establish special tutoring classes for female students, and offer more learning resources and support to help them improve their understanding and evaluation capabilities of AI. A mentorship system could be established,  where  mentors  guide  female  students  through their challenges and questions in AI learning. Furthermore, 
universities  should  create  more  opportunities  for  female students to interact with and use AI technologies, encouraging them to actively participate in AI-related clubs and community activities to increase their interest and engagement in AI and boost their self-efficacy. By implementing these practical and feasible measures, universities can ensure that every student benefits from the advancements brought by AI technologies. This approach not only facilitates the digital transformation of higher  education  but  also  helps  bridge  the  digital  divide, promoting  equitable  access  to  artificial  intelligence technologies[45]. 
Meanwhile, it is essential to regularly assess AI literacy among  university  students.  Only  by  understanding  which aspects of AI literacy students score lower on can targeted training  be  effectively  conducted.  When  conducting assessments, it is recommended to use this scale flexibly and make adjustments based on the discipline or major of the individuals being assessed, as the AILS-CCS scale developed in this study is intended for general college students rather than students in specific fields. 
C. Limitations and Further Research
This study has certain limitations that need to be addressed in future research. First, although the development process of  AILS-CCS  was  informed  by  extensive  literature  and research  frameworks,  it  cannot  be  guaranteed  that  these sources encompass all aspects of AI literacy. There may still be unidentified areas of knowledge, indicating that much work  remains  to  be  done  on  this  topic.  Although  this questionnaire  is  relatively  comprehensive,  it  currently includes only four dimensions. As AI technology continues to evolve, additional issues may arise that require attention. Therefore,  future  research  should  strive  to  expand  the dimensions and items of the AI literacy scale and ensure that the scale is continuously updated. 
Secondly, the AILS-CCS questionnaire is based on self- assessment  by  respondents,  but  a  limitation  of  self- assessment  questionnaires  is  that  the  results  may  be influenced by the subjective biases of the respondents. Thus, it is important to not only develop subjective scales but also to  create  objective  scales,  such  as  using  true/false  or multiple-choice  questions  to  measure  respondents'  AI literacy levels. Additionally, this study primarily employed questionnaire surveys; future research may need to explore other  methodologies.  For  example,  experimental  testing methods under controlled conditions could be used to collect data for the scale, and the structure and items of the scale could be optimized based on experimental data. 
Finally, this scale was developed based on a sample of Chinese university students, and future research needs to translate the scale into other languages to test its applicability in different linguistic and cultural contexts. This would help enhance the international applicability of the scale. Ensuring measurement invariance is an important condition for using AILS-CCS  cross-linguistically  and  culturally. VI. Conclusion 
In the AI era, AI literacy has become a fundamental skill and essential literacy for citizens. As digital natives, enhancing AI literacy among college students plays a vital role in promoting the sustainable development of higher education. Conducting AI literacy training is an important way to enhance college students'  AI  literacy,  and  scientifically  and  effectively assessing college students' AI literacy levels is the foundation for digital literacy initiatives. Therefore, developing an AI literacy scale for college students is crucial. 
The main contribution of this study is the development and validation of an AI literacy scale for Chinese college students (AILS-CCS),  enriching  the  research  on  AI  literacy  in  the context of developing countries. We primarily employed four steps to achieve the research objectives. The first step was the initial scale construction, mainly through a literature review to build a preliminary framework and initial item pool. Then, expert evaluations, pilot testing, and in-depth interviews were used to modify and refine the initial items, forming the first version of the AILS-CCS. We ensured that all suggested items genuinely reflected the AI literacy of college students. The second  step  involved  formal  survey  sampling,  with  valid questionnaires  being  divided  into  samples  A  and  B  for subsequent  analysis  and  validation.  The  third  step  was conducting exploratory factor analysis to refine the potential scale  structure  and  related  items.  The  fourth  step  was confirmatory  factor  analysis  to  test  the  consistency  of  the factor structure and to examine the model's convergent and discriminant validity. Through these steps, this study proposed a  reliable  AILS-CCS,  which  consists  of  four  dimensions: Awareness, Usage, Evaluation, and Ethics, encompassing 15 items. This represents an original contribution to the field of AI  research  in  developing  countries,  and  the  scale  holds significant  theoretical  and  practical  implications  for  the assessment, training, and enhancement of AI literacy among college students in developing countries in the future.
