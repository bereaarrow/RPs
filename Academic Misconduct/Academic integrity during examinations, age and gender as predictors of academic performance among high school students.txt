Academic  integrity during examinations, age  and  gender as predictors of  academic performance among  high  school  students 
Moses C. Ossai *, Nathaniel Ethe, Dennis E. Edougha, Onomiroro D. Okeh 
Department of Educational and Counselling Psychology, Delta State College of Education, Mosogar, PMB 4088, Sapele, Nigeria   A R T I C L E I N F O   A B S T R A C T  

Keywords: 
Academic Integrity Age 
Gender 
Academic Performance Examinations 
The study sought to determine if students academic integrity during examinations, age and gender predict their academic performance and examine its implications for adopting preventive actions against cheating in exam- inations. It was based on the Modified Theory of Planned Behaviour. Analysis of data collected with the aid of Academic  Integrity  Measurement  Instrument  (AIMI) led  to  significant  findings  that  Academic  Integrity  during examinations and Age significantly predict Academic Performance. There was significant predictive relationship between Age and Academic Integrity during examinations as well as between Age and Academic Performance. Also, there was a statistically significant difference between male and female students in the Academic Integrity during  examinations  as  well  as  in  their  Academic  Performances.  Whereas  mean  Academic  Integrity  score  of female students were slightly higher than that of the males; the mean Academic Performance of male students was  higher  than  that  of the  female  students.  These  findings  have  implications  for  test  development, adminis - tration  and  students’  academic  integrity  policies  and  practices.  Test  developers  and  administrators  should consider  inclusion  of a  segment  in  the  question  papers  that  assess  students’ academic  integrity  especially  in developing countries like Nigeria. The essence of such inclusion is to determine the relationship between students academic integrity quotient and their academic performance. In other words, the authors strongly recommend to the examination bodies and stakeholders to consider the possibility of determining the proportion of a student’s academic performance attributable to academic dishonesty or honesty.   

1. Introduction 
Relationship  between  academic  integrity  during  examinations and students’ academic performance is a relatively new area that has started attracting  the  attention  of  researchers  in  response  to  the  prevailing culture  of  examination  malpractices  in  schools  around  the  world.  In several studies, some factors have been identified as predictors of stu- dents academic performances such as study habits, examination anxiety, peer  influence,  honour  codes,  and  so  on.  For  instance,  Sasi  and  Anju (2020) in their study on the relationship between study habits and ac- ademic achievements of high school students in India found very strong correlation between the two variables. Similar results were obtained in the studies by Magulod (2019); Williams et al. (2013); and, Moeinikia and Zahed-Babelan (2010). It follows the course of logical reasoning to assume that students who study well for examinations are more likely to perform better than those who do not prepare adequately for the same examinations. It has also been proven in research studies that students who  suffer  from  high  examination  anxiety  have  more  tendency  to 
perform poorly in school examinations (DordiNejad et al., 2011; Balo-
gun et al., 2017; Zamani and Pouratashi, 2018). Given that these vari- ables  (study  habits  and  test  anxiety,  among  other  individual  and institutional factors) formed the basis for the earlier studies on academic integrity  such  as  McCabe  et  al.  (1996); McCabe  and  Trevino  (1997); McCabe  et  al.  (1999),  the  present  study  advanced  the  frontier  by generating  items  for  the  measurement of students’ academic  integrity during  examinations from  the  constructs  of study  habits,  examination anxiety, examination ethics, past experience and moral background of students and then testing the predictive relationship between each stu-
dent’s composite score on academic integrity during examinations and academic performance. 
Furthermore,  the  role  of  age  and  gender  in  the  determination  of academic performance and tendency to engage in academic dishonesty has been inconclusive. Whereas some previous studies found significant differences in academic performance and academic integrity on the basis of age  and  gender,  some  others  did  not.  For  instance,  the  studies  by McCabe  and  Trevino  (1997);  Hendershott  et  al.  (1999);  Becker  and

The background to this study lies in the ravaging effects of cheating in examinations or what is called “examination malpractices” in some countries. The menace of examination malpractices has threatened the very essence of education. In a twelve year study from 2002 to 2015 in the United States of America (USA), McCabe (2020) reported that: “95% 
of the surveyed students admitted to cheating on a test and homework, or  committing”  other  forms  of  academic  fraud.  Punch  Newspaper Editorial (2019) reported that China introduced seven-year jail term for 
those caught cheating in examinations from 2016 onwards. They further deployed  information  and  communication  technology  tools  to  scan students’ dress and paraphernalia before they are admitted into exam-
ination  halls.  In the  United  Kingdom,  there  was  11%  increase  in  pen- alties for cheating in examinations in 2019 compared to 2018 according 
to the Office of Qualifications and Examinations Regulation (2019). In Nigeria, the situation is so pathetic that Magaji (2019) stated that aca-
demic dishonesty is responsible for “most cases of collapsed buildings, death  through  medical  negligence,  drug  trafficking,  armed  robbery, drug adulteration, separatist agitation, kidnapping, insurgency” among 
other vices. Magaji could make such claims because there are significant number  of  students  caught  engaging  in  examination  malpractices  in public examinations conducted in Nigeria by West African Examinations Council  (WAEC); National  Examinations  Council  (NECO);  and,  Joint Admissions and Matriculation Board (JAMB). from  fraudulent  websites  as  well  as  employing  gadgets  that  transmit answers to students in the examination halls. 
The negative consequences of examination malpractices in Nigeria is 
so  far-reaching  that  Onyechere  (2017)  stated  they  contribute  to  the economic, social and political underdevelopment of Nigeria. There is no doubt  that  examination  malpractice  is  a  plaque  which  has  defied  all previous  measures  adopted  to  curb  it  and  that  its  consequences have been  very  disastrous  to  the  extent  that  the  governments, examination bodies  and  educational  institutions  in  Nigeria  have  adopted  punitive measures to curtail it but to no avail. For example, the alarming resur - gence  of examination malpractices during  the  Babangida  and  Abacha regimes  and  the  public  outcry  which  it  generated  culminated  in  the promulgation of a Decree in 1999 tagged “Examination Malpractices Act 
No. 33 of 1999′′by the Abdulsalami Abubakar administration. The Act failed to curb the menace of examination malpractices in Nigeria. It has been criticized for being too weak in some areas (such as Placing a fine of N500,  000  on  culprits);  too  strict  in  other  areas  (such  as 10–15  years imprisonment);  and  not  implementable  in  some  instances  (such  as banning  a culprit  from  future  examinations (Ogunlowo,  Onyechere  as cited in Ossai, 2013). 
In  view  of  the  negative  impacts  of  examination  malpractices  to educational values,  there  is need  for  research  scholars  to  initiate  pre- ventive  strategies  to  check  them. to  examination malpractices could  be  identified  and  reformed  before they sit for examinations. If the overall goal of education is to bequeath

1.1. Research questions 
The  following  research  questions  guided  the  study:  What  relevant psychological  constructs  could  adequately constitute  the  blueprint  for measuring academic integrity during examinations among high school students?;  What  are  the  psychometric  properties  of  the  Academic Integrity  Measurement  Instrument?  Does  the  Academic  Integrity  and Age of  high  school  students  predict  their  academic  performance  ?  Is there significant relationship between Gender and the academic integ -
rity scores and academic performance of the high school students? What are the implications of the findings of this study for adopting proactive strategies  towards  preventing students  from  engaging  in  examination malpractices? 
1.2. Theoretical framework 
This  study  is based  on  the  Modified  Theory  of Planned  Behaviour (MTPB). MTPB is an offshoot of the Theory of Planned Behaviour (TPB). TPB as enunciated and  espoused by Ajzen and Fishbein  (1980);  Ajzen (1985, 1991); and Ajzen and Madden (1986) identified three factors that combine to predict intention and behaviour. These are “attitudes toward 
the  behaviour, subjective  norms,  and  perceived  behavioural control”. For instance, as applicable to the present study, a student’s tendency to engage in “examination malpractices” is determinable from his attitude towards  “examination  malpractices”;  subjective  norms  (acceptability beliefs  in  student’s  environment) of “examination malpractices”; and, perceived behavioural control (how easy it is for students) to engage in “examination malpractices” without being caught. Items in the research istrument developed by the researchers for this study tagged “Academic Integrity  Measurement  Instrument  (AIMI)”  elicit  responses  that  are indicative of students’ dispositions in the tripod dimensions of TPB and 
the  additional  components  that  make  up  the  MTPB such  as moral  up- bringing, past experience and personality factors. These added compo - nents to TPB (moral upbringing, past experience and personality factors) emanated from the realization that there are other variables that could enhance or inhibit the three original factors. The three original elements of TPB which comprised of Attitude towards Behaviour (ATB), Subjec-
tive  Norms  (SN)  and  Perceived  Behavioral  Control  (PBC)  could  be influenced  by  demographic  factors,  moral  obligation  and  moral reasoning because the phenomenon of academic dishonesty is not just a spontaneous event but an activity that is planned and coordinated before execution. Several research studies have proved this fact. For instance, Passow et al. (2006) initiated the concept of MTPB by introducing more variables to the theory of planned behaviour. These variables are “de- mographics, pre-college cheating behavior, co-curricular participation” 
as  additions  to  TPB’s “moral  obligation  not  to  cheat,  attitudes  about cheating,  evaluation  of  the  costs  and  benefits  of  cheating,  perceived social pressures to cheat or not to cheat, and perceived effectiveness of academic  dishonesty  policies”.  They  found  all  eight  variables  to  be significant  predictors  of  cheating  behaviour  in  examinations.  Madara et  al.  (2016)  reviewed  twenty-two  theories  relevant  to  explaining cheating behaviour in school examinations including Kohlberg’s Theory 
of  Moral  Development  and  came  to  the  conclusion  that  “human- - behaviour, such as cheating” is such a broad and complex construct that 
a single  theory  may  not  be  sufficient  to  explain  it,  hence  they  recom - mended  combining  or  integrating several-different theories  or  models such as the MTPB for research studies in this field. Harding et al. (2007) 
found  that  the  modified  theory  of  planned  behavior  proved  to  be consistent  in  predicting  students  cheating  behaviour  in  examinations irrespective of the context in which it was applied. Bagraim et al. (2014) applied the TPB model in their study among South African students and 
found that “TPB significantly explains dishonest academic behaviour”. They  recommended  that  the  application  of  TPB  models  in  studies focusing  on  prediction  of  dishonest  academic  behaviour  should  be adopted  by  other  researchers in  this  field.  It  was  on  the  basis  of the findings and recommendations of these previous studies that Ossai et al. (2014) and Ossai et al. (2020) utilized the TPB and MTPB in their studies which yielded examination behaviour measurement instruments named Examination  Behaviour  Inventory  (EBI)  and  Tertiary  Examination Behaviour Inventory (TEBI) respectively. Most recent research by Juan 
et  al.  (2022)  found  the  MTPB most  significant  in  the  prediction  of dishonest academic behaviour among Chinese students. It is interesting to note that these previous studies also involved prediction of academic cheating  behavior  from  measures  of  attitude  towards  cheating  in  ex- aminations,  subjective  norms  of  the  examinations,  perceived  permis - siveness  of  the  examination  conditions,  demographic  factors,  moral obligation or reasoning, and past experience in cheating. These factors were  taken  into  consideration in the  construction of the  items  for  the AIMI. For  instance,  students’  study  habits  and  levels  of  examination anxiety could predicate their disposition towards examination integrity (Salehi and Gholampour; , 2021; Abdulmumin et al., 2020). Moreover, demographic factors such as age, gender, socioeconomic background as well as past experience have been implicated as predictors of students’ tendency  to  cheat  in  examinations  or  their  academic  performance (Ukwetang  et  al.  (2021);  McCabe  and  Trevino  (1997);  Hendershott ,1999;  Becker  and  Ulstad  (2007);  Olasehinde-Williams  et  al.,  2011); Zhang  et  al.  (2018)).  In  the  light  of  the  findings  from  these  related previous  studies,  the  present  study  sought  to  develop  an  instrument (AIMI) that  encapsulates  the  aforementioned  attributes  and  use  it  to measure  the  predisposition  of  students  towards  academic  integrity during examination and then compare the scores of the AIMI with their academic performance. 
The  advantage  of  the  MTPB over  other  theories  and  models  that explain some constructs used in the present study, such as moral back- ground  or  obligation,  is  that  it  amalgamates  principles  from  various theories  into  one.  For  instance,  some  aspects  of Kohlberg’s  theory  of moral development are accommodated in MTPB. The moral obligation and moral reasoning aspect of MTPB is rooted in Kohlberg’s theoretical assertion that society’s or institutional rules and regulations or laws and the  sanctions  meted  to  violators  determine  moral  development at  the “pre-conventional”  and  “conventional”  levels  (Sanders,  2022).  Moral reasoning  precedes  moral  action.  Therefore,  an  individual  builds  up moral  assets  from  the  training  received  at  home,  school,  social  in- teractions,  societal  and  institutional  norms  and  perceived  behavioral controls. These morality builders interfaces with other variables such as examination  anxiety,  study  habits,  age,  gender,  attitude  towards  ex- amination malpractices and past experience to determine how an indi- vidual will react to situations that demand academic honesty. This is the thrust  of the  MTPB upon  which  this  study  is based.  The  inclusion  of “ethics”  or “values”  governing certain kinds  of behaviour such as “ex- amination ethics”  among  the  variables  relevant  to  academic  integrity during  examinations is given  credence  by  Britannica  (2023)  where  it was stated that every society or institution has “moral rules”  or ethics that regulates individual behaviour.This study proposes that a students’ understanding,  internalization  and  possession  of  these  attributes  are measurable  with  an  instrument  that  contains  items  measuring  Study Habits;  Examination  Ethics;  Examination  Anxiety;  Moral  Background; Attitude towards Cheating in Examinations (Exam Attitude); and, Past Experience  in  Cheating  in  Examinations  (Past  Experience),  Age  and Gender. This study will prove whether propensity for “immoral actions” during examinations (examination malpractices) is more attributable to academically weak  students than  academically well prepared students 
by comparing students’ scores on AIMI and their academic performance. 
2. Method 
Design: Survey research design was used in the study. The population 
of the  study  consisted  of final  year  high  school  students  in Nigeria.  A sample  of 3214  students  were  drawn  from  6 geopolitical  zones  of the country and the Federal Capital Territory using multistage proportion -
ate random sampling technique which led to the selection of 1274 males and 1940 females. 
Instrument: data was collected with the aid of an Academic Integrity Measurement Instrument (AIMI) that was developed by the authors. It consisted of Sections A and B. Section A elicited responses on the stu-
dents Age, Gender among other variables and Section B had an initial total of 102 items made up of 6 segments. First segment measured Study Habits  with  16  items;  second  segment  measured  Examination  Ethics with  20  items;  third  segment  measured  Examination  Anxiety  with  20 items; fourth segment measured Moral Background with 15 items; fifth segment  measured  Attitude  towards  Cheating  in  Examinations (Exam Attitude) with 11 items; and, the sixth segment measured Past Experi-
ence in Cheating in Examinations (Past Experience) with 20 items.The AIMI was  first  subjected  to  face  and  content  validity  by 3 expert  Pro- fessors in the fields of educational measurement and evaluation, coun- selling psycho-metrics and counselling psychology. Their critiques and suggestions were used to fine tune the items and in some cases recon -
struct certain items and this reduced the items to 102 which were then subjected  to  a pilot  study  with  a sample  of 150  subjects  and  the  data generated was analyzed with Principal Component Factor Analysis and Rotated Component Matrix. Measure of internal consistency for the 102 
items produced Cronbach Alpha index of.91 while the split half method yielded Cronbach Alpha indices of.83 and.87 for each of the two parts of 
51  items;  and  Spearman  Brown  Coefficient  of.82.  The  criterion  of “Cronbach Alpha if Item Deleted” is satisfactory for all 102 items with 
the  least  index  being.91  (see  Appendix  A) but  the  items  were  further proned down to a total of 40 by means of Rotated Component Matrix and Factor Loading for each segment (see Table 4). The 40-item version of 
the  AIMI was  tested  for  reliability  and  internal  consistency  (Table  3,) and used to elicit responses from the 3214 sampled students. In addition to  the  40-item  AIMI, the  average  academic  performance score  in  per- centage for the 3214 sampled students in their last school term exami - nation was collected from their school authorities for comparison with the AIMI scores. 
The idea for developing the AIMI emanated from the observation in research literature that each of the segments of AIMI is associated with academic dishonesty. For instance, Oran et al. (2015) found that study habits is closely associated with nursing students academic honesty. In another study by Lambert et al. (2003), Past experience was found to be 
the  most  significant  predictor  of  academic  dishonesty.  Examination anxiety is also a strong predictor of academic integrity. In Abuh (2022), test  or  examination anxiety  “accounted  for  56.2%  of the  variation  in academic dishonesty”. Attitude and moral background are also related to academic honesty as seen in the study by Bassey and Iruoje  (2016) where  they  investigated  “test  anxiety,  attitude  to  schooling,  parental influence,  and  peer  pressure  as  predictors  of  students  cheating  ten- dencies” among secondary school students in Nigeria. The items in the examination ethics component of AIMI were developed by the authors in line with the rules and regulations of WAEC, the foremost high school certificate  examination  body  in  West  Africa.  Given  that  most  of  the previous instruments designed for measuring academic honesty focused on just one or two of the predictor variables, the AIMI stands out because 
it  consists  of items  covering  the  six predictor  variables  as highlighted above. AIMI was, therefore, conceived as an instrument that covers all the essential components of the Modified Theory of Planned Behaviour (MTPB), namely, attitude towards cheating in examinations, subjective norms of the examinations, perceived permissiveness of the examination conditions,  demographic  factors,  moral  obligation  or  reasoning,  and past  experience  (details  are  presented  in  the  Theoretical  Framework section). A four-point scale format was adopted where response to each 
item ranges from SA = Strongly Agree, A = Agree, D = Disagree and SD 
= Strongly Disagree. Depending on the direction of an item SA could be scored as 4 or 1; A as 3 or 2; D as 2 or 3 and SD as 1 or 4. This scoring pattern  helped  to  check  faking  of responses.  The  names  of the  six di- mensions were derived from common trait or attributes applicable to the 
items loading on each component (see Table 6 and Appendix B Rotated Component Matrix of the 40-items). International Center for Academic Integrity [ICAI] (2019) defined academic integrity “as a commitment … to  six  fundamental  values:  honesty,  trust,  fairness,  respect,  re- sponsibility,  and  courage”. From  the  students’ perspectives, these  six values  are  measured  by  items  in  the  Moral  Background,  Examination Ethics,  Attitude  towards  Cheating,  Past  Experience,  Study  Habits  and Examination Anxiety  components of AIMI. For  instance,  items  28–34 measure  honesty  or  moral  integrity;  items  5–11  measure  fairness  and respect  for  examination  ethics;  items  21–27  measure  trust  and  re- sponsibility or past experience of engaging in examination malpractices; 
and, items 1,2,3,4 measure courage or the lack of it as demonstrated by study  habits  of students  ahead  of examination as well  as items  12–20 that  measures  the  courage  of  students  in  dealing  with  examination anxiety (see Appendix B). 
Method of Data Analysis: The researchers personally administered the copies  of the  AIMI to  the  3214  respondents and  all  3214  copies  were correctly filled and returned. The Point Biserial Correlation was used to 
run the analysis for determining the association between Gender, Aca- demic Integrity during Examinations and Academic Performance since 
the independent variable (gender) is dichotomous while the dependent variables  (examination  integrity  and  academic  performance)  are continuous  or  scale  variables. 5. Conclusion 
Students’ academic integrity during examinations is the product of their  Study  Habits;  Examination  Ethics;  Examination  Anxiety;  Moral Background; Attitude towards Cheating in Examinations (Examination Attitude);  and,  Past  Experience  of  Cheating  in  Examinations  (Past Experience). It has been found to be a significant predictor of academic performance of students. Thus, each of these constructs that contributed 
items for the academic integrity measurement instrument are potential determinants of students academic performance. Guided students’ pro- gramme  of  activities  through  teaching,  orientation  and  counselling could enhance students confidence and change their predisposition to-
wards cheating in examinations. The age and gender of students should also  be  considered  when  drawing  up  proactive  strategies  aimed  at improving the academic integrity of students. 
The implications of the findings of this study for test development, administration and  students’ academic  integrity  policies  and practices 
are numerous. First, test developers and administrators should consider inclusion of a segment in the question papers that assess students’ aca-
demic  integrity  especially  in  developing  countries  like  Nigeria.  The essence of such inclusion is to determine the relationship between stu- dents  academic integrity  quotient and their  academic  performance. In other words, the authors strongly recommend to the examination bodies and stakeholders to consider the possibility of determining the propor - tion  of  a  student’s  academic  performance  attributable  to  academic dishonesty. This is very possible through further development of stan- dardized instruments such as the AIMI for measuring students’ academic integrity quotient. If Intelligence Quotient (IQ) can be measured, then it is  also  possible  to  measure  Academic  Integrity  Quotient  (AIQ).  The usefulness  of AIQ will  not  only  be  for  comparism  with  academic  per- formance but will also be used by other stakeholders in education and 
the industry in taking decisions. This fact is supported by Bagraim et al. (2014)  in  their  study  that  focused  on  “Understanding  dishonest  aca- demic behavior amongst business students: The business leaders of the future” where they recommended that “Validated TPB models that help 
to predict dishonest academic behaviour should be helpful in designing interventions  to  decrease  academic  dishonesty  and  thereby  help  to prevent later unethical business practices”. The present study keyed into 
their recommendation and developed the AIMI which is an instrument based on the MTPB model for predicting students’ academic dishonesty during examinations and further determined the instrument’s predictive power for academic performance. Second, School Counsellors and Psy- chologists could use AIMI scores as a basis for identifying and inviting students for preventive counselling against examination malpractices or academic dishonesty. Such identified students will be helped to improve 
their  study  habits  and  reduce  their  high  examination  anxiety.  Ossai (2012, 2013) and UNESCO (2000a)(2000b) showed that Guidance and Counselling  programmes  in  the  school  system  help  students  improve their study habits and normalize high examination anxiety. A number of items  in  the  AIMI assess  Study  Habits  and  Examination  Anxiety.  It is, therefore, imperative for school counsellors to help students who score low  in these  sections  of AIMI to  improve  their  study  habits  as well  as control  their  examination  anxiety  levels.  The  Ossai  (2011a);  (2000b) (2012);  and  (2013)  studies  identified  poor  study  habits  and  high  ex- amination anxiety as significant correlates of positive attitude towards examination  malpractices.  One  of  the  implications  of  this  finding  by Ossai is that when Guidance Counsellors help students to improve their study habits and reduce their examination anxiety levels, they may be less interested in perpetrating acts of academic dishonesty. Such iden- tified students will be subjected to relevant counselling therapies such as Cognitive  Behaviour  Therapy  (CBT) and  Rational  Emotive  Behaviour Therapy  (REBT) to  reform  or  re-orientate them  before  they  sit for the actual  examinations (Ossai,  2014,  2018). Moreover,  students  who  are identified to be prone to examination malpractices based on their scores in the AIMI will be assisted further through study habits induction and self-regulated learning strategies.
