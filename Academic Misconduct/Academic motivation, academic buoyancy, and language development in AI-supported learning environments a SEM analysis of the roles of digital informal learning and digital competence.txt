Ehsan Namaziandost

   ABSTRACT  
   In the digital era, informal learning through online platforms has 
   become  increasingly  prominent  among  English  as  a  foreign 
   language  (EFL)  learners.  This  study  investigates  the  impact  of 
   digital  informal  learning  and  digital  competence  on  language 
   development,  academic  buoyancy,  and  academic  motivation. 
   Despite  the  growing  body  of  research  on  digital  learning 
   environments,  the  interrelationships  among  these  factors  in  EFL 
   contexts  remain  underexplored.  A  Structural  Equation  Modeling 
   (SEM) analysis was conducted using data from 367 EFL learners in
   Iran.  The  ndings  revealed  that  digital  informal  learning 
   signi cantly  enhanced  language  development  by  fostering 
   academic  motivation  and  buoyancy.  Strong  correlations  were 
   observed  among  academic  buoyancy,  academic  motivation, 
   language  development,  and  digital  competence.  These  results 
   underscore the potential of digital tools to create engaging and 
   supportive  learning  environments,  offering  valuable  insights  for 
   educators  and  policymakers  seeking  to  enhance  language

Introduction
Arti cial intelligence (AI) is transforming language acquisition in EFL contexts, such as Iran,  where  challenges  like  limited  exposure  to  the  target  language  and  large  class sizes  persist  (Rezai,  Soyoof,  and  Reynolds 2024a).  AI-assisted  tools  offer  personalized learning opportunities and authentic practice; however, their effectiveness depends on psychological factors, including academic motivation and buoyancy. Academic motiv - ation, encompassing intrinsic and extrinsic drivers, fosters goal-oriented learning, such as the pursuit of language pro ciency (D rnyei 2001). Academic buoyancy enables lear - ners to navigate challenges and maintain a positive outlook (Martin and Marsh 2003). Literature review
The  integration  of  AI  into  language  instruction  has  transformed  how  learners  access, process, and internalize linguistic knowledge. AI-powered tools, including conversational agents, intelligent tutoring systems, automated feedback platforms, and adaptive learn - ing  environments,  enable  learners  to  engage  with  language  at  any  time  and  in  any place, thus extending education beyond the con nes of traditional classrooms (Zhang 2024). As digital tools become increasingly integral to daily life, technology-enabled infor - mal learning plays a critical role in language development, particularly in contexts where formal instruction is limited (Zhang  2024). Through social media, online gaming, video platforms, and language learning apps, learners actively engage with language without direct  teacher  guidance  (Dizon  and  Gayed  2021;  Song  and  Bonk  2016).  This  shift reflects a broader trend in knowledge acquisition, where learners construct information through self-directed digital interactions. AI-powered personalization fosters autonomy 
by providing adaptive support and real-time engagement to scaffold language learning (Godwin-Jones 2022). AI-powered chatbots promote digital literacy and linguistic compe - tence by offering continuous, context-embedded practice opportunities (Kukulska-Hulme and Viberg 2018). These advancements signal a learner-centered, decentralized paradigm 
in language education, mediated by intelligent systems (Hwang et al. 2023).
Digital competence, which is essential for navigating modern educational environ - ments,  extends  beyond  basic  technical  skills  (Abu  Al-majd  and  Belton  2024;  Ungerer 2016). It encompasses cognitive, technical, and socioemotional abilities that support com - munication,  information  management,  problem-solving,  teamwork,  and  creative expression (Liu et al., 2025b; Ungerer 2016). Carretero, Vuorikari, and Punie (2017) identify  ve  key  components:  information  and  data  literacy,  communication  and  teamwork, digital content production, digital safety, and problem-solving. These skills prove critical in ASLEs, where learners must evaluate AI-generated translations, interpret algorithmic feedback  on  grammar  or  pronunciation,  and  interact  efficiently  with  large  language models through well-crafted prompts (Chiu et al. 2024). Without sufficient digital compe - tence, learners may struggle to understand automated responses, over-rely on sugges - tions,  or  disengage  as  a  result  of  frustration.  Digital  competence  involves  not  only operational skills but also the critical evaluation of digital information and adaptability 
to emerging technologies (Sharpe, Beetham, and De Freitas  2010). Research indicates that  learners  with  strong  digital  competence  manage  their  learning  more  effectively and make informed decisions when using AI-powered language apps (Dogan, Dawson, and Ritzhaupt  2021).
Digital competence and informal learning signi cantly influence learners  motivation, psychological resilience, and linguistic pro ciency in evolving educational landscapes. Digital  informal  learning,  de ned  as  self-directed,  non-institutionalized  learning  that occurs  outside  formal  curricula,  is  facilitated  by  platforms  such  as  social  networks, online  forums,  mobile  apps,  and  ASLEs  (Dizon  and  Gayed  2021;  He  and  Zhu  2017). Unlike  teacher-led  classroom  education,  this  form  of  learning  is  initiated  by  learners, driven by personal interests, immediate needs, or curiosity (Rezai, Soyoof, and Reynolds 2024a). For language learners, digital informal learning provides extended exposure to authentic language use, encompassing cultural references, colloquial expressions, and real-time interactions with native speakers. It also supports repeated practice through adaptive AI-powered tools and gami ed activities, which foster deeper language proces - sing (Soyoof et al. 2023). This approach democratizes access to high-quality language resources, particularly in underfunded educational systems (Lee, Kim, and Sung  2023). Research indicates that learners who engage in digital informal learning exhibit greater autonomy, sustained motivation, and enhanced communicative competence compared 
to those who rely solely on formal instruction (Lai and Zheng 2018).
Digital competence enhances the bene ts of informal learning by enabling learners to effectively engage with digital tools in educational settings. Learners with strong digital skills  can  critically  evaluate  online  content,  collaborate  in  digital  environments,  and utilize AI-powered tools for personalized feedback and self-regulated learning (Abu Al- majd and Belton  2024; He, Zhu, and Questier 2018). As AI becomes integral to language teaching, those with higher digital competence adeptly use features like speech recog - nition,  real-time  translation,  and  adaptive  exercises  (Chiu  et  al.  2024;  Godwin-Jones 2022). For instance, a learner skilled in crafting precise prompts for AI-powered chatbots gains richer linguistic input compared to passive users. This strategic interaction trans - forms AI into an active learning partner, deepening engagement and improving learning quality (Godwin-Jones  2022). Digital competence thus serves as both a gateway and an ampli er for leveraging AI-powered tools in language acquisition.
Digital competence fosters academic motivation, the psychological drive sustaining goal-directed learning (Pintrich and Schunk 2002). Grounded in SDT, academic motivation includes intrinsic and extrinsic forms, with intrinsic motivation being particularly relevant in technology-rich settings (Ryan and Deci  2000). When learners experience autonomy, competence,  and  relatedness  in  digital  environments,  they  maintain  engagement (Ushioda  2020). AI-powered tools  enhance this by  allowing learners to  control when, where, and how they learn, fostering ownership and motivation. Lin et al. (2021) found that EFL learners using AI-powered chatbots for conversation practice reported greater enjoyment and persistence when personalizing interactions. Similarly, Huang, Lu, and Yang (2023) noted that user-friendly interfaces and immediate feedback reduce cognitive strain, boosting learners  con dence and motivation. Digital informal learning thus goes beyond content delivery, cultivating agency and sustained engagement in AISEs.
Academic buoyancy, the ability to recover from everyday academic setbacks like chal - lenging assignments or negative feedback, is closely linked to motivation (Martin and Marsh 2006). Unlike resilience, which addresses major hardships, buoyancy focuses on routine challenges. Research shows buoyant learners view failures as opportunities for growth, displaying persistence, adaptability, and openness to feedback (Diert-Bot Irati and Balbina  2024; Martin, Marsh, and McInerney 2013). In digital environments, where mistakes carry no social stigma, buoyancy is particularly valuable. AI-powered platforms create safe spaces for experimentation, allowing learners to retry exercises, revise work, and receive constructive feedback (Rezai, Soyoof, and Reynolds  2024a). Learners with higher buoyancy are more likely to explore new resources, revisit difficult material, and persist through setbacks, behaviors that enhance language development (Abu Al-majd 
and  Belton  2024).  The iterative nature  of  AI-supported practice fosters  metacognitive reflection, enabling learners to recognize errors and adjust their strategies effectively.
Limited empirical research explores the combined impact of digital informal learning and  digital  competence  on  academic  motivation,  academic  buoyancy,  and  language development in AISEs. While Taş and Bolat (2023) found that gami ed informal learning boosts  motivation,  and  Sj berg  et  al.  (2024)  linked  digital  competence  to  academic success, few studies integrate all  ve factors into a comprehensive model. Most existing research focuses on Western, resource-rich educational settings, assuming widespread technology access. This leaves a gap in understanding these dynamics in EFL contexts like Iran, where formal digital infrastructure may be limited, but informal digital engage - ment via social media and mobile devices is prevalent (Lee, Kim, and Sung  2023; Rezai, Soyoof, and Reynolds 2024a). In such settings, learners rely on self-directed digital activi - ties to supplement formal education, underscoring the importance of digital competence 
and informal learning. This study proposes hypotheses grounded in theoretical models and empirical data to address these gaps. 
H1: Digital informal learning positively influences academic motivation in AISEs.
Research shows that self-directed digital content engagement improves autonomy and personal relevance, two factors that are essential to intrinsic motivation (Lin et al. 2021; Ushioda  2020).  Learners  are  more  likely  to  experience  a  feeling  of  ownership  when they have autonomy over their learning process, including the ability to choose subjects, establish their own pace, and use AI technologies anyway they see  t. Their emotional commitment  and  perseverance  are  reinforced  by  this  feeling  of  choice.  According  to Hamalainen, Nissinen, and Mannonen (2021), learners who participated in digital activities of their own choosing expressed greater levels of involvement, especially when such activities matched their own objectives. 
H2: Digital informal learning positively influences academic buoyancy in AISEs.
Building resilience requires safe experimentation and mistake correction, which are made possible by informal digital settings (He 2024). Learners may participate in iterative learn - ing, bounce back from failures, and improve their capacity to handle academic problems since AI-powered platforms provide repeated practice without the stress of formal assess - ment  (Diert-Bot Irati  and  Balbina  2024).  These  interactions   low  stakes  promote  risk- taking  and  anxiety  reduction,  two  qualities  that  are  critical  for  the  development  of buoyant behaviors. 
H3: Digital informal learning positively influences language development in AISEs.
It has been shown that vocabulary, grammar, and speaking fluency may all be enhanced by using AI-powered tools (Dizon and Gayed  2021; Soyoof et al. 2023). Informal learn- ing s  self-paced  and  individualized  structure  encourages  sustained  engagement  by enabling learners to go over information again, change the level of difficulty, and get content that is appropriate for their level of expertise. Measurable gains in linguistic competency  are  the  result  of  regular  and  meaningful  engagement  with  the  target language over time. 
H4: Digital competence positively influences academic motivation in AISEs.
Strong digital learners are better able to locate useful materials, personalize their learning experiences, and navigate intricate interfaces. According to Ryan and Deci (2000), this enhances their perception of competence and control, which are two essential elements 
of SDT. As noted by Collie et al. (2017), learners who were more pro cient in digital skills expressed more self-assurance and involvement in online learning settings. A feeling of mastery that boosts motivation is a result of their capacity to resolve technical issues and modify tools to suit their requirements. 
H5: Digital competence positively influences academic buoyancy in AISEs.
Being  pro cient  with  digital  technologies  allows  learners  to  solve  problems,  look  for assistance online, and adjust their approach when they encounter difficulties. Academic buoyancy is based on a feeling of mastery, which is fostered by this problem-solving skill (Martin, Marsh, and McInerney 2013). He (2024) discovered that learners with excel - lent digital abilities were more likely to see obstacles as doable rather than overpowering and were more persistent in  nishing challenging online assignments. Even in the face of setbacks, they are able to sustain momentum because to their intelligent use of digital resources. 
H6: Digital competence positively influences language development in AISEs.
Effective use of AI-powered tools, such as grammar checkers, adaptive exercises, and speech  analyzers,  requires  digital  literacy.  Learners  who  can  critically  evaluate  and apply these tools achieve signi cant language competence gains (Chiu et al.  2024; Liu et al. 2025a, 2025b). For example, a digitally competent learner can interpret feedback on intonation or sentence structure to make targeted improvements, while less skilled users may misinterpret or ignore it. Digital competence thus determines how effectively learners convert technology access into language development. Theoretical framework
This study integrates SDT (Deci and Ryan 2000; Ryan et al. 2021) and the TPACK framework (Mishra and Koehler 2006) to examine digital informal learning, digital competence, aca - demic motivation,  academic buoyancy, and language development within AISEs. SDT posits that autonomy, competence, and relatedness serve as key drivers of motivation. Digital  informal  learning  fosters  autonomy  through  self-selected  resources,  enhances competence  through  the  development  of  digital  skills,  and  supports  relatedness through online collaboration (Guan, Li, and Gu  2024). The TPACK framework emphasizes the integration of technological, pedagogical, and content knowledge, highlighting the critical role of digital competence in leveraging AI-powered tools for effective language learning. While SDT explains internal motivational drivers, TPACK underscores the role of teachers in designing supportive environments that promote autonomy and digital skills. This approach aligns with constructivist principles, where active knowledge con - struction fosters resilience and deeper understanding (Piaget  1976; Martin and Marsh 2006). The synergistic interplay of learner-driven digital informal learning, educator-sup - ported digital competence, and the psychological needs outlined in SDT creates a motiv - ating and effective language learning experience in AISEs.
Method
Design and participants
This study utilized a cross-sectional survey design to examine the impact of digital informal learning and digital competence on academic motivation, academic buoyancy, and language learning outcomes among EFL learners in Iran. The cross-sectional approach facilitated data collection from a large sample at a single point, offering insights into variable relationships within AISEs (Creswell and Creswell 2017). This design efficiently captures complex inter - actions between digital practices and psychological constructs in a speci c setting.
The study included 367 EFL learners (205 males, 162 females; aged 17—25) from three language institutes in Borujerd City, Iran, selected for their use of AI-powered tools like ChatGPT as supplementary learning resources. Participants had at least six months of experi - ence with these tools to ensure relevant insights. Convenience sampling was employed for efficient recruitment, ensuring diversity in age, gender, and pro ciency levels (Etikan, Musa, and Alkassim 2016). Participants were informed about the study s purpose and procedures through their institutes, and informed consent was obtained, emphasizing voluntary partici - pation and response con dentiality. Ethical considerations were integrated throughout the research, adhering to international standards to maintain trust and transparency.
Instruments
Digital Informal Learning Scale (DILS)
The Digital Informal Learning Scale (DILS), developed by He and Li (2019), is a robust tool designed  to  measure  engagement  in  informal  learning  through  digital  technologies outside formal settings. Selected for its comprehensive coverage of digital informal learn - ing,  the  DILS  provides  valuable  insights  into  how  learners  interact  with  technology beyond traditional education. The scale comprises 12 items across three subscales: Cog - nitive Learning, Metacognitive Learning, and Social and Motivational Learning, each with four items. The Cognitive Learning subscale assesses the use of digital tools to acquire and expand knowledge, exempli ed by the item:  I frequently employ digital technologies to improve my comprehension of school course materials in informal learning settings.  The Metacognitive Learning subscale evaluates planning, monitoring, and reflection using digital resources, as in:  I often use digital technologies to seek learning strategies and tips.   The  Social and  Motivational  Learning  subscale  examines  peer  collaboration  and motivation through digital platforms, with an item like:  I often use digital technologies to  sustain  motivation  in  learning.   Responses  are  recorded  on  a  7-point  Likert  scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree).
Digital Competence Scale (DCS)
The Digital Competence Scale (DCS), developed by He and Zhu (2017), is a comprehensive psychometric tool designed to assess digital competence, encompassing the skills and knowledge  needed  to  navigate  digital  technologies  effectively  in  various  contexts. Selected for its robust framework, the DCS captures essential competencies for success in AISEs. The scale includes 29 items divided into three subscales: Technological Dimen - sion, Cognitive Skills Dimension, and Ethical Knowledge Dimension. The Technological Dimension (11 items) evaluates pro ciency in using digital tools and applications, exem - pli ed by:  I can use some software programs to handle data visualization.  The Cognitive Skills Dimension (12 items) assesses the ability to critically analyze and organize digital information, as in:  I can represent a text with a graph using digital tools.  The Ethical Knowledge Dimension (6 items) examines awareness of ethical responsibilities, such as privacy and responsible online conduct, with an item like:  When I surf online, I have awareness of Privacy (personal information, personal photos, etc.).  Responses are col - lected on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree).
Academic Motivation Scale (AMS)
The Academic Motivation Scale (AMS), developed by Vallerand et al. (1992), is a well- designed  measure  for  assessing  academic  motivation  in  diverse  educational  settings, including AISEs. Grounded in SDT, the AMS comprises 28 items across seven subscales: Intrinsic Motivation — to know (4 items), assessing knowledge pursuit for its own sake, e.g.  Because I experience pleasure and satisfaction while learning new things ; Intrinsic Motivation — toward accomplishment (4 items), evaluating the drive for personal aca - demic goals, e.g.  For the pleasure I experience while surpassing myself in my studies ; Intrinsic Motivation — to experience stimulation (4 items), measuring engagement from intellectual stimulation, e.g.  For the intense feelings I experience when I am communicat - ing my own ideas to others ; Extrinsic Motivation — Identi ed Regulation (4 items), focus - ing on valued outcomes, e.g.  Because I think that a college education will help me better prepare for the career I have chosen ; Extrinsic Motivation — Introjected Regulation (4 items), assessing internalized pressures, e.g.  To prove to myself that I am capable of com - pleting my college degree ; Extrinsic Motivation — External Regulation (4 items), evaluat- ing external rewards, e.g.  Because with only a high-school degree I would not  nd a high- paying job later on ; and Amotivation (4 items), capturing lack of motivation, e.g.  Hon - estly,  I  don t  know;  I  really  feel  that  I  am  wasting  my  time  in  school.   Responses  are recorded on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree).
Academic Buoyancy Questionnaire (ABQ)
The Academic Buoyancy Scale (ABS), developed by Jahedizadeh, Ghonsooly, and Ghani - zadeh (2019), is a reliable instrument for assessing academic buoyancy. Comprising 27 items across four subscales, it captures key aspects of academic resilience. The Sustain - ability subscale (7 items) evaluates the ability to maintain con dence and perseverance despite setbacks, exempli ed by:  When I fail, I do not lose my self-con dence.  The Regu - larity Adaptation subscale (7 items) assesses the capacity to adjust learning strategies to meet changing academic demands, as in:  I try to adapt myself to new academic situ - ations.  The Positive Personal Eligibility subscale (7 items) measures enthusiasm, motiv - ation,  and  self-efficacy  in  academic  pursuits,  e.g.  I  feel  full  of  energy  to  learn  and practice.  The Positive Acceptance of Academic Life subscale (6 items) gauges an optimis - tic attitude toward academic challenges, exempli ed by:  I have an optimistic view about learning activities.  Responses are gathered on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Dis - agree, 5 = Strongly Agree).
Language Development Test (LDT)
The Language Development Test (LDT) is a researcher-developed psychometric instru - ment designed to assess English language pro ciency across four domains: speaking, lis - tening, reading, and writing. Comprising 13 items organized into four subscales, the LDT effectively evaluates language skills in traditional and technology-enhanced settings. The Speaking subscale (4 items) measures oral communication, focusing on fluency, accuracy, and coherence, e.g.  Describe a recent personal or academic experience in detail.  The Lis - tening  subscale  (4  items)  assesses  comprehension  of  spoken  English,  exempli ed  by: 
 What  are  two  ways  AI  helps  learners  in  learning  English?   The  Reading  subscale  (4 items)  evaluates  comprehension  and  critical  analysis  of  texts,  as  in:  What  are  two examples of digital informal learning mentioned?  The Writing subscale (1 item) measures written communication through tasks like:  Write a short essay (150—200 words) on:  The 
advantages and disadvantages of using AI for learning English.   Each subscale is scored 
on a 20-point scale, totaling 80 points, based on fluency, accuracy, vocabulary, coherence, 
and comprehension. Speaking and writing are evaluated qualitatively, while listening and 
reading are scored based on correct answers (5 points each).
Before administering the instruments, the author translated them into the participants  
mother tongue to ensure comprehension. A pilot study was conducted with 26 EFL lear -
ners to measure the reliability of the instruments. Cronbach s alpha values were 0.83 for 
DILS, 0.87 for DCS, 0.69 for AMS, 0.86 for ABQ, and 0.79 for LDT. Additionally, the validity of the instruments was assessed through expert judgment, ensuring that the items were appropriate ASLEs. Finally, the instruments were given to six EFL learners to check the comprehensibility and relevance of their content.
Data collection and analysis
Data collection occurred over a four-week period. The instruments were distributed to prospective participants via email, offering a convenient and efficient method for data collection. Participants were informed about the purpose of the study and encouraged 
to provide thoughtful responses to the questions in the instruments. To facilitate engage - ment and address any concerns, the author made himself available to answer questions, ensuring that participants felt supported throughout the process. During the data collec - tion period, reminders were sent to participants to encourage timely responses and to emphasize the importance of their contributions to the research.
Con rmatory factor analysis (CFA) and structural equation modeling (SEM) were con - ducted using SMART PLS to analyze the collected data. CFA validated the measurement model, con rming that observed variables accurately represented the underlying con - structs.  SEM  evaluated  the  hypothesized  relationships  among  variables,  an  approach well-suited for examining complex interactions among variables (Khine  2013). Model  t indices and path coefficients were assessed to determine the strength and signi cance 
of these relationships. Bootstrapping was employed to enhance the accuracy of estimates, ensuring the robustness of path coefficient signi cance.
Results
Descriptive statistics revealed mean scores ranging from 54.66% (Identi ed Regulation) to 88.40% (Writing) of the maximum possible scores, indicating moderate to high perform - ance  across  the  measured  scales  (see  Appendix  A).  No  ceiling  or  floor  effects  were observed,  suggesting  that  the  scales  effectively  captured  the  range  of  participant responses. Large standard deviations for most subscales, such as Digital Informal Learning (SD = 8.624) and Academic Buoyancy (SD = 16.260), indicate considerable participant het - erogeneity,  reflecting  variations  in  engagement,  ability,  or  motivation.  For  instance, Language  Development  (M = 17.207,  SD = 3.045)  and  Writing  (M = 4.420,  SD = 1.137) exhibited high performance with relatively low variability, whereas Identi ed Regulation 
(M = 15.305, SD = 4.599) and Amotivation (M = 22.049, SD = 4.454) showed lower perform- ance with greater variability in motivational levels.

Discussion
This study found that digital informal learning signi cantly enhances academic motiv - ation,  academic  buoyancy,  and  language  acquisition  among  Iranian  EFL  learners  in AISEs.  SDT  explains  the  positive  link  between  digital  informal  learning  and  academic motivation,  as  self-directed  activities  align  with  learners   interests,  fostering  intrinsic motivation and deeper engagement (Deci and Ryan  2000; Ryan and Deci 2000, 2023; Ryan  et  al. 2021).  Studies  by  Lee,  Kim,  and  Sung  (2023),  Taş  and  Bolat  (2023),  and Zheng et al. (2018) show that platforms like online gaming and social media boost motiv - ation through interactive and engaging features. AI-supported platforms further enhance 
this  by  tailoring  content  and  feedback  to  individual  needs,  improving  the  learning 
experience and enabling personalized goal-setting (Lodge, Barba, and Broadbent  2023; Searson, Langran, and Trumble  2024). These adaptive learning paths support autonomy, competence, and relatedness, the core needs outlined in SDT (Ryan and Deci  2000). By offering choices, personalized feedback, and peer connections, digital informal learning 
in ASLEs strengthens intrinsic motivation and academic engagement, creating a robust foundation for language acquisition and sustained learner drive.
This study found that digital informal learning signi cantly influences academic buoy - ancy, de ned as the ability to navigate academic setbacks effectively. Vygotsky s Sociocul - tural Theory highlights the role of social interactions and cultural contexts in learning (Vygotsky 1978). Digital informal learning creates a dynamic social environment where learners exchange experiences, seek help, and engage in collaborative problem-solving, fostering resilience. Martin and Marsh (2006) noted that buoyant learners bene t from strong  support  networks  and  effective  coping  strategies.  In  ASLEs,  learners  connect with diverse peers and mentors through digital platforms, cultivating a sense of belong - ing and support that bolsters academic buoyancy (Rezai, Soyoof, and Reynolds  2024a). This social support equips learners with tools to manage challenges, reinforcing their resi - lience. By facilitating meaningful interactions, digital informal learning enhances learners  ability to persist through academic difficulties, making it a critical factor in sustaining aca - demic buoyancy in ASLEs.
This study also revealed that digital informal learning signi cantly enhances language learning in ASLEs, aligning with constructivist principles (Piaget 1976). Digital platforms, such as social media and online forums, provide EFL learners with opportunities for auth - entic communication, enabling active practice and re nement of language skills. These 
 ndings  are  supported  by  He  (2024)  and  Sun  and  Zhang  (2024),  who  demonstrated 
that digital platforms foster meaningful interactions, leading to improved language acqui - sition. AI technologies further amplify these bene ts by offering immediate feedback and personalized learning pathways (Lodge, Barba, and Broadbent  2023). As a result, ASLEs empower  learners  to  independently  enhance  their  language  pro ciency.  These 
 ndings, consistent with prior research, underscore the critical role of digital informal learning in promoting academic motivation, academic buoyancy, and language develop - ment. Teachers should prioritize creating supportive, engaging digital learning spaces to leverage these bene ts and foster effective language acquisition in ASLEs.
Furthermore, the study s  ndings reveal that digital competence signi cantly enhances academic motivation, academic buoyancy, and language development, underscoring its 
vital role in modern education as technology becomes more integral to learning. Research 
by Liu et al. (2024) supports this  nding, demonstrating that learners with stronger digital skills show greater engagement and motivation in digital learning environments. This con - nection proves especially relevant in ASLEs, where technology tailors learning experiences 
to individual student needs. Bandura s Social Cognitive Theory (Bandura 2012) offers further clarity, suggesting that self-efficacy, closely tied to digital competence, fosters academic motivation. Learners who feel con dent in their digital skills tend to engage more actively 
and dedicate greater effort to their studies. These  ndings highlight the importance of edu - cational programs prioritizing digital competence to empower learners to effectively utilize technology, ultimately improving their academic outcomes in ASLEs.
The TPACK framework (Mishra and Koehler 2006) further illuminates the importance of digital  competence.  Effective  technology  integration,  according  to  this  framework, requires teachers to possess a deep understanding of how to combine technology, peda - gogy, and content knowledge. This understanding allows them to utilize technology to inspire learners and create more interactive learning environments (Kohnke, Moorhouse, and Zou  2023). From a constructivist perspective, learning is fundamentally an active process  of  experiencing  and  reflecting  on  the  world.  In  ASLEs,  learners  with  robust digital competence are better equipped to leverage the adaptive capabilities of AI-sup - ported  tools,  securing  customized  learning  experiences  that  enhance  engagement, promote academic motivation, and facilitate efficient learning (Edmett et al. 2024).
The results of the study highlight a signi cant relationship between digital compe - tence and academic buoyancy in ASLEs. Research by Abu Al-majd and Belton (2024), He (2024), and Yang and Lay (2024) similarly emphasizes digital competence as key to adapting to academic challenges. ASLEs provide unique bene ts, such as adaptive feed - back and personalized learning pathways, which enhance resilience, particularly for EFL learners  facing  linguistic  and  cultural  barriers  (Rezai,  Soyoof,  and  Reynolds  2024b). According to Zimmerman and Schunk (2013), learners with strong digital competence navigate AI-powered platforms more effectively, fostering greater academic buoyancy. Additionally,  AI-powered  tools  reduce  cognitive  load  through  real-time  assistance, helping learners manage academic pressures (Biju et al. 2024; Rezai, Namaziandost, and Hwang 2024). Although some studies question AI s role in building resilience (Khasawneh, Ismail, and Hussen 2024), these  ndings suggest that AI-powered tools act as scaffolding, especially for digitally literate learners. These results extend prior research and underscore the growing importance of digital competence in enhancing adaptability within ASLEs.
Finally,  the  survey  identi ed  a  signi cant  link  between  digital  competence  and language development in ASLEs, aligning with research highlighting AI s transformative role in language acquisition (Ji, Han, and Ko 2023; Rusmiyanto et al. 2023). Digital compe- tence enables EFL learners to effectively utilize AI-powered systems, which offer person - alized  learning,  adaptive  feedback,  and  interactive  content.  These  features  support targeted  practice  tailored  to  individual  weaknesses  and  learning  styles,  as  noted  by 
D rnyei (2009). Wang, Cheung, and Chai (2024) further emphasize that real-time feedback and customized learning paths sustain motivation and enhance language acquisition out - comes. Learners with strong digital competence navigate these AI-powered tools more effectively, maximizing their bene ts (Rezai, Soyoof, and Reynolds  2024a; Yuan 2024). Additionally, Wang et al. (2024) highlight that digital competence fosters metacognitive skills, crucial for self-regulated learning in technology-mediated settings. These  ndings underscore the necessity of developing digital literacy to fully harness AI s potential in education  (Gayed  et  al.  2022),  positioning  digital  competence  as  both  a  prerequisite and a catalyst for success in AI-supported language learning.
Conclusions
This study revealed signi cant positive correlations, demonstrating that digital informal learning and digital competence strongly predict enhanced academic motivation, aca - demic buoyancy, and language development in EFL learners. Engagement with digital resources outside formal instruction, such as language apps, authentic videos, or social media,  signi cantly  shapes  learning  experiences,  while  digital  competence,  reflecting learners  ability to effectively navigate digital tools beyond mere access, is critical. The connection to academic motivation aligns with SDT (Deci and Ryan 1985), as autonomous engagement with digital content fosters intrinsic motivation and ownership of learning. Similarly, academic buoyancy enables learners to overcome challenges, utilize AI feed - back, and maintain persistence, supporting linguistic development through regular prac - tice  and  meaningful  interaction  with  authentic  input.  The  model  accounted  for substantial  variance  in  language  development,  which  underscores  the  centrality  of digital practices in modern language acquisition. Theoretically, these  ndings contribute 
to SDT and TPACK frameworks, with autonomy supporting intrinsic motivation (Deci and Ryan 2000) in ASLEs that meet SDT s needs of autonomy, competence, and relatedness. The role of digital competence aligns with the  Technological Knowledge  component of TPACK (Mishra and Koehler 2006), emphasizing that effective technology integration requires robust digital skills. The interplay between digital informal learning and compe - tence creates a reinforcing cycle, enhancing motivation and development, advocating for curricula that prioritize digital competence to maximize AI-supported and informal learn - ing bene ts.
This study provides practical insights for EFL teachers to enhance learning through digital strategies. To maximize impact, they should foster self-directed digital learning, recognizing its integral role in education. Institutions can encourage learners to create digital learning portfolios, documenting their engagement with online resources like pod - casts,  language  exchange  platforms,  or  AI-powered  tools,  promoting  reflection  and learner  agency.  Assignments  requiring  speci c  digital  tools,  rather  than  optional  use, can further support independent learning. Ongoing guidance is essential to ensure equi - table access and address challenges in navigating these resources. The  ndings highlight 
the importance of developing digital competence as AI-supported technologies become central to language learning. Educational institutions should provide targeted training on using  language  learning  applications,  collaborative  tools,  and  AI-powered  resources, extending beyond basic computer literacy. This training could involve peer-to-peer learn - ing, where learners share expertise. Fostering intrinsic motivation and academic buoyancy 
is equally critical. EFL teachers can empower learners by leveraging digital technologies for  independent  practice and  teaching  self-regulation  strategies,  such as  goal  setting and reflective practice. Teachers need a deep understanding of learner motivation, resili - ence, and effective digital tool integration to create dynamic, supportive learning environ - ments that enhance student outcomes.
This study has several limitations that require acknowledgment. The primary focus on Iranian EFL learners aged 17—25 may constrain the applicability of the  ndings to other populations.  To  enhance  the  generalizability  of  these  results,  future  research  should include  participants  from  diverse  age  groups,  cultural  backgrounds,  and  pro ciency levels. A broader sample would provide a more robust assessment of the relationships observed. Additionally, the cross-sectional design of this study limits the ability to draw causal conclusions about the connections among digital informal learning, digital compe - tence, academic motivation, academic buoyancy, and language development. While SEM can  identify  relationships,  longitudinal  studies  tracking  these  variables  over  time  are necessary  to  establish  directionality  and  understand  their  dynamic  interplay.  Further - more, although the LDT was meticulously designed, its non-standardized nature may reduce  comparability  with  established  assessments  such  as  IELTS  or  TOEFL.  Future research  should  incorporate  or  correlate  ndings  with  these  standardized  metrics  to improve generalizability. Lastly, the study s focus on ASLEs may not fully extend to tra - ditional  classroom  settings.  Comparative  studies  examining  digital  informal  learning and  digital  competence  in  both  ASLEs  and  conventional  environments  would  clarify whether the bene ts of digital competence are speci c to technologically advanced con -
texts or applicable across all learning environments.
