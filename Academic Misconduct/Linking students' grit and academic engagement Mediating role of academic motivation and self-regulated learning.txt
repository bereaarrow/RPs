Abstract
The study investigated the relationships between stu- dents'  grit,  academic  engagement,  motivation  and self-regulated  learning  (SRL).  It  explored  the  me- diating role of academic motivation and SRL in the relationship  between  students'  grit  and  academic engagement.  Understanding  these  dynamics  can help  educators  foster  environments  that  enhance student engagement through targeted interventions. A predictive correlational design was used to model the relationships among the variables. Stratified ran- dom sampling selected 190 senior high school stu- dents from the Kwahu Afram Plains District in Ghana. Data were collected using validated instruments: the University  Student  Engagement  Inventory  (USEI); Academic Grit Scale (AGS); Motivated Strategies for Learning Questionnaire (MSLQ); and Self-Regulated Learning  Scale  (SRLS).  Analysis  was  performed using partial least squares structural equation model- ling to handle non-normality in the data. The results indicated that academic grit positively influenced aca- demic motivation (β = 0.631, p < 0.001), academic en- gagement (β = 0.320, p = 0.001) and SRL (β = 0.756, p < 0.001).  Academic  motivation  and  SRL  partially mediated the relationship between grit and academic engagement. INTRODUCTION
Globally, there has been a concerted effort to improve the quality of education through increased  student  engagement,  enrolment  and  greater  students'  satisfaction  following Sustainable Development Goal 4 (SDG 4), which strives for inclusive and high-quality edu- cation for everyone (Arkorful et al., 2020; Jones et al., 2021; Žalėnienė & Pereira, 2021). While many regions have made significant strides, Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) still faces considerable challenges in achieving these educational goals (Ainscow, 2020; Arkorful et al., 2020; UNICEF, 2021). Despite various interventions in SSA, issues such as low aca- demic engagement, low academic performance, high dropout rates and low attrition rate persist (Agormedah, 2020; Arkorful et al., 2020). For instance, in Ghana, the academic

landscape has witnessed increasing concerns regarding student performance and engage- ment in educational activities, despite various educational reforms and interventions. Many students exhibit low levels of academic engagement, which can significantly hinder their academic success and long-term educational outcomes (Agormedah, 2020; Chanimbe & Dankwah, 2021; Kwegyiriba, 2021; Mohammed & Kuyini, 2021).
Academic engagement is characterised by students' active participation in learning activ- ities, level of interest, effort, motivation, enthusiasm, cognitive and emotional investment in their studies (Eroğlu & Kaya, 2023; Finn & Zimmer, 2012; Fredricks et al., 2004). According to Fredricks et al. (2004), academic engagement encompasses behavioural, emotional and cognitive dimensions of involvement in learning activities. The behavioural dimension encompasses the activities in the classroom, including active class participation and vol- unteering (Fredricks et al., 2004). Emotional engagement refers to students' interests and reactions in the classroom. Cognitive engagement refers to dedication for learning and the ability to strategically plan the learning process (Fredricks et al., 2004). Higher engagement levels are correlated with improved academic outcomes, such as higher grades and per- sistence (Agormedah, 2020; Fredricks et al., 2004) and negatively related to dropout rates (Archambault et al., 2009). On the contrary, low academic engagement among students has led to academic failure, dropping out of school and the increase of negative emotions such as anxiety and depression (Leslie et al., 2010; Li & Lerner, 2011). Students' academic engagement is a critical component of institutional effectiveness and teacher quality. In recognition of its importance, many educational institutions have implemented measures to promote student engagement in learning.
Students' engagement in learning can be influenced by several factors, including intelli- gence and socioeconomic background. However, recent research emphasises the role of non-cognitive factors such as grit, learning motivation and self-regulated learning (SRL) (Bandura, 2012; Duckworth et al., 2007; Pintrich, 2004). Academic grit is defined as perse- verance and passion for long-term academic goals. Grit has been conceptualised to con- tain two main dimensions: perseverance of effort and consistency of interest (Duckworth et al., 2007). Perseverance of effort pertains to enduring hardships, failures and adver- sities to achieve long-term goals, while consistency of interest entails sustaining interest in activities that lead to goal achievement (Duckworth & Quinn, 2009). Grit is positively associated with behavioural outcomes, including academic achievement and persistence, self-efficacy (Credé et al., 2017; Duckworth et al., 2007). Recent literature revealed that stu- dents' academic grit has a significant positive influence on learning engagement (Babiera 
& Quirap, 2024; Datu et al., 2023; Hodge et al., 2018; Jeon et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2024). Research suggests that grittier students are more likely to engage deeply in their studies, persist through challenges and achieve academic success (Credé et al., 2017). Students with high levels of grit are more likely to persist in their studies despite setbacks, making it a crucial predictor of sustained academic effort in resource-constrained environments (Eroğlu 
& Kaya, 2023; Hoferichter & Raufelder, 2023). However, the direct influence of grit on aca- demic engagement in Ghanaian senior high education remains underexplored.
The nexus between academic grit and academic engagement may be mediated by learn- ing motivation and SRL. Thus, both learning motivation and SRL may serve as the mech- anisms through which grit influences academic engagement. Academic motivation refers to the internal and external factors that drive students to learn and perform academically (Ryan & Deci, 2020). Academic motivation—whether intrinsic, driven by internal satisfaction and interest, or extrinsic, driven by external rewards like grades and recognition—also sig- nificantly influences how engaged students are in their academic work (Ryan & Deci, 2020; Shin & Bolkan, 2021). Motivation plays a crucial role in student engagement, influencing their willingness to participate in academic activities (Schunk & Zimmerman, 2012). According to Ryan and Deci (2000), intrinsic motivation fosters personal growth and engagement, while extrinsic motivation can encourage initial participation but may not sustain long-term in- volvement. Extant researchers have confirmed that academic motivation has a significant positive effect on academic engagement (Daumiller et al., 2021; Huang et al., 2023; Shao et al., 2024). Prior studies suggest that grittier students are often more motivated, which in turn enhances their engagement (Muenks et al., 2017). This study examines whether aca- demic motivation mediates the grit–engagement relationship among Ghanaian senior high students.
SRL refers to the processes through which students take an active, purposeful role in managing their own academic efforts (Panadero, 2017; Schunk & Zimmerman, 2012). SRL, involving the strategies students use to plan, monitor and evaluate their learning, further enhances academic engagement by empowering students to take control of their educa- tional journeys (Schunk & Zimmerman, 2012; Teng & Zhang, 2020). Recent studies have demonstrated a positive relationship between SRL and learning engagement (Fokkens- Bruinsma et al., 2021; Park & Kim, 2022; Setiani & Wijaya, 2020; Zhao & Cao, 2023). Sallam et al. (2022) suggest that SRL should be encouraged as it may reinforce student motivation and engagement. This study investigates whether SRL mediates the relationship between grit and academic engagement.
Study context and gaps
In recent years, the academic landscape in Ghana has witnessed increasing concerns re- garding student performance and engagement in educational activities. Despite various educational reforms and interventions, such as competency-based education, instructional technology and active learning strategies, many students exhibit low levels of academic engagement, which significantly hinders their academic success and long-term educational outcomes (Ministry of Education, 2019). The Ghanaian education system presents a unique set of challenges, including large class sizes, limited learning resources and varying levels of teacher support. These factors can significantly impact students' motivation and self- regulation, making it essential to understand how grit contributes to academic engagement in this context. In remote and hard-to-reach areas like the Afram Plains in Ghana, students face significant challenges that hinder their academic performance and engagement, such as limited resources, high student–teacher ratios, socioeconomic barriers and dropout rates. These challenges make it difficult for students to maintain consistent academic performance and engagement (Shikalepo, 2020). Despite the importance of academic engagement, there is a significant gap in the literature on the factors that influence academic engagement among students in Ghana.
Recent educational literature emphasises the importance of psychological attributes such as grit in enhancing students' engagement with academic activities (Babiera & Quirap, 2024; Credé et al., 2017; Datu et al., 2023; Duckworth et al., 2007; Duckworth & Quinn, 2009; Hodge et al., 2018; Zhao et al., 2024). However, despite the significance of grit in fostering academic engagement, there is a lack of substantial empirical re- search  addressing  how  grit  influences  engagement  among  Ghanaian  students,  par- ticularly in the context of local educational challenges and cultural beliefs. Substantial literature indicates academic motivation and SRL as critical mediators in understanding the relationship between grit and academic engagement (Daumiller et al., 2021; Fokkens- Bruinsma et al., 2021; Huang et al., 2023; Park & Kim, 2022; Setiani & Wijaya, 2020; Shao et al., 2024; Zhao & Cao, 2023). However, there remains a gap in understanding how they function in the Ghanaian educational landscape. Despite the recognised impor- tance of academic grit, motivation, engagement and SRL, these factors have received limited research attention in academic contexts in SSA (Anggraeni et al., 2024; Lam & Zhou, 2022). The influence of these factors on academic engagement among students in different cultural and academic settings needs further exploration. In such resource- constrained environments, academic engagement, grit, self-motivation and SRL become crucial for sustaining students' interest and success in school (Eroğlu & Kaya, 2023). This study examined the association between academic grit and engagement and also the mediating roles of academic motivation and SRL in this association.
Given Ghana's status as a developing country, this study highlights how psychologi- cal constructs like grit and self-regulation function in educational systems with different infrastructural and economic constraints. This contributes to a broader understanding of education in diverse global contexts. By situating this study within Ghana, the find- ings contribute to the global discourse on student persistence and engagement, offer- ing insights into how grit functions in a non-Western, resource-constrained environment. This study integrates grit theory, self-determination theory and SRL to propose a novel framework explaining how grit translates into academic engagement through motivation and SRL. By employing quantitative methods to test these relationships in the Ghanaian context, the study provides empirical evidence that can be compared with findings from other regions, helping to assess the universality or context-specificity of these constructs. Understanding how grit, mediated by academic motivation and SRL, affects academic engagement can provide valuable insights for developing interventions to improve educa- tional outcomes in these challenging contexts. In Ghana, understanding these constructs becomes increasingly important given the relevance of educational attainment to national development. The study offers actionable insights for educators, curriculum developers and policymakers by identifying key drivers of student engagement. This can lead to tar- geted interventions, such as training programmes that foster self-regulation and intrinsic motivation among students.
THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK
This study is grounded in two theoretical frameworks: self-determination theory (SDT) and social cognitive theory (SCT). In the current study, SDT is used to explain learning motiva- tion and how it influences academic engagement. SDT proposes that human behaviour (i.e., student engagement in learning) is motivated by three innate psychological needs: autonomy, competence and relatedness (Deci & Ryan, 2000, 2017). SDT also proposes that motivation can be categorised into two types: intrinsic motivation and extrinsic motivation (Deci & Ryan, 2000, 2017). Intrinsic motivation refers to engaging in an activity for the sake of enjoyment or interest. Extrinsic motivation refers to engaging in an activity for external rewards or pressures. In the context of this study, SDT suggests that students' engagement in academic activities is influenced by their learning motivation (Deci & Ryan, 2017; Ryan 
& Deci, 2000). For instance, students in the Afram Plains who are motivated intrinsically or extrinsically will be willing to engage in learning activities.
Similarly, Bandura (1986) developed SCT, which posits that learning and behaviour (i.e., engagement in learning) occurs through the interaction of personal, behavioural and envi- ronmental factors. This theory proposes that personal factors, such as self-efficacy, influ- ence students' behaviour (i.e., academic engagement). Within this framework, both grit and SRL can be understood as a personal characteristic that influences how students approach their academic challenges. SRL, an essential aspect of SCT, includes tactics such as set- ting goals and monitoring oneself (Bandura, 1986; Zimmerman, 2002). In the context of this study, SCT suggests that students' learning grit and SRL influence their academic engage- ment. In this study, SCT is used to explain how grit and SRL, as personal factors, influence academic engagement.
Hypothesis development
Research has shown that grittier individuals are more likely to maintain their interest and effort in their goals despite setbacks and difficulties. Grit is positively associated with adaptive outcomes among youth and adults, such as general self-efficacy, work satis- faction, career retention and positive affect (Credé et al., 2017). Studies in various edu- cational settings have demonstrated a positive correlation between grit and academic engagement (Babiera & Quirap, 2024; Datu et al., 2023; Eroğlu & Kaya, 2023; Hodge et al., 2018; Muenks et al., 2017; Vahedi, 2022; Zhao et al., 2024), suggesting that stu- dents who exhibit higher levels of grit are more likely to be engaged in their academic activities. For example, Babiera and Quirap (2024) established a positive significant effect of grit on learning engagement. Moreover, evidence has shown that grit has a positive association with academic motivation (Akos & Kretchmar, 2017; Hayri & Gökalp, 2023; Hodge et al., 2018; Jehanghir et al., 2024; Liu, 2022) and self-directed learning (Akos & Kretchmar, 2017; Hodge et al., 2018; Martin et al., 2022; Wolters & Hussain, 2015). Hence, it was hypothesised that:
H1.  Academic grit has a positive influence on academic motivation. H2.  Academic grit has a positive influence on academic engagement. H3.  Academic grit has a positive influence on self-regulated learning.
Academic motivation and SRL are critical factors influencing academic engagement. Academic motivation, where students engage in learning activities for inherent satisfaction, has been shown to enhance engagement, characterised by increased participation and en- thusiasm (Karimi & Sotoodeh, 2020; Wu, 2019). Numerous studies have confirmed that academic motivation is positively related with academic engagement (Daumiller et al., 2021; Huang et al., 2023). Shao et al. (2024) found a positive significant relationship between learning motivation and learning engagement. Research has further shown that intrinsically motivated students exhibit higher academic engagement, learning satisfaction, and achieve- ment (Agormedah, 2025).
SRL, involving goal setting, progress monitoring and behaviour adjustment, is essential for sustained academic engagement (Estévez et al., 2021). Effective use of SRL strategies cor- relates with better academic outcomes and higher engagement (Cleary & Zimmerman, 2012; Panadero, 2017). Researchers have found that SRL significantly predicts learning engage- ment (Fokkens-Bruinsma et al., 2021; Park & Kim, 2022; Setiani & Wijaya, 2020; Zhao & Cao, 2023). Thus, it was hypothesised that:
H4.  Academic motivation has a positive influence on academic engagement. H5.  Self-regulated learning has a positive influence on academic engagement.
Grit is linked to positive academic outcomes and sustained engagement (Steinmayr et al., 2018). However, the pathways through which grit influences engagement often involve academic motivation and SRL (Martin et al., 2022). Studies suggest that students with high grit levels develop intrinsic motivation, driven by an internal desire to learn and achieve, en- hancing their academic engagement (Anggraeni et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2023). Additionally, students with high grit are more likely to employ SRL strategies, such as goal setting and progress monitoring, which help maintain focus and motivation, leading to increased ac- ademic engagement (Theobald, 2021). Empirical evidence supports that SRL enhances the effectiveness of grit by providing tools to manage learning processes more effectively, resulting in sustained engagement and improved academic performance (Jiang et al., 2021; Theobald, 2021). Thus, academic motivation and SRL significantly mediate the relationship between grit and academic engagement, emphasising the importance of fostering these skills in students. Hence, the following hypotheses were formulated:
H6.  Academic motivation mediates the positive influence academic grit has on academic engagement.
H7.  Self-regulated learning mediates the positive influence academic grit has on academic engagement.

RESEARCH METHODS
Research design and participants
The study employed a predictive correlational design to model and understand the rela- tions that exist among students' grit and academic engagement, learning motivation and SRL. This design is deemed fit because of the use of quantitative variables in the predic- tion of relationships. A stratified random sampling technique was used to select 370 sen- ior high school (SHS) students from the Kwahu Afram Plains District of Ghana. First, the Kwahu North and South districts were put into separate strata and, within each stratum, three schools were randomly selected using the fishbowl approach. Finally, a random sam- ple of 370 SHS students was selected from the strata. Out of 370 questionnaires distributed,

Measures
Data were collected using measures adapted from the University Student Engagement Inventory (USEI; Maroco et al., 2016), Academic Grit Scale (AGS; Clark & Malecki, 2019), Motivated Strategies for Learning Questionnaire (MSLQ; Pintrich et al., 1993) and Self- Regulated Learning Scale (SRLS; Manganelli et al., 2015). Except for the demographic pro- files of the respondents, all the items on the questionnaire were rated using a five-point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (5).
Endogenous variable: Academic engagement
Students' academic engagement was measured using items modified from the USEI (Maroco et al., 2016). The scale has 15 items with three sub-dimensions, namely behavioural en- gagement (BE), emotional engagement (EE) and cognitive engagement (CE). Some of the items are as follows: ‘I follow the school's rules’ (BE); ‘I like being at school’ (EE); and ‘I try to integrate the acquired knowledge in solving new problems’ (CE). The scale has been vali- dated using different cohorts, confirming the three-facture structure with sound psychometric properties (Assunção et al., 2020; She et al., 2023; Sinval et al., 2021). In this current study, the three-factor structure of the USEI has been confirmed with a high reliability coefficient (α = 0.843).
Exogenous variable: Academic grit
Students'  academic  grit  was  assessed  using  items  adapted  from  the  AGS  (Clark  & Malecki, 2019). The AGS is a novel measure of academic-specific grit. The AGS was adapted from the general grit scale (Duckworth et al., 2007; Duckworth & Quinn, 2009). The AGS has 10 items measuring three sub-scales (determination, resilience and focus). Some of the items for each dimension are: ‘I push myself to do my personal best in school’ (de- termination); ‘I complete my schoolwork no matter how difficult it is’ (resilience); and ‘Even when I could do something more fun, I give schoolwork my best effort’ (focus). The AGS demonstrated evidence of sound reliability and validity (Clark & Malecki, 2019) and was sup- ported by a recent validation study (Lin et al., 2024). The three-factor structure of the AGS was confirmed with a high reliability coefficient (α = 0.843).
Mediating variable: Academic motivation
Students' academic motivation was measured using items adapted from the MSLQ (Pintrich et al., 1993). The MSLQ was developed to measure the types of learning strategies and aca- demic motivation used by students. The scale has eight items assessing both the intrinsic and extrinsic learning motivation of students. Some of the items for each sub-scale are: ‘I choose assignments that I can learn from even if they don't guarantee a good grade’ (intrinsic motiva- tion) and ‘I want to get better grades in this class than most of the other students’ (extrinsic motivation). The MSLQ has been used among several groups of students with adequate validity and reliability (Chow & Chapman, 2017; Duncan & McKeachie, 2005; Erturan Ilker et al., 2014; Segura-Robles et al., 2021). The reported MSLQ reliability coefficient (α = 0.846) was high.
Mediating variable: Self-regulated learning
Students' SRL was assessed using adapted items from the SRLS (Manganelli et al., 2015). The SRLS has 15 items measuring five sub-dimensions: knowledge extraction (KE); knowl- edge networking (KN); knowledge practice (KP); knowledge critique (KC); and knowledge monitoring (KM). Some of the items for each sub-dimension are: ‘I make summaries of the most important things that I do’ (KE); ‘I look for connections between the different subjects that I study’ (KN); ‘I always repeat the most important concepts to learn them better’ (KP); ‘I try to develop my idea about the things that I study’ (KC); and ‘I try to be sure to understand well what I am studying’ (KM). The scale has provided adequate construct validity and inter- nal consistency (Manganelli et al., 2015). The study also reported a high reliability coefficient (α = 0.842) for the SRL inventory.
Procedures for data collection
Data were collected during 2 weeks in August 2023. Preceding data collection, ethical clearance, with reference number DAA/P.1/Vol.1/39, was obtained from the Ethical Review Board of the University of Education, Winneba, Ghana. A copy of this letter was distributed to headmasters of selected schools to seek permission and approval for data collection. This was done to also create rapport between the researchers, class teachers and respond- ents. During data collection, the purpose of the study was explained to the students. They were informed of the right to participate or withdraw from the study without any penalty. Respondents were assured of their confidentiality and anonymity. Also, respondents were encouraged to give an honest response to the items in the questionnaire and assured that any information provided would be used solely for academic purposes. In addition, the stu- dents were asked to sign a consent letter and for students under the age of 18, the school headmasters served as their proxy parents. After their consent, the questionnaire was dis- tributed to students with the help of research assistants who were briefed and trained on the questionnaire. These research assistants were postgraduate students with considerable experience in the research process. In all, 370 questionnaires were distributed to students, however, only 190 questionnaires were valid for analysis, giving a response rate of 51.35%. The average response rate was due to internal and external examinations at the time of the data collection exercise.
Data analysis strategy
The data were first analysed using WebPower and SPSS to assess skewness and kurtosis (univariate and multivariate) and common method bias (CMB), respectively. After cleaning the data according to the guidelines of Hair et al. (2022), the analysis was performed using SmartPLS 3 software (Cheah et al., 2023; Ringle et al., 2015). This software employs par- tial least squares structural equation modelling (PLS-SEM), which is effective in examining complex relationships between different latent variables, such as mediation effects, while maximising explained variance (Becker et al., 2023).
RESULTS
Preliminary results
Univariate and Mardia's multivariate skewness and kurtosis
The study assessed univariate and multivariate normality using skewness and kurtosis tests. While the univariate skewness (−1.271 to −0.944) and kurtosis (0.927 to 2.312) were within acceptable ranges (Byrne, 2010), the multivariate skewness (4.8225) and kurtosis (33.9904) (see Appendix A) exceeded the recommended thresholds (±1; ±20) (Grilo et al., 2024; Vaithilingam et al., 2024). Consequently, since the assumptions of normality were violated, PLS-SEM was employed to analyse the data.
Common method bias
To mitigate CMB, Harman's one-factor test (Fuller et al., 2016; Kock, 2020) and variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis (Hair et al., 2022) were conducted to assess the presence of CMB. The results of Harman's one-factor test showed that the first factor explained 36.55% (<40%) of the variance (Podsakoff et al., 2003) and all VIF values were less than 5 (see Table 1) (Hair et al., 2022), suggesting no violation of the CMB problem in this study.
Assessment of measurement model
The assessment of the measurement model evaluates the reliability and validity of the con- structs used in the study. Table 1 presents various metrics for four constructs: motivation, academic engagement, grit and SRL, each with seven items. All the indicators of each scale that had a factor loading below 0.50 were deleted from the analysis. Their deletion enhances the reliability coefficient and average variance extracted (AVE) (Hair et al., 2024). The in- dicator loadings (IL) for all items are above 0.60, indicating strong individual item reliability (Hair et al., 2024). Cronbach's alpha (α) values range from 0.842 to 0.846 (see Table 1), demonstrating high internal consistency (Hair et al., 2024). The rho_A and composite reli- ability (CR) values provide further support, all exceeding the threshold of 0.70, confirming the constructs' reliability. AVE values are above 0.50 (Hair et al., 2024), indicating adequate convergent validity. The VIFs for the outer model range from 1.311 to 2.585 (see Table 1), all below the critical value of 5, suggesting no multicollinearity issues. All p-values are highly significant (p < 0.001), affirming the statistical significance of the indicator loadings. Further, the indicators showed robust loadings on their respective constructs (see Appendix B). These results collectively affirm the robustness of the measurement model in terms of reli- ability and validity. Figure 2 shows the PLS-SEM algorithm results.

DISCUSSION
This study investigated the complex relationships between student grit, academic motivation, SRL and academic engagement among SHS students in the Kwahu Afram Plains District of Ghana. Utilising theoretical frameworks from SDT and SCT, this study underscores the

significance of these factors in enhancing educational outcomes. The findings reveal that academic grit positively influences both academic motivation (H1) and engagement (H2), aligning with Duckworth et al.'s (2007) assertion that perseverance and passion for long- term goals are essential for academic success. This result is supported by recent studies, including those by Muenks et al. (2017), Vahedi (2022) and Eroğlu and Kaya (2023), which also report a positive correlation between grit and academic engagement. Additionally, the study demonstrates that grit significantly impacts SRL, validating H3. This finding is con- sistent with Zimmerman's (2002) SCT framework, which highlights the pivotal role of self- regulation in academic achievement.
Further evidence from Wolters and Hussain (2015) and Martin et al. (2022) underscores the importance of self-regulation in academic success. Moreover, the study found that aca- demic motivation directly and positively influences the academic engagement of Ghanaian SHS students, thereby confirming H4. This finding aligns with SDT, which highlights intrinsic and extrinsic motivations as key drivers of students' active participation in their learning processes (Ryan & Deci, 2000). It suggests that academic motivation significantly impacts academic engagement, underscoring the importance of both intrinsic and extrinsic motiva- tions in fostering active participation in academic tasks. Previous research supports these findings, indicating that motivated students tend to exhibit higher levels of engagement and achievement (Froiland & Worrell, 2016; Karimi & Sotoodeh, 2020; Wu, 2019).
Additionally, SRL positively influences academic engagement (H5), in line with SCT (Bandura, 1991). This theory emphasises that self-efficacy and proactive learning strate- gies are crucial for enhancing students' academic engagement. This finding is supported by previous research, which consistently shows that SRL positively impacts academic en- gagement by highlighting the importance of self-efficacy and proactive strategies. For in- stance, Cleary and Zimmerman (2012) and Panadero (2017) demonstrated that effective self-regulation, including goal setting and progress monitoring, is essential for sustained engagement and improved academic outcomes. While grit is shown to positively affect ac- ademic engagement, our study delved into a more nuanced interaction by emphasising the mediating roles of academic motivation and SRL. This insight is particularly pertinent for marginalised regions in developing countries, such as Ghana, where students face consid- erable educational challenges, including limited resources and suboptimal teaching quality.
Academic motivation and SRL are pivotal for enhancing students' resilience and growth mindset, which in turn fosters higher levels of academic engagement and improves educa- tional outcomes. The study reveals that academic motivation partially mediates the relation- ship between grit and academic engagement (H6), indicating that motivational processes enhance the direct impact of grit on engagement. Similarly, SRL also partially mediates this relationship (H7), underscoring the vital role of self-regulation. These findings align with previous research, which demonstrates that students with high levels of grit tend to develop intrinsic motivation and employ SRL strategies, such as goal setting and progress monitor- ing, to sustain engagement and improve performance (Jiang et al., 2021; Martin et al., 2022; Theobald, 2021). Consequently, higher levels of motivation and self-regulation amplify the effects of grit on academic engagement.
Strengths and limitations
A key strength of the present study is its use of a predictive correlational design with the PLS-SEM approach. This method effectively models the relationships between grit, motiva- tion, SRL and engagement among SHS students. Additionally, the stratified random sam- pling technique used ensures that the sample is representative of both rural and urban settings, thereby enhancing the generalisability of the findings. However, the study also has limitations. The reliance on self-reported data may introduce biases, such as response bias, potentially affecting the accuracy of the results. Furthermore, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to make causal inferences. This study conceptualised SRL within Bandura's SCT instead of Zimmerman's SCT, and this may not fully capture the complexities of SRL. Also, Bandura's SCT may not explicitly address metacognition processes such as planning, monitoring and evaluating, which are crucial for SRL. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted with caution. Future research should address these limitations by employing lon- gitudinal designs to gain a deeper understanding of causal relationships and to mitigate the biases associated with self-reported data.
Implications
This study enhances a clearer understanding of the factors influencing students' academic engagement in developing countries. By integrating the SDT and SCT, it clarifies the medi- ating roles of academic motivation and SRL in the relationship between grit and academic engagement. This integration provides both theoretical and practical insights, as discussed below.
Theory implications
This study makes a significant theoretical contribution by advancing a clearer understanding of the intricate relationships between student grit, academic motivation, SRL and academic engagement among SHS students, particularly in marginalised districts such as the Afram Plains in Ghana. By applying SDT and SCT, this study integrates two prominent theoretical frameworks  to  explain  the  relationship  between  grit,  motivation,  SRL  and  academic engagement. By applying SDT and SCT, this study provides a nuanced understanding of the psychological mechanisms underlying the relationship between grit and academic engagement. Grounded in SDT and SCT, the research affirms that grit enhances both academic motivation and engagement, supporting the view that perseverance and passion for long-term goals are crucial for academic success. The findings also align with SCT by demonstrating that grit positively influences SRL—a critical factor in academic achievement. Moreover, the study reveals the mediating roles of academic motivation and SRL, showing that grit indirectly fosters academic engagement through these mediators. These theoretical insights are especially valuable for developing effective educational strategies in resource- limited settings. The outcomes emphasise the importance of nurturing both motivation and self-regulation while focusing on grit as a key driver to enhance student engagement and improve academic outcomes.
Practical implications
This study provides actionable insights for educators and policymakers aiming to enhance academic engagement in marginalised districts like the Afram Plains in Ghana. The findings have implications for educational practice, particularly for students in remote or underserved areas like the Afram Plains, who face educational challenges such as limited resources and suboptimal teaching quality. First, recognising the positive link between academic grit and motivation, educational programmes could emphasise developing students' perseverance and long-term goals. This goal can be achieved through specialised grit training workshops. These workshops could target goal-setting exercises, perseverance-building activities, self- reflection and self-awareness, mindset training, role modelling and mentorship. Second, given the crucial role of grit in SRL, it is important to integrate self-regulation strategies into the curriculum. These strategies include goal setting, progress monitoring and self- reflection techniques.
In the Ghanaian context, grit and self-regulation may manifest differently due to cultural and socioeconomic factors. For instance, Ghanaian students may demonstrate grit by persevering through challenges such as limited access to resources, inadequate infrastructure and soci- etal expectations. In this context, grit may involve adapting to unfamiliar situations, seeking out opportunities for learning and maintaining motivation despite obstacles. To develop grit and self-regulation among students in Ghana, educators and policymakers could consider the following strategies: (a) incorporate local values, customs and experiences into teaching practices to make learning more relevant and engaging; (b) teach students to adapt to limited resources and find creative solutions to problems; (c) pair students with role models who can provide guidance, support and motivation, and encourage students to apply what they have learned in real-world settings, such as through service- learning projects or internships.
Third, since academic motivation directly impacts engagement, teachers are encouraged to employ strategies that enhance intrinsic and extrinsic motivation. This pathway can be achieved by providing meaningful learning experiences, recognising achievements and of- fering students greater autonomy in their learning activities. In addition, the study's finding that SRL enhances engagement underscores the need for teaching approaches that pro- mote self-efficacy and proactive learning. Educators are encouraged to focus on develop- ing students' self-regulation skills through structured cognitive-behavioural activities that emphasise planning, organisation and self-evaluation. Addressing these factors can signifi- cantly improve academic engagement and educational outcomes for students in remote and underserved regions.
Conclusions
This research, focusing on SHS students in the Kwahu Afram Plains District of Ghana, re- vealed how grit influences academic engagement indirectly through academic motivation and SRL. Current findings highlight the importance of grit and also suggest a more complex relationship than the traditionally assumed direct link between grit and engagement. The findings emphasise the mediating roles of academic motivation and SRL. This outcome underscores the essential roles of both intrinsic and extrinsic motivation, self-efficacy and proactive learning strategies in enhancing academic engagement. This insight is particularly relevant for students in remote or underserved areas who face educational challenges such as limited resources and suboptimal teaching quality. Educators and policymakers in Ghana can leverage these findings to improve academic engagement by implementing strategies that foster motivation and SRL, thereby promoting a growth mindset and grit among SHS students. Overall, this research not only contributes to the theoretical understanding of these dynamics but also offers practical recommendations for improving academic performance and achieving quality educational outcomes, in line with SDG 4.
