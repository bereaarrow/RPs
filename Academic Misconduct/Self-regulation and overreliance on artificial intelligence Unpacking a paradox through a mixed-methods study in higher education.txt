A B S T R A C T
The  rapid  emergence  of  generative  artificial  intelligence  (AI)  in  higher  education  has  raised  concerns  about students’ overreliance  on  these  tools  for  academic  and  personal  tasks.  Although  generative  AI can  enhance productivity  and  creativity,  excessive  use  may  undermine  key  learning  competences.  In  this  context,  self- regulation  may  function  as  a  crucial  protective  factor.  This  study  examined  the  relationship  between  self- regulation  and  overreliance  on  generative  AI among  university  students.  The  sample  consisted  of  404  un- dergraduates (MAge  = 20.78, SD = 4.12) enrolled in education-related degrees at the University of the Basque 
Country. Validated self-report scales measured dimensions of self-regulation and AI overreliance, and mediation analyses  were  combined  with  qualitative  content  analysis  of open-ended  responses.  Results  showed  that  goal setting significantly predicted perseverance, decision-making, and learning from mistakes. A paradoxical pattern 
emerged: goal setting was positively associated with greater AI overreliance, yet its indirect associations through perseverance  and  learning  from  mistakes  were  negative,  suggesting  an  inverse  relationship,  indicating  that stronger regulatory strategies are linked to lower levels of overdependence. These findings suggest that students 
who set clear goals tend to report higher initial use of AI tools, while perseverance and reflective learning are associated  with  patterns  that  counterbalance excessive  reliance.  Qualitative  analyses  supported  this  gradient, revealing that most students used AI minimally or for basic information searches, whereas a smaller subgroup 
relied on it for academic tasks or decision-making. This distribution indicates that overreliance is concentrated among  a  limited  portion  of  students,  consistent  with  the  protective  role  played  by  strong  self-regulatory 
competencies.

1. Introduction
1.1. Self-regulation in higher education
The  conceptualization of Self-regulation is established  as  a  funda -
mental  construct  in  behavioral  sciences  and  education,  being  particu - larly  relevant  in  the  context  of higher  education. It  is defined  as  the ability  to  control  individual  thoughts,  emotions,  and  behaviors  to 
achieve specific goals (Zimmerman, 2000). Self-regulation enables stu-
dents  to  address  the  demands  of academic  life,  manage  diverse  prior - ities,  and  persevere  in  the  face  of  adversity.  The  benefits  of  robust self-regulation in higher education extend beyond mere academic suc-
cess.  Students  who  exhibit  a  high  capacity  for  self-regulation tend  to show stronger intrinsic motivation, a greater willingness to persevere in 
the face of setbacks, and experience an increased sense of control over their  learning  processes  (Rezayi  et  al.,  2024;  Zimmerman,  2000).

Artificial  Intelligence  (AI) is generally  understood as the  ability  of machines or computer systems to imitate and perform tasks commonly linked to human intelligence, such as reasoning, learning, or problem- solving (Morandín-Ahuerma, 2022). Over recent decades, this technol -

with  generative  AI for  academic  purposes,  while  approximately  one third  report  never  having  used  such  tools  nor  intending  to  do  so 
(Johnston et al., 2024).
Nowadays,  higher  education  students  employ  generative  AI for  a wide  array of academic purposes,  particularly for writing support and workload management. Empirical evidence shows that students use AI 
to enhance efficiency, generate templates, refine sentence quality, and improve  clarity  of expression  (Wang  et  al.,  2024; Wang  & Ren,  2024; 
Crˇ ˇcek  &  Patekar,  2023).  Other  common  uses  include  overcoming 
writer's block, boosting productivity, and increasing confidence in aca- demic output (Chatto & Logronio, 2025; Zhao et al., 2024). Students also value  AI for  idea  generation,  content  structuring,  and  acquiring  new linguistic  expressions,  as  well  as  for  addressing  specific  writing  diffi- culties (Chen et al., 2024; Oh et al., 2023; Wang, 2024). Beyond writing, generative AI is often used to mitigate time constraints, reduce academic workload,  and  diminish  language  barriers  while  enhancing  overall writing performance (Sterling et al., 2025). On this use, students report 
both clear benefits and notable drawbacks in the use of generative AI. Advantages include heightened productivity, efficiency, linguistic sup-
port,  and  improvements  in  clarity,  confidence,  and  satisfaction  with 
academic tasks (Chatto & Logronio, 2025; Chen et al., 2024; Oh et al., 2023; Sterling  et  al.,  2025; Wang,  2024; Wang  et  al.,  2024; Crˇ ˇcek  & 
Patekar,  2023). However,  concerns  are  also  frequently  raised,  such  as the  risk  of generating  superficial  or  generic  content,  factual  inaccura - cies,  diminished  originality,  potential  plagiarism  and  excessive  over-
reliance on AI (Chatto & Logronio, 2025; Wang et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024; Crˇ ˇcek & Patekar, 2023). This study is focused on this last potential 
risk.
Overreliance on AI refers to an excessive or habitual dependence on artificial  intelligence tools  to  perform  tasks,  make  decisions,  or  solve problems  in  ways  that  diminish  one's  own  independent  skills,  judge - ment,  or  learning  processes  (Morales-García,  Guti´errez-Santiuste,  & Lafuente-Lechuga, 2024).  Baird  and  Maruping  (2021) emphasize  that the more natural and human-like the responses of AI systems or chatbots become,  the greater the risk of users developing problematic reliance. 
This  issue  can  be  analyzed  through  the  Unified  Theory  of Acceptance and  Use of Technology  (UTAUT) (Venkatesh  et  al.,  2003),  which  ex- plains AI reliance by identifying key factors behind its adoption and use. 
The  theory  highlights  the  roles  of performance expectancy,  effort  ex- pectancy, social influence, and facilitating conditions. Overreliance may arise  when  users  perceive  that  AI enhances  task  efficiency  (high  per- formance  expectancy),  simplifies  complex  activities  (low  effort  expec- tancy),  is  promoted  by  peers  or  colleagues  (social  influence),  and  is supported  by  sufficient  infrastructure  or  resources  (facilitating conditions).
Recent  studies  estimate  that  the  prevalence  of  overreliance  on  AI tools, such as ChatGPT, among university students ranges between 16% and 25% (Hong & Chen, 2024; Huang, Chen, et al., 2024; Stojanov, Liu, 
& Koh,  2024).  Although  these  figures  may  suggest  that  widespread overreliance is not yet pervasive, a recent systematic review synthesiz -
ing  72  studies  highlights  concerns  regarding  disengagement  and cognitive  risks  associated  with  AI  use,  including  reduced  critical thinking  and  overreliance (Lo et  al.,  2024). Consequently,  despite  the current  prevalence  being  moderate,  the  rapid  growth  and  expanding functionality of AI in educational settings warrant careful attention and 
may  justify  institutional guidance  or  regulation  (Liu et  al.,  2023; Sto- janov, Pejovi´c, & Drakuli´c, 2024).
In  the  present  study,  overreliance  is  operationalized  through  a behavioral  frequency  indicator:  students’  self-reported  frequency  of generative AI use for core academic writing tasks. Thus, overreliance is 
not conceptualized as a latent psychological trait but as a behaviorally anchored  tendency  inferred  from  high-frequency  delegation  in academically central activities, reducing conceptual ambiguity between 
the theoretical construct and its empirical operationalization.
1.3. Effects of generative artificial intelligence on self-regulation and decision-making
In the context of self-regulation, generative artificial intelligence has shown a dual role. On the one hand, recent research highlights that these tools  can  facilitate  self-regulated  learning  processes  by  offering personalized feedback, metacognitive support, and adaptive experiences that allow students to set clear goals, monitor their progress, and reflect on  their  results  (Sardi  et  al.,  2025).  For  example,  by  interacting with AI-G platforms,  students  are  able  to  deal  with  complex  tasks  more effectively, taking advantage of these tools' ability to simplify informa -
tion  and  provide  practical  guidance  (Zhou,  Wang,  &  Zhang,  2024). However, this same potential also carries significant risks. Overreliance on  generative  AI  can  lead  to  ‘cognitive  offloading,’  in  which  users delegate  complex  tasks  to  technology,  reducing  their  active  participa - tion in learning processes and limiting the development of fundamental skills such as critical reflection (Gerlich, 2025).
In the field of decision-making, AI has been equally transformative. 
These  tools  enable  users  to  process  large  volumes  of information effi- ciently, which can optimize problem-solving and analytical capabilities 
in challenging contexts. For example, studies have shown that AI tools can  act  as  virtual  assistants  to  analyze  data,  propose  innovative  solu- tions,  and  improve  decision-making accuracy  (Zhou,  Wang,  & Zhang, 2024). However, recent  research has revealed that  users tend to over- estimate their performance when using these technologies, even when 
their objective performance does not improve significantly (Fernandes et al., 2025). This phenomenon, linked to overconfidence, affects both users with little experience in artificial intelligence and those with high AI literacy, who, paradoxically, show disproportionate confidence in the responses generated by these tools.
To enhance the benefits of AI and mitigate its risks, it is essential to foster  educational  and  organizational  strategies  that  promote  critical and  reflective  use  of  these  technologies.  First,  artificial  intelligence educational programmes should be developed to train users to interact 
with  these  tools  effectively,  promoting  both  critical  thinking  and  self- regulation (Sardi et al., 2025). In addition, it is necessary to design AI platforms that encourage interaction and reflection, incorporating fea-
tures such as additional questions or suggestions for verifying informa -
tion, which can help users avoid blind reliance on these tools (Fernandes et  al.,  2025).  Finally,  educational  institutions  and  professional  envi- ronments should adopt a hybrid approach that combines the capabilities 
of AI with  human  creativity  and  judgement,  ensuring  that  technology complements, but does not replace, fundamental skills (Fernandes et al., 2025; Sardi et al., 2025).
1.4. Purpose of the study
Generative artificial intelligence is rapidly reshaping higher educa -
tion, raising critical concerns regarding students’ overreliance on these tools  for  academic  and  personal  tasks.  Although  generative  AI may enhance  productivity  and  creativity,  excessive  reliance  risks  under - mining  core  competences  such  as  autonomous  decision-making  and reflective learning. Within this landscape, self-regulation emerges as a potentially protective  mechanism. Recent  scholarship  highlights  chal- lenges  including  cognitive  offloading,  reduced  critical  reflection,  and limited understanding of how students balance instrumental AI use with 
the development of independent skills (Fernandes et al., 2025; Gerlich, 2025; Sardi et al., 2025). However, empirical research examining how specific dimensions of self-regulation relate to AI overreliance remains scarce, with most studies focusing on prevalence, acceptance factors, or general attitudes (Hong & Chen, 2024; Huang, Liu, et al., 2024).
The  literature also  presents  conflicting  perspectives. While  several theoretical  accounts  posit  that  self-regulation  mitigates  technological dependency, emerging evidence suggests that highly goal-oriented stu-
dents  may  strategically  intensify  AI  use  as  an  efficiency-enhancing resource (Zhou, Wang, & Zhang, 2024). This tension reflects a broader 
paradox in technology-mediated learning: goal setting can function as a double-edged sword. On the one hand, clearly defined goals may drive increased AI engagement to optimize task completion; on the other, they activate  regulatory  processes  that  foster  persistence  and  reflective evaluation (De la Fuente, Kauffman, et al., 2020; Zhou, Wang, & Zhang, 2024).
Grounded  in Social  Cognitive  Theory  and  contemporary models  of Self-Regulated  Learning  (Panadero,  2017;  Zimmerman,  2000),  goal setting is conceptualized as the forethought-phase mechanism that ini-
tiates  subsequent  performance and  self-reflective  processes.  Empirical evidence indicates that well-defined goals are positively associated with sustained effort and perseverance (De la Fuente, Kauffman, et al., 2020; Moeller et al., 2020), as well as with more deliberate and autonomous decision-making  in  digitally  mediated  contexts  (Broadbent  &  Poon, 2015; Huang,  Liu,  et  al.,  2024).  Goal  clarity  has  also  been  linked  to enhanced metacognitive monitoring and constructive engagement with 
errors,  facilitating  learning  from  mistakes  rather  than  avoidance  be- haviors  (Panadero  et  al.,  2022).  Accordingly,  the  present  model  con- ceptualizes  goal  setting  as  an  antecedent  of  perseverance  (a1), decision-making (a2), and learning from mistakes (a3).
In  line  with  process-oriented accounts  of self-regulation, persever - ance during task execution increases sustained engagement with feed- back, thereby enhancing opportunities for error detection and adaptive revision  (d31) (Panadero  et  al.,  2022; Sitzmann  & Ely, 2011).  Specif- ically, the path from perseverance (performance phase) to learning from mistakes  (self-reflection  phase),  labeled  d31,  is  grounded  in  cyclical models  of  self-regulated  learning  (Zimmerman,  2000).  From  this perspective, sustained effort during performance generates continuous self-monitoring information that  feeds  forward  into  the  self-reflection phase.  Students  who  persist  despite  difficulty  are  more  likely  to confront  discrepancies  between  intended  and  actual  performance, increasing  the  likelihood  of adaptive  self-evaluation  and  constructive responses  to  errors.  Thus,  perseverance  provides  the  cognitive  and motivational conditions that facilitate learning from mistakes.
Similarly,  adaptive  decision-making  supports  evaluative  judgment and  reflective  calibration, both  of which  are  essential  for  error-based learning  in  technology-rich  environments  (Huang,  Liu,  et  al.,  2024; Winne  & Hadwin,  1998).  Decision-making  during  task  execution  in- volves selecting, adjusting, and regulating strategies based on ongoing monitoring.  These  control  processes  strengthen metacognitive  aware - ness of performance standards and outcomes, thereby directly informing reflective  appraisal  and  attribution  processes  in  the  subsequent self-reflection  phase  (Zimmerman,  2000). Consequently,  students  who engage  in  deliberate  and  adaptive  decision-making are  more  likely  to interpret  mistakes  as  informative  feedback,  promoting  learning  from errors rather than avoidance.
Therefore,  perseverance  (d31)  and  decision-making  (d32)  are  hy- pothesized to positively predict learning from mistakes within the pro- posed mediation sequence.
Regarding  overreliance  on  generative  AI, emerging  evidence  sug- gests  that  diminished  regulatory  control  is  associated  with  greater cognitive  offloading  and  uncritical  acceptance  of  automated  outputs (Gerlich,  2025; Sardi  et  al.,  2025).  In  contrast,  students  who  demon - strate  sustained  effort  and  autonomous decision-making appear  more likely  to  critically  evaluate  AI-generated  responses  and  maintain epistemic agency (Fernandes et al., 2025). Furthermore, learning from mistakes  may  reinforce  metacognitive monitoring and  epistemic  vigi- lance  (Panadero  et  al.,  2022),  potentially  functioning  as  a  protective factor  against  technological  dependency  Accordingly,  perseverance (b1), decision-making (b2), and learning from mistakes (b3) are expected 
to negatively predict overreliance on generative AI.
At the same time, consistent with the paradoxical role of goal setting in technology-mediated learning (Zhou, Wang, & Zhang, 2024), a direct association between goal setting and overreliance (c’) is also examined. 
On this matter, highly goal-oriented students may strategically intensify AI  use  to  maximize  efficiency,  drawing  on  motivational  cues  that 
emphasize performance, which has been shown to increase reliance on algorithmic assistance in performance-framed contexts (Nicolas, 2025). Moreover,  evidence  from  online  and  technology-supported  learning environments indicates  that  goal  clarity  can  significantly  enhance  the adoption  of  digital  resources,  suggesting  that  strong  goal  focus  may encourage  more  intensive  engagement  with  technological  tools (Heintalu et al., 2025).
Although  emerging  evidence  has  begun  to  acknowledge  this  dual dynamic,  its  simultaneous  modeling  as  both  a  direct  and  indirect pathway  within  the  same  regulatory  framework  remains  relatively scarce.  Addressing  these  theoretical  tensions  and  empirical  gaps,  the present  study  analyzes  how  distinct  components  of  self-regulation jointly and indirectly relate to overreliance on generative AI.

Additionally, in view of the rapid incorporation of generative AI in higher education, it is crucial to explore students' perspectives on how these  tools  influence  their  decision-making  processes  and  regulatory strategies.  For  example,  Johnston  et  al.  (2024) found  that  students exhibit ambivalence about AI's legitimacy, while qualitative work sug- gests  users  actively  negotiate  when  to  delegate  tasks  versus  maintain autonomous control  (Wang  et  al.,  2024).  Similarly,  Zhou,  Wang,  and Zhang  (2024) note  that  self-regulated  learners  may  leverage  AI for metacognitive  support  yet  remain  vulnerable  to  overconfidence  in AI-generated  outputs.  Despite  these  insights,  systematic  evidence  on how students perceive AI's role in their academic, professional, or per- sonal decisions, and how this relates to their regulatory approaches, is still emerging. Based on this research gap, the second objective of the present study is. 
O2  To explore students' perspectives on the role of generative AI in 
their decision-making and self-regulated learning.
Overall, this study is explicitly positioned as an early empirical step 
in theorizing  the  emerging  dynamics  between  SRL and  artificial  intel - ligence. By adopting a mixed-methods approach, it provides a founda - tional  yet  exploratory inquiry  into  how  students  navigate  the  tension between autonomous regulation and technological assistance.
2. Method
2.1. Sample
The  study  sample  comprised  404  undergraduate students  (MAge  = 20.78, SD = 4.12) enrolled in education-related degree programs at the University  of  the  Basque  Country  (Spain).  Among  them,  158  self- identified  as  male,  243  as  female,  and  3  as  another  gender.  Sixteen students were pursuing the double degree in Early Childhood and Pri- mary Education, 35 were enrolled in Early Childhood Education, 287 in Primary  Education,  and  66  in Pedagogy.  With  respect  to  their  year  of study, 43 were in the first year, 209 in the second, 85 in the third, and 67 
in the fourth. The average grade point average (GPA) for the preceding academic year, on the Spanish 10-point scale, was 7.63 (SD = .71).
Participants were recruited through a non-probabilistic convenience sampling procedure, as all had previously been taught by one or more members  of the  research  team.  It  is worth  noting  that  earlier  studies suggest  that  non-probabilistic sampling  methods  may  produce  results comparable  to  those  obtained  using  random  sampling  techniques (Coppock  et  al., 2018). Nonetheless,  it is important to  emphasize  that the sample is restricted to education students from a single university, which  may  limit  the  generalizability  of  the  findings  across  different

2.2. Instruments
Personal  Information:  An  ad  hoc  questionnaire  was  developed  to gather students’ personal and academic details, including age, gender, year of study, degree program, and academic performance.
Self-regulation: Self-regulation  was  assessed  using  the  Spanish Short Self-Regulation Questionnaire (Garzon ´ et al., 2017), which consists of 17 items  distributed  across  four  dimensions.  The  first  dimension,  Goal setting (α = .81),  evaluates  students'  ability  to  establish  and  monitor objectives (e.g., “I usually keep track of my progress toward my goals”). 
The second, Perseverance (α = .71), measures persistence and resistance to distractions when pursuing tasks (e.g., “I get easily distracted from my 
plans”). The third, Decision making (α = .76), reflects students’ capacity 
to  make  choices  and  manage  competing  alternatives  (e.g.,  “I put  off making decisions”). Finally, learning from mistakes (α = .79) captures 
the extent to which individuals reflect on and adapt based on prior errors (e.g.,  “I learn  from  my  mistakes”).  All items  were  rated  on  a  5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree).
Frequency  of  Generative  AI  Use: To  evaluate  the  overreliance  on generative AI use, students were asked: “How often do you use gener -
ative  artificial  intelligences  (such  as  ChatGPT  or  similar)  for  writing essays,  reports,  or  other  academic  documents?”  Responses  were collected  using  a 5-point  Likert  scale  ranging  from  1 (never)  to  5 (al- ways). This item was used as a proxy for overreliance because frequent reliance on generative AI for core academic tasks—such as writing es-
says,  reports,  or  other  graded  documents—reflects  a  shift  from  occa- sional support to a habitual substitution of students' own cognitive and writing efforts. Therefore, higher scores on this item are interpreted as greater  overreliance on  AI for  academically essential  work.  Although multi-item  scales  are  typically  preferred  for  complex  psychological constructs,  single-item  measures  can  demonstrate adequate  reliability and validity when assessing concrete, unidimensional, and behaviorally specific variables (Wanous et al., 1997; Bergkvist & Rossiter, 2007). In 
the  present  case,  the  item  targets  a  clearly  defined  and  observable behavior—frequency  of  AI use  for  academic  writing—which  reduces construct ambiguity and supports its use as a focused behavioral  indi-
cator. Importantly, this item directly measures frequency of use rather than overreliance as a multidimensional psychological construct. In the present  study,  overreliance is inferred  indirectly  from  high-frequency use in core academic writing tasks. We conceptualize overreliance not merely as frequent use, but as a functional shift from augmentation (i.e., AI as a supportive scaffold) to substitution (i.e., AI replacing students’ own  planning,  organization, and  text  production processes).  When  AI tools  are  used  systematically  for  the  production  of  graded  academic texts,  such  repeated  delegation  may  reflect  a  behavioral  pattern consistent  with  substitution  rather  than  complementary  assistance. However, authors acknowledge that frequency alone does not constitute 
direct  evidence  of  diminished  learning,  reduced  effort,  or  impaired self-regulation. Thus, in this study, the term “overreliance” refers to an inferred  behavioral  tendency  toward  substitution  in  high-frequency contexts,  rather  than  a  clinically  validated  or  diagnostically  assessed reliance construct.
Relevance  of  Generative AI  in decision-making:  An open-ended  item was  included  to  explore  the  perceived  relevance  of  generative  AI in students’  decision-making  processes.  Specifically,  participants  were asked:  “What  role  do  generative  AIs (such  as  ChatGPT)  play  in  your academic, professional, or personal decisions?“.
All items corresponding to the scales used can be found in Supple - mentary Material 2 of this study. Likewise, all measures in this study rely 
on self-report data, which may be subject to common-method bias and social desirability effects, particularly regarding the sensitivity of AI use in  education.  To  mitigate  these  risks,  participants  were  guaranteed complete anonymity and confidentiality to encourage honest reporting.
2.3. Procedure
Following the selection of data collection instruments, the research team  digitized  them  using  Google Forms. Data  were  collected  in  early September 2025, both during class sessions and remotely, with students completing  the  survey  on  their  personal  computers.  On  average, participation required approximately 15 min.
With  respect  to  ethical  considerations, the  study  was  exempt  from formal review by the ethics committee in accordance with the University of  the  Basque  Country's  regulations,  which  stipulate  that  anonymous surveys  involving  adult  participants  and  excluding  sensitive  personal information  do  not  require  mandatory  evaluation.  Nonetheless,  all procedures adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Prior to  participation, students  were  informed  of the  study's  objectives  and provided their informed consent. They were reminded that participation was  voluntary  and  that  they  could  decline  or  withdraw  at  any  stage without  penalty.  Anonymity  and  confidentiality were  safeguarded:  no personally  identifiable  data  were  collected,  and  all  responses  were securely stored in a private repository accessible solely to the research team.

15  should  be  reviewed  (Byrne,  2010; Kline,  2016).  To establish  con- current validity, average variance extracted (AVE) and composite reli- ability (CR) was computed, targeting AVE ≥.50 and CR ≥ .70 (Fornell &

After refinement, descriptive statistics (means  and  standard  deviations),  Pearson  correlations,  and  internal consistency  (Cronbach's  alpha)  were  reported.  Subsequently,  a media - tion  analysis  was  conducted  in  PROCESS for  SPSS Statistics  27  using Model  80  and  10,000  bootstrap  samples. For the qualitative component, open-ended responses were analyzed inductively. 3. Results
Preliminary  adjustments  were  carried  out  to  the  self-regulation model in order to examine its construct validity. The initial model pre-
sented poor fit indices (χ2/df = 7.13; CFI = .691; RMSEA = .123; AIC = 807.077).  Several  items  with  low  factor  loadings  were  subsequently 
removed, namely item 2 (λ = .287), item 3 (λ = .292), item 7 (λ = .450), 
and  item  16  (λ = .404).  These  items  also  exhibited  high  modification indices  (MI  > 15)  with  other  items  showing  stronger  loadings,  a threshold  which,  according  to  previous  methodological recommenda - tions,  warrants  closer  evaluation  and  potential  item  removal  (e.g., Byrne, 2010; Kline, 2016). After refinement, the final model, formed by 
13  (items  1,  4-6,  8-15,  17)  of  the  original  17  items  assessing self-regulation, showed substantially improved fit indices (χ2/df = 3.20; 
CFI = .919; RMSEA = .074; AIC = 237.817), with all indices entering 
into the recommended thresholds of χ2/df < 5, CFI >.90, TLI >.90, and RMSEA <.080 (Hooper et al., 2008). Factor loadings ranged from λ =
.497 (lowest, item 6) to λ = .833 (highest, item 4), indicating acceptable to  strong  relationships  between  the  observed  variables  and  their respective latent factors. All standardized factor loadings from the final 
CFA measurement model are reported in Supplementary Material 3.
To further establish the validity and reliability of the measurement model, the average variance extracted (AVE) and composite reliability (CR) were calculated for each dimension of the scale. The results showed satisfactory reliability across the four factors: Goal setting (4 items; AVE 
= .488;  CR = .786),  Perseverance (2  items;  AVE = .526;  CR = .686), Decision making  (4  items;  AVE = .455;  CR = .769),  and  Learning from mistakes  (2  items;  AVE = .501;  CR = .660).  According  to  Hair  et  al. (2019),  CR  values  above  .70  are  generally  considered  acceptable, although  values  close  to  .60  may  be  deemed  adequate  in  exploratory research.  In terms  of convergent  validity,  AVE values  approaching or exceeding  the  .50  threshold  (Fornell  & Larcker,  1981) indicate  that  a substantial portion of the variance is explained by the latent construct. Taken  together,  these  indices  provide  additional  support  for  the construct validity of the instrument.
Descriptive analyses showed that mean scores ranged from 1.99 for overreliance on artificial intelligence to 3.79 for learning from mistakes, with  standard  deviations  between  .643  and  .834,  indicating  adequate variability  across  the  dimensions.  However,  given  that  all  constructs were  assessed  through  self-report  measures,  these  descriptive  results should  be  interpreted  with  caution.  In  particular,  the  relatively  low mean level of overreliance on artificial intelligence may reflect potential underreporting  due  to  social  desirability  and  common-method  bias, especially  within  an  educational  context  characterized  by  prior instructor–student relationships and the sensitivity surrounding norms of AI use.
In  terms  of  correlations,  the  correlation  analysis  revealed  that overreliance on AI was negatively associated with several self-regulation dimensions. Specifically,  significant  negative relationships were  found 
with decision making (r =   .131, p = .009) and learning from mistakes (r  =   .148,  p  = .003),  suggesting  that  students  who  reported  higher reliance on artificial intelligence tended to exhibit greater difficulties in making  decisions  and  in  drawing  lessons  from  previous  errors.  In contrast, the correlation between overreliance on artificial intelligence 
and perseverance was negative but smaller in magnitude (r =   .107, p 
= .031), while the association with goal setting was positive yet weak (r 
= .118, p = .018). Overall, these findings indicate that greater reliance on  artificial  intelligence  is linked  to  lower  self-regulatory functioning, particularly  in  critical  areas  such  as  decision  making  and  reflective learning.
Regarding  internal  consistency, reliability  coefficients  ranged  from .625  for  learning  from  mistakes  to  .781  for  goal  setting.  The  two  di- mensions  with  values  below  the  conventional  .70  thresh - olds—perseverance (α = .676) and learning from mistakes (α = .625)— 
are composed of only two items each, and Cronbach's alpha is known to be highly sensitive to the number of items. In this sense, values slightly below .70 are still considered acceptable when scales include few items or capture complex constructs, as suggested by Cortina (1993). Never- theless,  prior  analyses  demonstrated  satisfactory  construct  validity, thereby supporting the adequacy of these dimensions within the overall scale.
Next, in order to respond objective 1, a mediation analysis was done. 
The mediation analysis showed that goal setting was positively associ-
ated with perseverance (b = .512, p < .001), decision making (b = .138, 
p = .021), and learning from mistakes (b = .163, p = .007). Perseverance 
(b = .147, p = .002) and decision making (b = .261, p < .001) were also positively  associated  with  learning  from  mistakes.  With  respect  to 
overreliance  on  generative  artificial  intelligence,  it  was  negatively associated  with  perseverance (b =   .121,  p  = .011)  and  by  learning from mistakes (b =   .126, p = .011), whereas the path from decision making was not significant (b =   .082, p = .094). The direct association 
between  goal  setting  and  overreliance  on  generative  artificial  intelli - gence remained positive and significant (b = .241, p < .001). The model 
explained  15.6%  of  the  variance  in  perseverance,  1.3%  in  decision- making, 15.3% in learning from mistakes, and 7.1% in overreliance on generative  artificial  intelligence.  These  results  indicate  that  self- regulatory  capacity  may  account  for  only  a small  portion  of students’ overreliance  on  generative  AI—a  construct  inferred  here  through  a frequency-based measure. However, this estimate should be interpreted 
with caution due to potential underreporting in self-reports and the use of a single-item proxy, which  may not fully  capture the complexity of overreliance.  Likewise,  other  factors  such  as  AI  self-efficacy,  trust, perceived  usefulness,  institutional  norms,  or  AI  literacy  could  also contribute to these patterns. Thus, self-regulation should be framed as a 
partial explanatory framework rather than a comprehensive one.
At  the  indirect  association  level,  the  strongest  estimated  indirect association  was  observed  for  the  link  involving  perseverance alone  in relation  to  overreliance  on  AI (b =   .062,  95%  CI [  .117,    .012]), followed  by  a  smaller  but  significant  indirect  association  involving perseverance and learning from mistakes considered sequentially (b =   .009,  95%  CI  [  .022,    .001]).  The  remaining  indirect  associa - tions—those  involving  decision  making  alone  (b  =   .011,  95%  CI [  .032,  .002]),  learning  from  mistakes  alone  (b  =   .021,  95%  CI [  .052, .000]), and decision making together with learning from mis- takes  (b =   .005,  95%  CI [  .012,  .000])—were  not  reliably  different from zero. Consistent with these findings, the most pronounced contrast among  indirect  paths  favored  the  perseverance-only  route  over  the three-step route involving decision making and learning from mistakes. Overall, the pattern highlights perseverance as the key mediator atten -
uating the association between goal setting and overreliance on gener - ative  artificial  intelligence,  with  limited  evidence  for  pathways involving decision making.
This  study  revealed  an  apparently paradoxical pattern:  while  goal setting  was  positively  associated  with  perseverance, and  perseverance was  negatively  associated  with  AI overreliance, the  direct  association 
between goal setting and AI overreliance was positive. It is important to clarify that the term “overreliance” in this study refers specifically to a behavioral  proxy  reflecting  high-frequency  use  of  generative  AI  for essential  academic  tasks,  rather  than  general  AI use  for  occasional  or 
supportive  purposes.  Therefore,  the  paradox  observed  arises  from  the distinction  between  instrumental,  strategic  use  of  AI and  habitual  or substitutive use that may indicate overreliance.
A plausible explanation is that students with clear goals may report a more  intensive  use  of  AI tools  as  instrumental  resources  to  achieve desired outcomes more quickly, which is associated with higher levels of reliance.  However,  perseverance  appears  to  be  linked  to  a  protective pattern,  as students  who  persist  in  their  efforts  tend  to  report  greater reliance on their own strategies and lower reliance on AI support. Thus, 
goal  setting  is  associated  with  both  adaptive  self-regulation  and, simultaneously, a risk of instrumental overreliance on AI, depending on 
how strongly perseverance is present within the pattern of associations observed. A visual representation of the main findings can be found in Fig. 2.
Importantly,  these  mediation  results  should  be  interpreted  with caution,  as all  constructs  were  assessed  using  self-report  measures,  as commented before.  Common-method bias  and  social  desirability may have  influenced  both  the  magnitude  and  direction  of  the  estimated paths,  particularly  given  the  educational  context,  prior  instructor–- student relationships, and the sensitivity surrounding norms of AI use. Consequently,  the  observed  associations—especially  those  involving overreliance  on  generative  AI, which  was  operationalized  through  a frequency-based measure—may reflect conservative estimates.
To  address  the  second  objective,  students’  open-ended  responses were analyzed to explore their perceptions of generative AI in decision- making and self-regulated learning. The content analysis identified nine distinct categories (see Supplementary Material 4 for the full thematic breakdown, frequencies, and representative examples).
The results indicate that generative AI remains a peripheral tool for most  students,  with  42.51%  reporting  minimal  or  no  use  and  16.60% limiting  its  use  to  basic  information  search  and  evaluation.  From  a theoretical  standpoint,  the  most  relevant  categories  involve  active integration into  cognitive  and  metacognitive processes.  These  include strategy use for academic tasks (12.96%), decision-making and option appraisal (7.69%), and goal setting and planning (2.43%).
Notably, despite the potential for overreliance, a segment of students (3.24%)  explicitly  reported  ethical  caution  or  deliberate  avoidance, citing concerns over originality and responsibility. Overall, the pattern 
of responses  suggests  that  while  some  students  employ  AI to  support higher-order  functions  like  workflow  organization  or  alternative appraisal, for the majority, these tools are not yet a central element in 
their decision-making or self-regulated learning routines.
Overall,  the  pattern  of  responses  indicates  that  only  a  small  pro- portion of students use generative AI in ways that meaningfully shape their  decisions  or  self-regulated  learning  processes.  For  most  re- spondents,  these  tools  either  play  no  role  or  are  limited  to  basic information-seeking purposes. Thus, generative AI appears to remain a complementary  resource  rather  than  a  central  element  in  students’ decision-making routines.
4. Discussion
4.1. Main findings
This  study  pursued  two  complementary  objectives  concerning  the role  of generative  artificial  intelligence  in  higher  education. The  first objective  was  to  examine  how  specific  dimensions  of  self-regulation relate to education students' overreliance on generative AI. The second objective explored students’ perspectives on how AI tools contribute to 
their decision-making and self-regulated learning, drawing on qualita -
tive insights from an open-ended question.
Concerning  the  results  from  the  mediation  analysis,  an  apparently paradoxical pattern was found: while goal setting was associated with adaptive  regulatory  processes  such  as perseverance and  learning  from mistakes, it also showed a positive direct association with AI use, sug- gesting that students with clear goals may employ AI tools strategically to achieve their objectives. Perseverance, in contrast, appeared to buffer against overreliance, highlighting its central role within self-regulatory processes.
However, it should be noted that overreliance was assessed using a single-item  frequency  measure,  which  captures  a  concrete  behavioral indicator but does not fully represent habitual or cognitive reliance on 
AI. Therefore, these associations should be interpreted cautiously, as the narrow operationalization may influence the observed patterns.
4.2. Theoretical and practical implications
The  present  findings  offer  several  contributions  to  self-regulated learning theory in the age of generative AI. As an early empirical step, this study is positioned to initiate the theorization of SRL–AI dynamics, emphasizing an exploratory yet foundational approach to this emerging field.  Classical  socio-cognitive  and  motivational models  conceptualize Self-regulation learning  as  a cyclical  process  in  which  learners  proac - tively  set  goals,  deploy  strategies,  monitor  progress,  and  reflect  on outcomes (Pintrich, 2004; Zimmerman, 2000). The mediation analysis suggests  that,  under  conditions  of  ubiquitous  AI  access,  these  SRL components may diverge: strong goal setting can coexist with a pattern of instrumental, heavy AI use, whereas perseverance and learning from

This nuance  aligns with recent mapping  work at the intersection of AI and Self-regulation learning,  which  shows  that  most  AI-supported  SRL in- terventions target  cognitive  and  metacognitive processes,  while  moti- vational regulation remains comparatively underexplored (Banihashem

Beyond  self-regulation  learning  theory,  the  specific  associations found enrich emerging conceptualizations of generative AI in education. Systematic  reviews  of ChatGPT  in  educational settings  highlight  both substantial opportunities (e.g., enhanced feedback, increased access to explanations) and significant risks, including academic integrity issues and the possibility that students bypass productive struggle (Adel et al., 2024;  Adigüzel  et  al.,  2023;  Montenegro-Rueda  et  al.,  2023). Meta-analytic  evidence  further  indicates  that  ChatGPT-based  in- terventions  can  improve  learning  performance  and  higher-order thinking,  but  that  these  benefits  depend  critically  on  how  the  tool  is embedded  in  instruction  (Deng  et  al.,  2024).  Our  specific  pattern  of associations,  where  perseverance  and  learning  from  errors  are  nega- tively related to AI reliance, offers empirical support for the concern that unstructured AI use  may  erode  the  very  regulatory  processes  that  un- derpin  deep  learning.  These  paths  thus  help  specify  what  an  “AI-de- pendent” self-regulation learning profile might look like: one in which students  outsource  planning  and  evaluation  to  AI,  weakening error-based  learning  and  sustained  effort  over  time  (Kasneci  et  al., 2023).
For researchers, the findings regarding the indirect and direct asso-
ciations  underscore  the  need  to  study  AI and  self-regulation relations using more fine-grained approaches. Systematic reviews emphasize that 
AI is predominantly used  as  an  intervention layer  supporting  specific self-regulation components (e.g., metacognition, planning, monitoring), 
often  within  higher  education  contexts  (Heikkinen  et  al.,  2023; Bani- hashem  et  al.,  2025). However,  much  less  is known  about  how  every day, student-initiated use of general-purpose tools like ChatGPT trans -
forms  motivational regulation,  help-seeking  thresholds, and  error pro- cessing.  Future  studies  should  therefore  (a)  combine  self-report  SRL measures  with  trace  data  from  AI interactions, (b)  adopt  longitudinal and  experimental designs,  and  (c)  examine  learner-profile differences, 
as  recommended  in  recent  AI–self  regulation  research  agendas (Banihashem et al., 2025; Faza & Lestari, 2025).
From  a  pedagogical  standpoint,  the  pattern  of  direct  and  indirect associations identified in the mediation model suggest that teachers and instructional designers should treat generative AI explicitly as a regula- tion-sensitive  tool.  Studies  indicate  that  “guidance-based”  ChatGPT frameworks,  where  students  attempt  tasks  independently  before consulting  the  tool,  can  enhance  Self-regulation  and  higher-order thinking  more  effectively  than  unconstrained  use  (Klar,  2025). Digital-age  self-regulation  reviews  also  show  that  technologies  yield benefits  when  aligned  with  clear  strategy  instruction,  metacognitive prompting, and  structured opportunities to  learn  from  errors  (Faza  & Lestari, 2025). Consistent with the observed positive direct association between goal setting and AI overreliance, alongside the negative indi- rect associations through perseverance and learning from mistakes, our findings  are  consistent  with  instructional approaches that  (a)  concep - tually  position  generative  AI  as  a  second  step  after  initial  student reasoning, (b) emphasize the inclusion of prompts requiring students to explain or critique AI outputs, and (c) highlight the importance of pre- serving  phases  of  productive  struggle  from  full  delegation  to  AI. For teacher  education,  this suggests  the  relevance  of training  teachers  not only  in AI literacy  but  also  in the  pedagogical  orchestration of AI use across  the self-regulated  learning cycle (Kasneci  et  al., 2023; Pintrich, 2004; Zimmerman, 2000).
For education students, the observed pattern in which perseverance and  learning  from  mistakes  are  negatively  associated  with  AI over- reliance  highlights  the  need  to  integrate  AI literacy  with  regulatory competencies.  Surveys  show  that  university  learners  exhibit  high acceptance  and  frequent  use  of  ChatGPT,  often  in  pragmatic  and outcome-oriented ways (Habibi et al., 2023; Adel et al., 2024). At the 
same  time,  rapid  evidence  reviews  emphasize  that  learning  benefits occur  when  students  actively  engage  with  the  tool,  for  example,  by refining prompts, comparing responses, or checking claims against other sources (Deng et al., 2024; Lo, 2023; Montenegro-Rueda et al., 2023). In light  of  our  qualitative  findings,  learner-facing  interventions  should include routines such as (a) articulating one's plan before prompting AI, (b) using AI primarily for feedback and alternative perspectives, and (c) documenting  how  AI  suggestions  were  adapted  or  rejected.  These practices  directly  foster  perseverance,  decision-making,  and  learning from mistakes while reducing the risk of dependency.
Finally,  the  results  have  implications  for  policy  makers  and  in- stitutions seeking to align with recent global guidance on AI in educa -
tion. UNESCO's Guidance for Generative AI in Education and Research and the OECD Digital Education Outlook 2023 both call for human-centred 
AI ecosystems grounded in trust, transparency, and robust governance (Miao & Holmes, 2023; OECD, 2023). Our quantitative and qualitative findings  suggest  that  institutional  policies  should  not  only  regulate acceptable  uses  but  also  invest  in  capacity  building  in  self-regulation learning  and  AI  literacy.  This  includes  embedding  self-regulation learning  outcomes  in  curricula,  developing  teacher  training  on AI-supported  pedagogy,  and  ethically  using  analytics  to  monitor  pat- terns that may indicate emerging overreliance (Heikkinen et al., 2023; Banihashem et al., 2025).
4.3. Limitations and prospective
This study presents several important limitations that should guide future  research.  First,  its  cross-sectional  and  single-institution  design limits the ability to draw causal inferences about the relationships be-
tween  self-regulation and  AI overreliance, echoing  concerns  in  recent reviews that much of the current evidence on educational AI is obser - vational in nature (Lo, 2023; Montenegro-Rueda et al., 2023). In addi -
tion, the sample was limited to undergraduate education students, who may be particularly sensitive to norms regarding AI use or less familiar with  AI and  digital  technologies  compared  with  students  in other  dis- ciplines. These contextual constraints should be considered when gen- eralising the findings to higher education more broadly. Future research should  therefore  aim  to  include  larger  and  more  diverse  samples, encompassing students from multiple  institutions and a variety of dis- ciplines,  to  capture  potential  differences  in  SRL–AI dynamics  across educational contexts, and should also employ longitudinal and experi - mental  designs,  such  as  cross-lagged  panel  models  or  SRL-based  in- terventions,  to  determine  whether  low  perseverance  and  limited error-based learning prospectively predict increases in AI overreliance or, conversely, whether intense AI use gradually erodes core regulatory processes (Huang, Lai, et al., 2024).
Second,  all  constructs  were  measured  via  self-report,  raising  con- cerns  about  common method  variance  and  potential discrepancies be- tween  perceived  and  actual  AI use.  Recent  scholarship  highlights  the value  of  combining  validated  scales  (e.g.,  Morales-García,  Sair- itupa-S´anchez,  & Morales-García, 2024) with digital trace data to more accurately capture online learning behaviours (Heikkinen et al., 2023). Additionally, AI overreliance was inferred indirectly from a single-item measure capturing frequency of generative AI use for academic writing. Although this item targeted a clearly defined and observable behavior, frequency  alone  might  not  provide  direct  evidence  of cognitive  disen - gagement, reduced effort, or diminished learning. Therefore, the oper- ationalization  reflects  a  behavioral  proxy  rather  than  a multidimensional  assessment  of  AI  overreliance.  While  single-item measures  can  be  appropriate  for  concrete  and  behaviorally  specific 

9 
H. Galindo-Domínguez et al.                                                                                                                                                                                                                 Computers in Human Behavior 181 (2026) 108985 
constructs,  future  research  should  develop  and  validate  multi-item  in- struments that explicitly distinguish between instrumental use, strategic augmentation,  and  substitution  processes  in  order  to  more  precisely capture overreliance as a regulatory phenomenon.
A third limitation concerns the lack of contextual granularity, as the study  did  not  differentiate between  academic  domains  or  types  of AI- supported  tasks—despite  evidence  that  reliance  on  AI varies  substan - tially  across  learning  activities  and  student  profiles  (Stojanov,  Liu, & Koh,  2024).  To  address  this  gap,  subsequent  studies  should  examine SRL–AI dynamics  across  diverse  disciplines  and  task  types  to  identify when  generative  AI functions  as  a  scaffold  that  supports  regulation versus a substitute that displaces productive struggle.
Fourth, the model accounted for only a modest portion of the vari- ance in AI overreliance, indicating that self-regulatory processes explain just part of students’ tendencies to rely on AI. Important motivational, contextual,  and  AI-specific  variables,  such  as  AI  self-efficacy,  trust, perceived  usefulness,  or  normative  climates,  were  not  included.  Map- ping reviews indicate that SRL is shaped by a constellation of cognitive, motivational,  and  contextual  factors  (Banihashem  et  al.,  2025),  and future  research  should  therefore  broaden  the  predictive  framework  to include these elements, which may yield richer explanatory power and help  identify  “at-risk”  learner  profiles  that  require  tailored interventions.
Together,  addressing these limitations  will not  only  strengthen the empirical  base  of AI-related  SRL research  but  also  support  the  devel- opment  of  more  targeted  pedagogical  and  institutional  strategies  for reducing AI overreliance while fostering deep, autonomous learning.
