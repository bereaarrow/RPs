Perceptions of nursing students and  faculty  members towards academic  integrity and  professionalism in Saudi  Arabia:  A cross-sectional study 
Mariam Almutairi a,b,*, Talal Ali F. Alharbi c, Fiona Timmins a 
a UCD School of Nursing, Midwifery and Health Systems, UCD Health Sciences Centre, 4, Stillorgan Rd, Belfield, Dublin, Ireland b Department of Nursing, College of Applied Medical Science, Prince Sattam bin Abdulaziz University, Al-Kharj, Saudi Arabia

A R T I C L E I N F O  
Keywords: Academic integrity Professionalism Academic dishonesty Nursing student Faculty member 
A B S T R A C T  
Background: Maintaining and nourishing academic integrity along with professionalism in nursing education is integral and imperative to the development of the nursing profession. However, evidence shows that academic dishonesty and incompetency exist and are raising problems among college students of all disciplines including nursing. To date, limited studies have been conducted in this area in Saudi Arabia. 
Objectives: To determine the perceptions of nursing students and faculty members towards academic integrity and professionalism. 
Design: A cross-sectional design. 
Settings and participants: A convenience sample of undergraduate students enrolled in government universities in Saudi Arabia, and their faculty members. 
Methods: 342 Saudi nursing students and 113 nursing faculty members were surveyed with a valid and reliable questionnaire modified to the study setting. 
Results: Findings revealed that the majority of nursing students and faculty members were aware of the nature of academic dishonesty and unprofessionalism. Less 40 % of students and teachers reported academic dishonesty and unprofessionalism as frequent. The majority of respondents in both groups revealed there were no existing policies  in  their  colleges  relevant  to  academic  integrity/dishonesty. For  those  who  reported  the  presence  of a policy, the highest percentage of the respondents expressed just occasional implementation. There was no sig- nificant difference between the perceptions of students and faculty in all scenarios regarding academic integrity and  professionalism. Lastly,  the  level  of education  and  affiliated  nursing  college  was  associated  with  the  re- spondents’ perceptions. 
Conclusions: Nursing  students  and  faculty  members  have  considerable  cognizance  of the  concept  of academic integrity  and  professionalism.  However,  the  occurrence  of  academic  dishonesty  and  unprofessionalism  was observed at both universities. The lack of established policies is a particular challenge for management. Hence, it 
is  essential  that  educational  institutions  formulate  clear  academic  policies  that  cultivate  academic  integrity, prevent academic dishonesty, and enhance the level of professionalism.   

1. Introduction 
Nursing  as  a  profession  is  imbued  with  indispensable  values  of integrity,  honesty,  and  utmost  professionalism.  These  values  are embedded  in  the  nursing  curriculum  and  form  a  crucial  part  of  the teaching-learning process. However, the academic and clinical demands might  result  in stress  among  nursing  students,  which  in turn  can  be  a contributing factor to academic dishonesty. Students under pressure to perform  both  clinically  and  academically  may  naively  resort  to 
dishonest  approaches  to  ensure  their  success  (Kratovil,  2021).  Mean- while,  academic  dishonesty  is frowned  upon  by nursing  faculty,  espe- cially as it goes against the core ethical and moral principles of nursing 
as  a  profession  (Kratovil,  2021).  Therefore,  breaches  of  academic integrity  reflect  dishonest  behavior  and  can  cause  gaps  in  knowledge and skills acquisition among students, who will become faculty members 
in the future and who will carry this misconduct in their practice. This unethical conduct leads to negative repercussions which extend beyond students’  educational  years,  affecting  ethical  professional  practice

On the other hand, academic integrity establishes students’ personal and professional facets with moral sense and the fundamental values of honesty, fairness, trust, responsibility and courage (International Center for  Academic  Integrity,  2021).  Despite  the  well-known  importance of academic  integrity,  evidence  from  related  studies  demonstrate  an alarming  global  increase  in  acts  of academic  dishonesty  across  higher education course specializations (Balbuena and Lamela, 2015; Lin and Wen,  2007;  Mustapha  et  al.,  2017),  including  nursing  programmes (McClung and Gaberson, 2021; Rafati et al., 2020; Keçeci et al., 2011). Academic  dishonesty  includes,  plagiarizing work,  cheating  on  assign- ments and examinations, data fabrication and unauthorized collabora - tions  between  students  (Ampuni  et  al.,  2020;  Birks et  al.,  2018).  The underlying  reasons  vary  from  peer  approval,  low  perceptions  of  its seriousness, attitude and perceived social norms on cheating, and lack of knowledge  regarding  academic  policies  (Taradi  et  al.,  2011;  McCabe et  al.,  2010).  Cultural  beliefs  regarding  the  acceptability of academic misconduct  (Yukhymenko-Lescroart,  2014)  and  the  advancement  in technologies and digitalization of educational materials and submissions have also been attributed as influencing factors (Bachore, 2014; Szabo and Underwood, 2004). 
Although  there  have  been  several  studies  conducted  worldwide regarding  this  phenomenon,  limited  research  can  be  found  in  Saudi Arabia where findings to date have focused on elements and effects of academic  integrity  (Farahat,  2022;  Tayan,  2017).  Also,  extended research  has  been  made  on  lapses  in  professionalism in  relation  with academic integrity (Babelli et al., 2015), academic integrity perceptions 
and  misconduct  among  university  students  in  general  (Razek,  2014; Guruswami  et  al.,  2022)  and  E-learning  factors  (Muhammad,  2020). Hence, there is scarce literature relevant to faculty and nursing students’ perception on academic integrity and professionalism in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, which prompted this research. 
2. Background 
Academic integrity is essential in higher education since it contrib -
utes  to  the  acquisition  of intellectual competence  and  moral  develop - ment of college students (Nuss, 2012). Within nursing education this is especially  important  as  the  requirement  for  academic  integrity  and ethical conduct goes hand in hand with the moral and ethical standards required for professional nursing practice (Lynch et al., 2022). Profes- sionalism  is vital  in  the  context  of  nursing  practice  and  involves  the application of knowledge to make and act upon ethical and moral de- cisions  (Registered  Nurses’  Association  of  Ontario,  2007).  In  spite  of this,  there  are  considerable  findings  that  reveal  academic  dishonesty among  nursing  students  (Krueger,  2014;  Klocko,  2014;  Keçeci  et  al., 2011) and clinical settings (Abusafia et al., 2018). 
Examples  of  academic  dishonesty  in  the  nursing  field  include cheating  (assignments  and  examinations), sharing  answers  with  class- mates,  and  plagiarism  (Park  et  al.,  2013;  McCabe,  2009;  Hart,  2021). Notably,  research  findings  from  Krueger  (2014)  reveal  that  dishonest behavior  also manifests in clinical settings, and can include, failing  to report  incidents  and  errors  with  patients,  falsification  of records,  and breaking  confidentiality.  Other  forms  of  professional  misconduct  like breaching patient’s privacy and inaccurate recording of vital signs and documenting findings that were not assessed or charting false findings 
were also revealed in the findings of Birks et al. (2018) and Bultas et al. (2017).  These  findings  establish  the  correlation  between  academic dishonesty and unprofessional behavior, which is supported with older research by Hilbert (1985) which found unethical classroom behaviors 
were significantly positively related to total unethical clinical behaviors. A more recent study by Maoz et al. (2022) also confirmed the assump -
tion that academic dishonesty is related to clinical dishonesty. As such, 
the consequence of these actions may not only affect students’  knowl -
edge but could result in unethical professional practice (LaDuke, 2013; Abusafia et al., 2018) and misconduct in clinical areas that jeopardizes patient care and outcomes (McClung and Gaberson, 2023). 
The extent to which a student commits academic dishonesty relates to their perceptions of the seriousness of their actions, level of education about the concept of academic integrity as well as awareness of policies 
that  address  these  problems  (Tabsh  et  al.,  2017;  Yang  et  al.,  2013; McCabe  et  al.,  2010).  Kiekkas  et  al.  (2020)  identified  several  contrib - uting factors such as demands for high grades, absence of severe pun- ishment for cheating, examination settings and even unfair treatment by 
school  personnel.  Further,  Costley  (2019)  revealed  that  students consider academic dishonesty as a natural aspect of their learning pro-
cess and Macale et al. (2017) found students acclimatization to deceitful behaviors may cause stabilization of misconduct. These findings imply 
that  students’  perception  greatly  influences  their  involvement  in  such activities (Bisping et al., 2010). In the same manner, the role of educa -
tors in deterring acts of dishonesty is vital and similarly influenced by their  own  perceptions  of  academic  integrity/academic  dishonesty. McClung and Gaberson (2021) put emphasis on fostering honesty and integrity in learning environments as a way to reduce engagement with dishonest  behaviors,  rather  than  being  reactive  once  the  action  has occurred. 
There  is a paucity  of research  focusing  on  the  perceptions of aca- demic integrity and professionalism among nursing students and faculty in  the  Kingdom  of Saudi  Arabia.  Understanding their  perceptions can assist with gaining insight into their respective views on the seriousness of  the  matter  and  could  encourage  the  implementation  of  strong rigorous standards and relevant policies (Macale et al., 2017; Gaberson, 2007)  that  endorse  academic  integrity  and  prevent  academic dishonesty. 
3. Methods 
This study utilized a cross-sectional design through an online survey 
of  nursing  students  and  academic  faculty  members  at  two  reputable government universities in Riyadh region, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia. 
3.1. Sample 
The  sample  comprised  of  342  nursing  students  and  113  faculty members  which  were  selected  through  convenience  sampling.  The  in- clusion criteria were established for both subject categories that act to control  some  variables  that  might  influence  the  study  results.  For 
nursing  students,  those  who  were  currently  enrolled  in levels  three  to eight  of  the  Bachelor’s  degree  in  Nursing  in  the  academic  year 2021–2022  were  included.  Furthermore,  for  faculty  members,  those affiliated with nursing colleges of chosen universities of the same school 
year  were  included.  These  target  respondents  gave  their  consent  to participate in this study. 
3.2. Instrument 
The questionnaire, which was developed by Austin et al. (2005) and 
then  adopted  by  Aaron  et  al.  (2011),  was  used  to  collect  information from  the  study  participants.  Official  permission  to  use,  modify  and translate  the  questionnaire was  obtained  from  the  authors  in  order  to suit  its  utilization  in  Saudi  culture.  The  new  developed  (translated) questionnaire comprised of two parts: the first part determined the de- mographic characteristics, and the second part focused on twenty-three scenarios  on  academic  integrity  (scenarios  1–18)  and  professionalism behaviors (scenarios 19–23). 
The  questionnaire was  translated into  Arabic  and  then  back  trans - lated into  English by the researcher to ensure  the participants’ under - standing of its statements, which in turn can guarantee the reliability of 
their  answers.  Two  bilingual  academic  professionals  revised  the

A  back-translation  was  conducted  by  the researcher and revised by another bilingual translator to assure that the translated  statements  retained  the  same  meanings.  This  helped  in identification and modification of differences between source and target language,  and  in  achieving  conceptual  equivalence  (Maneesriwongul and Dixon, 2004). To establish content validity, the questionnaire was submitted to a jury composed of six experts in the field of nursing ed- ucation  to  confirm  the  relevance  of  questionnaire  statements  to  the study  objectives.  Five  out  of  six  of  the  jury  members  gave  feedback. Based  on  the  feedback,  some  modifications  were  made  to  the  ques- tionnaire  to  be  more  compatible  with  Saudi  culture.  The  Cronbach’s alpha  was  computed  to  determine  the  degree  of  internal  consistency homogeneity between  the  instrument items.  Based  on  the  result,  this tool was highly reliable with alpha value of 0.931. 
3.3. Ethical considerations 
The  study  was  granted  ethical  approval  from  Institutional Review Boards (IRB) from the study site. Written Approval to conduct the study 
was  obtained from the administrative personnel  at colleges of nursing where  the  study  was  conducted.  The  participants were  assured  in  the invitation  letter  with  the  questionnaire  that  their  participation  was voluntary and identities would be kept confidential. The names of uni-
versities were also kept anonymous. Data privacy and management was extremely considered. All soft copies were kept in a secure folder file. 
3.4. Data analysis 
The data were analyzed using SPSS version 26. Descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentage and means) were used to interpret the data on demographic  profile,  perceptions  on  academic  integrity  and  profes- sionalism. Pearson’s chi-square test was used to identify the significant difference and association between the identified variables at p < 0.05 significance level. 
4. Results 
4.1. Demographic profile 
There were 342 nursing students who completed the survey. Three- quarters  of the  students  255  (74.6  %)  were  aged  between  20  and  22 years old, with a mean age 21.1 ± 1.3. The majority of the students were single 268 (78.4 %), and one fifth of them were married: 71 (20.8 %). 
Studying  nursing  in  Saudi  Arabia  takes  4 years  (8  levels),  and  in  this study  the  first  year  (levels  1  & 2)  were  excluded.  The  reason  for  the exclusion is that students at these levels study foundation subjects that 
have  nothing  to  do  with  nursing,  and  some  of  the  scenarios  in  the questionnaire are  related  to  assignments  on  core  subjects  taken  from level  3.  Therefore,  the  participants were  from  level  3 to  level  8.  One- quarter of the students were on the 4th level in their study, and about one-fifth  were  on  the  6th  level.  Among  fourth-year students,  only  40 (11.7 %) were from 8th level. Slightly more than two thirds 223 (65.2 %) 
of the  respondents were  from  college-  B, denoting  119  (34.8  %) were from College-A (Table 1). 
Furthermore, 113  faculty  members  were  involved  in  the  study,  of whom,  sharply  57  (50.4  %)  were  within  the  age  group  30–39  year. About 18 (16 %) were <30 years old, and 6 (5.3 %) were 50 years old 
and above. The mean age of faculty members was 37.1, with standard deviation  of  ±7.1.  About  99  (87.6  %)  of  the  faculty  members  were 
married, while 9 (8 %) were single. Within the level of education, which 
is  classified  as  the  positions:  professor,  associate  professor,  assistant professor  and  lecturer,  36  (31.8  %)  and  54  (47.8  %)  were  assistant professors and lecturers respectively. The majority of the faculty mem-
bers were from college-A accounting for 79 (69.9 %). The student and faculty  response  rates  were  100  % since  all  participants answered  the questionnaire. 
The  findings  further  unveiled  that  the  demographic  profile  of the respondents, age (p = 0.269; p = 0.861) and marital status (p = 0.722; p 
= 0.093), was not associated with perceptions on academic integrity and professionalism with the p-values >0.05 level of significance. What was found however, was an association between the level of education (p = 0.019; p = 0.003) and affiliated nursing college (p = 0.002; p = 0.044) with the perceptions of both group of respondents (students and faculty respectively) being statistically significant.

4.3. Perception on professionalism 
As reflected in Table 3, the majority of responses from students and 
faculty  were  in  agreement  on  scenarios  that  constitute  unprofessional behavior. The average percentages from all scenarios were 81.0 % and 88.70 % from nursing students and faculty members respectively. When asked  if unprofessional behaviors  are a common phenomenon at  their respective colleges, 69.2 % of students and 64.6 % of faculty members answered “No”. The majority of the students (68.4 %) and faculty (66.3 %)  affirmed  that  there  were  no  existent  policies  addressing  unprofes - sional  behaviors  at  their  college.  For  those  who  responded  about  the presence of a policy, the highest percentage (51.1 %- students and 65.8 %-faculty)  of the  respondents expressed  that  the  implementation was just “sometimes” (Table 3).

Among respondents there was also consensus regarding an absence of  existing  policies  that  deal  with  academic  dishonesty  and  unpro - fessionalism.  Of  those  who  indicated  policies  existed,  the  majority concurred  that  the  implementation was  just  occasional.  Lack of insti- tutional  policies  can  greatly  influence  cheating  behaviors  among  stu- dents  and  encourage  misconduct  that  violates  academic  integrity  and professionalism (Kwong  et  al.,  2010).  Alternatively, the  presence  and awareness of policies can inhibit acts of academic dishonesty (Jordan, 2001),  highlighting  the  importance  of  developing  and  implementing policies that promote academic integrity.

0.019; p = 0.003) and affiliated nursing college (p = 0.002; p = 0.044) with the perceptions of both group of respondents (students and faculty respectively)  being  statistically  significant.  This  implies  that  for  re- spondents  in  this  study,  educational  attainment  as  well  as  affiliated institution,  influence  perceptions  involving  academic  integrity  and professionalism. As such, it is vital that educational institutions develop programmes that shape faculty and students to value and foster integrity academically and professionally (Guerrero-Dib et al., 2020), and support prevention. 
6. Limitations 
In spite of the notable contribution of the findings of this study in the literatures, there are still some limitations that needs to be considered. The  generalizability  of  the  results  may  be  limited  due  to;  the  non- probability  sampling  used,  and  an  absence  of  gender  demographics captured in the data. 
7. Conclusions 
Nursing students and faculty members have considerable knowledge about the concept of academic integrity and professionalism. However, academic dishonesty remains a regular occurrence. This can be due to the  lack  of awareness,  established  policies  and/or  strong  implementa - tion  of  suggested  strategies  to  address  these  problems.  Hence,  it  is 
essential that educational institutions formulate clear academic policies that cultivate positive behavior and prevent unacceptable practices that affect  not  just  learning  but  future  professional  practice.  This  can  be brought  into  fruition  with  proper  coordination,  orientation  and dissemination from the school administration, faculty, parents and stu- dents  as  stakeholders. It  is vital  that  educational institutions develop programmes that shape faculty and students to value and foster integrity academically and professionally (Guerrero-Dib et al., 2020), and support prevention. This particularly in light of the ongoing stress that nursing students face, and the potential effect of this on both their well-being, but  also  their  intention  to  stay  in  nursing.  The  consequences  of  aca- demic misconduct could further push these nursing students away from the  profession  at  a  time  when  there  is  a  global  nursing  shortage. Certainly  the  universities  ought  to  act  as  gatekeepers for  professional misconduct, and there are potential links between academic and clinical dishonesty.  That  said  for  many  students  this  is  as  a  consequence  of pressure  to  perform  within  the  limit  timeframes, and  over  burdening themselves,  or  feeling  overwhelmed  within  the  programme.  It  is important  that  academic  dishonesty  prevention  is offered  globally  as part of a holistic student support initiatives, and any such initiatives are underpinned by clear and transparent policies that support the students’ towards success. The scope of this paper is limited to two universities, and the findings and limitations have indicated areas of recommenda -
tion for further research. First, it is recommended that future researchers should broaden the scope of the study and cover more universities. Also, they need to examine the development and implementation of policies on academic dishonesty as well as how to address the factors that may contribute to academic dishonesty.
