Introduction
Feedback is widely acknowledged as a key driver of student learning (Hattie & Timperley, 
2007). In practice, however, its impact often falls short of its potential, especially in higher 
education. A key challenge is that many students do not substantially engage with or act 
on the feedback they receive (Carless & Boud, 2018). Researchers have noted generally 
limited student uptake of feedback in university settings (e.g. assignments returned with 
comments usually receive minimal follow-up from students). One important factor under
lying this gap is feedback literacy (FL), defined as ‘the understandings, capacities and 
dispositions needed to make sense of information and use it to enhance work or learning 
strategies’ (Carless & Boud, 2018, p. 1315). Because effective feedback depends not only

on information quality but also on learners’ capacity to interpret and act on it, FL is 
conceptually aligned with formative assessment (FA) principles. FA frameworks view 
feedback as formative only when learners compare their current performance against 
explicit criteria and take purposeful action to close gaps (Black & Wiliam, 1998; Sadler, 
1989). Accordingly, FL represents the learner-side capability that operationalises this 
process by eliciting, making sense of, and using feedback (Carless & Boud, 2018). 
Developing such literacy enables students to engage with feedback in ways that fulfil 
the formative purpose of assessment and ultimately enhance learning outcomes.

Over the past decade, several frameworks have clarified the multifaceted nature of FL. 
These models emphasise that effective engagement with feedback involves more than 
passive reception; it is a dynamic, learner-driven process that spans cognitive, emotional, 
behavioural, and social domains. For example, Sutton (2012) introduced a framework of 
‘knowing, being, and acting’, highlighting that becoming feedback literate requires not 
only intellectual understanding but also affective readiness and behavioural enactment. 
Similarly, Carless and Boud’s (2018) four-dimensional model includes appreciation of 
feedback, judgement-making, affect regulation, and taking action. Molloy et al. (2020) 
extended these frameworks by analysing student perspectives and identifying seven core 
components of FL – ranging from understanding feedback purposes and seeking infor
mation to processing feedback, regulating emotion, and engaging in judgement-making 
and information use. They further highlight learners’ role in co-constructing feedback 
opportunities within educational settings. Together, these frameworks present FL as 
a holistic capability that integrates belief systems, strategic behaviours, and social 
interaction.

Despite this strong foundation, a disconnect remains between conceptual models and 
their empirical operationalisation. While Sutton (2012), Carless and Boud (2018), and 
Molloy et al. (2020) emphasise the interplay between belief-oriented and behaviour- 
oriented dimensions, most existing measurement tools have thus far captured only 
selected aspects of the construct. Belief-oriented instruments typically assess learners’ 
dispositions – such as self-efficacy, emotional readiness, or perceived value of feedback – 
with items like ‘I am good at seeking feedback from different sources (e.g. teachers, peers 
or school mentors) to improve my learning’ (Zhan, 2022, p. 1100) or ‘I believe that I have 
the ability to deal with written feedback effectively’ (Song, 2022, p. 1450). Conversely, 
behaviour-focused tools emphasise tangible actions – for example, ‘I reconsider and 
refine my learning strategies based on feedback’ (Woitt et al., 2025, p. 260) or ‘I carefully 
consider comments about my work before deciding if I will use them or not’ (Dawson 
et al., 2024, p. 361) – but often do not address the attitudes or motivations behind those 
behaviours. Moreover, few instruments (Dawson et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2022) consider the 
social-relational aspects of feedback engagement, such as giving feedback to peers or 
participating in feedback dialogues, despite their centrality in socio-constructivist models. 
These varied emphases – shaped by different theoretical orientations, item formats, and 
validation aims – are perhaps unsurprising given that the measurement of FL remains in 
a formative stage of theoretical and methodological development.

This measurement landscape has important implications. Learners may hold posi
tive beliefs about feedback but lack the behavioural strategies to act on them – or 
may engage in feedback-related behaviours without genuine cognitive or emotional 
engagement. This misalignment limits the effectiveness of current instruments for

diagnosing FL and designing responsive pedagogical interventions. In response, 
scholars have called for measurement models that reflect the full conceptual complex
ity of FL (Noor et al., 2024). Rather than merging constructs within individual items, 
a more scalable and theoretically aligned approach is to represent cognitive-affective, 
behavioural, and social-interactional dimensions as distinct but interrelated subcom
ponents, allowing for meaningful analysis of their interplay within a coherent 
framework.

Moreover, socio-constructivist perspectives have deepened our understanding of FL 
by emphasising its dialogic and interactive nature. From this standpoint, feedback is 
not a one-way transmission from instructor to learner but a co-constructed process 
involving negotiation, shared interpretation, and mutual responsibility (Carless & Boud, 
2018). Within this paradigm, feedback-literate learners are not only consumers of 
feedback but also active contributors to feedback exchanges – capable of eliciting, 
interpreting, and providing feedback in collaborative settings. Although the reciprocal 
dimension has gained increased conceptual attention, it remains underrepresented in 
most existing assessment tools, further underscoring the need for more comprehen
sive models.

In response to these theoretical and methodological gaps, the present study 
introduces and validates the Learner Feedback Literacy Scale, a new instrument 
grounded in an integrated, socio-constructivist framework. This scale conceptualises 
FL as a multidimensional construct encompassing internal dispositions, feedback- 
related behaviours, and reciprocal interactions. Drawing on recent theoretical and 
empirical work (e.g. (Carless & Boud, 2018; Dawson et al., 2024; Molloy et al., 2020; 
Zhan, 2022), we identified a set of key dimensions that collectively capture these 
elements. In brief, our framework posits that a feedback-literate learner: appreciates 
the value of feedback, is proactive in seeking and eliciting feedback for improvement, 
effectively processes feedback information and takes action to implement, can manage 
the affective challenges associated with receiving critique, and understands feedback 
as part of a reciprocal, social process. These hypothesised dimensions (detailed in the 
Method section) integrate the attitudinal and behavioural aspects of FL while also 
emphasising the learner’s participation in feedback dialogues. We expected that these 
dimensions would be interrelated and together define an overall higher-order FL 
competency.

To develop the scale, we followed a rigorous process of item generation, expert review, 
pilot testing, and statistical validation. We also examined how the new scale relates to an 
external construct – namely, students’ academic help-seeking behaviours – to gather 
evidence of criterion validity. Academic help-seeking was chosen because it is concep
tually linked to FL: seeking help is a form of proactively obtaining feedback or guidance, 
and effective help-seeking (especially instrumental help-seeking aimed at learning) aligns 
with key aspects of FL (Fletcher, 2018; Tempelaar et al., 2018). Thus, we anticipated that 
students who score high on FL would also exhibit more productive help-seeking tenden
cies. In the sections below, we describe the methodology of our scale development and 
validation in detail. By providing a clear account of the design and implementation 
procedures, we aim to demonstrate that the resulting instrument is theoretically 
grounded, psychometrically sound, and useful for advancing research and practice in 
student FL.

Method
The development of the scale followed a structured multi-phase process involving Design 
and Implementation stages. Each stage comprised specific procedures to ensure the 
scale’s content validity, construct validity, and reliability. In the design phase, we estab
lished a conceptual foundation and created the initial item pool, whereas the implemen
tation phase involved administering the scale to a sample of students and performing 
statistical analyses for validation (see Table 1).

Design phase

Conceptual framework
To establish a strong foundation for the scale, we conducted literature review 
focused on multidimensional perspectives of FL. Our conceptualisation was primar
ily informed by Carless and Boud’s (2018) model, which emphasised the learner’s 
active role in appreciating feedback, making evaluative judgements, managing 
affect, and taking action. We further drew on Molloy et al’.s (2020) framework, 
which introduced additional dimensions such as recognising feedback as a dialogic 
process. In addition to these models, we reviewed FL measurement tools devel
oped, which informed both item wording and construct operationalisation. 
Synthesising these sources, we identified six interrelated dimensions that capture 
key learner capabilities in engaging meaningfully with feedback. These dimensions 
are summarised in Table 2 along with how they have been represented in prior 
studies, including recent FL scales.

Appreciating feedback. Recognising the value of feedback and being open to receiving 
it. This dimension reflects a learner’s disposition to view feedback as a useful tool for 
growth rather than as mere evaluation. Feedback-literate students understand that feed
back can come in various forms (comments from instructors, peer critique, automated 
hints, etc.) and actively welcome feedback opportunities. They acknowledge feedback’s 
role in improving their learning and thus approach feedback with a positive, appreciative 
mindset (Carless & Boud, 2018).

Eliciting feedback. Taking initiative to seek feedback information to address one’s 
learning needs. Rather than passively waiting for feedback, feedback-literate learners 
proactively seek input on their work. This can include asking teachers or peers for specific 
feedback, using exemplars or rubrics to self-generate feedback, or leveraging digital tools 
for immediate performance feedback. This dimension aligns with the concept of learner 
agency; by formulating questions and seeking clarity, students take ownership of the 
feedback process (Winstone et al., 2017).

Managing affect. Regulating one’s emotional responses to feedback to remain receptive 
and constructive. Receiving critical feedback or lower-than-expected evaluations can 
trigger negative emotions (such as anxiety, defensiveness, or disappointment) that 
impede learning (Carless & Boud, 2018). A feedback-literate student can manage these 
affective reactions – for example, by viewing criticism as a learning opportunity rather 
than a personal attack. Molloy et al. (2020) noted that effective feedback recipients

acknowledge their emotional responses and work through them, rather than ignoring or 
being overwhelmed by them.

Processing feedback. Instead of merely accepting feedback passively, learners assess its 
credibility by considering the expertise, reliability, and trustworthiness of the sources. This 
process includes establishing criteria for ‘good work’ standards and participating in 
discussions about effective practices. Additionally, Zhan (2022) highlights the significance 
of students comprehending and evaluating the quality of feedback, extracting actionable 
information, and appreciating diverse perspectives. By developing their sense-making 
abilities and evaluative judgement, learners can make informed decisions regarding the 
quality of their work.

Enacting feedback. Taking concrete action based on feedback to improve future 
performance. This can include rewriting a paper after receiving comments, practicing 
a skill differently based on performance feedback, or incorporating suggestions into 
subsequent assignments. Feedback-literate students don’t just file away feedback; they 
actively implement changes and then monitor the results of those changes on their 
learning. By demonstrating that feedback is acted upon, learners reinforce the value of 
feedback and are likely to engage more in the future. This dimension corresponds to 
what Carless and Boud (2018) term ‘taking action’ and what Molloy et al. (2020) 
observed as students using feedback information from various sources in 
a deliberate manner.

Acknowledging feedback reciprocity. Acknowledging that feedback is a reciprocal, 
social process and engaging in it as such. This dimension captures the learner’s under
standing that effective feedback involves interaction – not only does the student receive 
feedback, but they also can contribute to the feedback process (for example, by giving 
feedback to peers or by dialoguing with instructors about feedback). While many earlier 
FL models did not explicitly include this aspect, our framework incorporates it to ensure 
a more holistic coverage. We drew inspiration from Molloy et al’.s (2020) model and 
Dawson et al’.s (2024) FLBS, both of which stress the importance of learners participating 
in feedback dialogues and peer feedback. In practice, this dimension might be evidenced 
by students engaging in peer review exercises or actively discussing feedback with their 
instructor – treating feedback as an ongoing conversation rather than a one-off comment.

Together, these six dimensions formed the theoretical basis. Notably, each hypothe
sised dimension in our framework can be mapped to similar constructs in at least one 
previously published model or scale of FL. This convergence with prior literature gave us 
confidence that our chosen framework was well-founded.

Item generation and content validation
We developed 27 items to ensure that the scale captured all key aspects of FL while 
remaining concise and practical for student use. The six dimensions in our framework are 
theoretically grounded and have been reflected in prior conceptual and empirical studies. 
Some dimensions also overlap with constructs already operationalised in existing instru
ments, allowing us to build on established conceptual boundaries without unnecessary 
repetition. Approximately 4–5 items were written for each dimension.

Content validation was carried out in multiple stages, primarily aimed at improving 
item quality. In the first stage, five experts rated each item’s relevance on a three-point 
scale (‘essential’, ‘useful but not essential’, ‘not necessary’) and provided qualitative feed
back on wording and alignment. Revisions were made based on their input to improve 
clarity and representativeness.

In the second stage, interviews were conducted with six undergraduates. Participants 
paraphrased each item and identified any confusing or unfamiliar expressions. Minor 
modifications were made based on their input, such as simplifying wording and replacing 
less familiar terms. A language specialist also reviewed items for grammatical accuracy 
and cultural appropriateness.

These two stages focused on refining and improving item clarity and conceptual 
alignment. After these qualitative refinements, we proceeded to a more formal content 
validity analysis with a larger panel of experts. Experts rated the necessity of each item 
using the same three-point scale. As a result of this process, 2 items that fell below the 
acceptable CVR were removed from the pool (for 14 evaluators, the threshold CVR ≈0.51). 
The remaining 25 items were thus judged to have strong content validity evidence, 
covering the essential aspects of the FL construct.

Incorporating help-seeking construct
As part of the construct validation process, academic help-seeking was identified as 
theoretically relevant external measure for evaluating the criterion-related validity of the 
scale. Help-seeking is widely acknowledged as a self-regulated learning strategy that aligns 
conceptually with FL, as both involve proactive engagement with learning challenges and 
the use of external input for improvement (Fletcher, 2018; Tempelaar et al., 2018). In 
particular, instrumental help-seeking – where learners request guidance to enhance under
standing and solve problems independently – closely mirrors the principles of FL, which 
emphasises active involvement, agency, and the productive use of feedback (Makara & 
Kuusinen, 2023; Nelson Le Gall, 1981). Conversely, executive help-seeking, characterised by 
efforts to obtain direct answers with minimal cognitive effort, represents a more superficial 
approach to learning that is theoretically misaligned with FL (Makara & Kuusinen, 2023).

Accordingly, we selected instrumental and executive help-seeking as external constructs 
to test the criterion-related validity. We hypothesised that FL would correlate positively with 
instrumental help-seeking, as both reflect a learner’s willingness to engage with feedback- 
like information for self-improvement, and negatively with executive help-seeking, which 
reflects avoidance of cognitive effort and limited feedback use. These hypothesised patterns 
are supported by previous findings linking adaptive learning strategies to deeper engage
ment and long-term learning outcomes (Karabenick & Newman, 2006). By examining these 
relationships, we aimed to determine whether the FL scale meaningfully distinguishes 
between students who seek help to learn and those who seek help to complete tasks 
with minimal effort, thereby providing empirical support for its criterion-related validity.

Implementation phase

Study group and data pre-processing
The scale was administered online and distributed through 12 departments across 8 
universities, yielding 564 voluntary student responses from Türkiye. Data were screened

for outliers using Mahalanobis distance, and 90 cases with values below 0.01 were 
excluded. The final dataset consisted of 474 participants.

The demographic characteristics of the participants were as follows: 67.5% female 
and 31.4% male. In terms of faculty distribution, 81% were from Education, 7.6% from 
Science and Literature, 4.7% from Engineering, 2.1% from Economics and 
Administrative Sciences, 1.9% from Health Sciences, 1.3% from Pharmacy, and 1.4% 
from other disciplines. Regarding academic standing, 3.4% were in the 
preparatory year, 21.5% in the first year, 27% in the second year, 35.7% in the 
third year, and 12.4% in the fourth year.

Criterion validity instrument
To evaluate criterion-related validity, two subscales – executive and instrumental help- 
seeking – from Şahin et al. (2025) were utilised. Cronbach’s α values were .939 for 
executive help-seeking and .930 for instrumental help-seeking.

Data analysis

Psychometric evaluation included confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and supporting 
analyses. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin value was 0.97, indicating excellent sampling adequacy.

Reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s α and composite reliability. Validity evidence 
was obtained through convergent and discriminant validity analyses. A second-order CFA 
was conducted to examine the relationship between the overall construct and its 
dimensions.

To test measurement invariance across different learner groups, multi-group CFA was 
performed within a structural equation modelling framework. Measurement invariance 
ensures that a construct is interpreted similarly across groups and was assessed through 
four hierarchical stages: configural, metric, scalar, and strict invariance. Each level imposes 
additional constraints, from the equivalence of factor structure to the equality of residual 
variances.

These analyses aimed to determine whether the FL scale consistently measured the 
construct across groups without bias.

Finally, the scale’s association with theoretically related constructs – instrumental and 
executive help-seeking – was tested to provide further support for construct validity. The 
final scale form administered to students included 37 items: 25 measuring learner FL and 
12 measuring help-seeking tendencies.

Findings

Processing and Enacting dimensions; these were merged. During this refinement process,

cross-loadings or redundancy across dimensions, with close monitoring of changes in 
psychometric properties and factor covariances. The final model (18 items, five dimen
sions) showed acceptable fit (see Table 3) and strong standardised item loadings (range 
.70–.91, all p < .001; Figure 1). Inter-factor correlations ranged .56–.83, indicating that

which feedback to use, and implement appropriate changes or strategies accordingly. 
In other words, the Decision-Making and Taking Action dimension represents learners’ 
skill in extracting meaning from feedback and using it to inform their subsequent actions, 
including the judgement of whether and how to incorporate feedback.

and help-seeking behaviours. Correlation analysis showed significant positive relation

no significant correlation with executive help-seeking, while Decision-Making and Taking 
Action had the strongest correlation with instrumental help-seeking (r = .66, p < .001). 
These findings suggest that feedback-literate learners are more likely to engage in 
productive, self-regulated help-seeking behaviours.

Discussion
The present study confirms that learner feedback literacy (FL) can be conceptualised as an 
integrated and multifaceted competency encompassing cognitive, affective, behavioural, 
and social dimensions. Its five-factor structure – Appreciating Feedback, Eliciting 
Feedback, Managing Affect, Decision-Making and Taking Action, and Acknowledging 
Feedback Reciprocity – was supported through rigorous psychometric analyses. Each 
dimension was operationalised through behaviourally and attitudinally grounded items 
that collectively capture the ways in which learners navigate feedback processes. This 
comprehensive structure reflects an understanding of FL not merely as a set of beliefs or 
attitudes, but as a repertoire of interrelated dispositions and practices enacted across 
individual and social learning contexts.

The cognitive dimension of FL is captured through the Appreciating Feedback factor, 
which reflects learners’ recognition of feedback as a tool for self-improvement. Items such 
as ‘Feedback makes me realise my strengths and weaknesses’ operationalise metacogni
tive awareness and an orientation towards continuous development – key features of 
a growth mindset. The affective dimension is represented through the Managing Affect 
factor, which includes learners’ capacity to regulate emotional responses to evaluative 
input. Maintaining openness to critique and emotional composure when faced with 
negative feedback are essential preconditions for constructive engagement (Carless & 
Boud, 2018). The behavioural dimension is reflected in both the Eliciting Feedback and 
the Decision-Making and Taking Action factors. These assess the learner’s proactive stance 
in feedback processes – seeking input, interpreting its relevance, and applying it to 
improve work. This active orientation aligns with conceptions of agentic feedback 
engagement, wherein learners do not passively receive feedback but rather mobilise it 
to support self-regulated learning (Winstone et al., 2017). Finally, the social dimension is 
addressed through Acknowledging Feedback Reciprocity, which recognises learners as 
both recipients and providers of feedback. By incorporating peer-oriented items such as ‘I 
provide input to help my peers refine their work’, the scale operationalises feedback as 
a dialogic and socially situated process (Molloy et al., 2020).

A key structural refinement of the instrument was the empirical and conceptual 
merging of Processing Feedback and Enacting Feedback into a unified Decision-Making 
and Taking Action factor. This integration is supported by factor loadings and cross- 
loading patterns observed during confirmatory factor analyses, as well as superior 
model fit indices. Conceptually, the coalescence of interpretation and action reflects the 
inseparability of comprehension and implementation in authentic learning contexts. 
Learners rarely process feedback in a vacuum; rather, interpretation and uptake are 



12 M. TEPGEÇ ET AL.

often part of a cyclical, iterative process whereby understanding guides adaptation. Prior 
studies have drawn process-oriented versus action-oriented distinctions (e.g. Carless & 
Boud, 2018; Dawson et al., 2024; Molloy et al., 2020; Zhan, 2022), yet our results suggest 
that these stages are not only sequentially related but frequently co-occurring in practice.

This study also contributes to the literature by addressing gaps in existing instruments, 
many of which privilege one dimension of FL – typically cognitive or behavioural – at the 
expense of others. While Carless and Boud’s (2018) foundational model has informed 
several existing measures, these scales often rely on self-perceptions (e.g. ‘I am good at 
implementing feedback’) that may not reflect actual practices. For instance, Zhan’s (2022) 
instrument includes items that assess learners’ beliefs about their feedback capacities, but 
such items risk conflating intended behaviour with enacted engagement. Dawson et al’.s 
(2024) behaviour-oriented scale represents a major advancement by focusing on observa
ble actions and feedback reciprocity; however, it omits the cognitive dimension of appre
ciation, such as recognising feedback as integral to learning. In contrast, our scale bridges 
these divides by incorporating cognitive appreciation, emotional regulation, proactive 
engagement, and social contribution within a single, coherent framework. This holistic 
structure offers a more ecologically valid tool for investigating how learners authentically 
engage with feedback across cognitive, affective, behavioural, and interpersonal domains.

Evidence of criterion-related validity further supports the instrument. Higher FL scores 
were significantly correlated with increased instrumental help-seeking, a form of self- 
regulated learning where students ask for hints or clarification to improve performance 
(Fletcher, 2018). This aligns with prior research indicating that feedback-literate learners 
actively mobilise external input to monitor and regulate their learning (Tempelaar et al., 
2018; Tepgec et al., 2025), reinforcing the theoretical link between FL and self-regulated 
learning strategies.

Developing students’ FL is pedagogically critical for making formative assessment (FA) 
truly effective. Researchers note that even the most carefully designed feedback has little 
formative value unless learners interpret and act upon it (Hattie & Timperley, 2007). In FA 
frameworks, teachers and learners share responsibility across five key strategies – clarify
ing learning intentions, eliciting evidence, providing feedback that moves learning for
ward, activating students as owners of their learning, and as instructional resources for 
one another (Wiliam, 2017). FL directly supports these strategies: Appreciating Feedback 
aligns with recognising goals and standards; Eliciting Feedback mirrors gathering evi
dence; Managing Affect sustains openness to critique; Decision-Making and Taking Action 
embodies using feedback to move learning forward; and Acknowledging Feedback 
Reciprocity enables peer activation. Thus, FL provides the psychological and behavioural 
infrastructure that allows FA to operate as intended. By cultivating FL, educators trans
form feedback from one-way information into an interactive process that enhances 
evaluative judgement, self-regulation, and sustained performance improvement.

Limitations and future research
Despite its contributions, some limitations warrant consideration. The sample, though 
diverse and drawn from eight institutions, was concentrated within education majors, 
potentially limiting generalisability. Future studies should examine the applicability of 
the scale across disciplinary and cultural contexts. Moreover, reliance on self-report

measures introduces potential biases, including social desirability and limited intro
spective access. Triangulating self-reports with behavioural observations, dialogic ana
lysis of feedback exchanges, or longitudinal performance data could enhance the 
instrument’s predictive and diagnostic utility. Future research could also investigate 
how strengthening learners’ FL impacts the enactment of formative assessment in 
classrooms or online settings. Experimental or design-based studies may reveal how 
FL-oriented pedagogies foster active learner engagement with feedback cycles. Lastly, 
while the current five-factor structure is both theoretically and statistically sound, 
future adaptations might explore the inclusion of context-specific variables such as 
learners’ preferences for feedback timing or modality, which could further enrich our 
understanding of feedback engagement in complex learning environments.

Conclusion
This study developed and validated a five-dimensional Learner Feedback Literacy Scale for 
higher education. The scale’s factor structure and psychometric properties are robust: all item 
loadings exceeded .70 and reliability coefficients were high (overall α = .956). Its dimensions 
(Appreciating Feedback, Eliciting Feedback, Managing Affect, Decision-Making and Taking 
Action, and Feedback Reciprocity) capture both well-established and novel aspects of how 
students engage with feedback. The expected positive associations with adaptive help- 
seeking behaviours confirmed its criterion validity, linking theoretical constructs to measur
able outcomes.

By integrating cognitive, emotional, behavioural, and social-interactional elements of 
feedback, the instrument provides a comprehensive diagnostic of students’ feedback 
literacy. We anticipate that this tool will be valuable to educators and researchers for 
diagnosing and improving students’ engagement with feedback. In sum, the scale 
advances theoretical understanding of FL and offers a practical means to support learners’ 
effective use of feedback.
