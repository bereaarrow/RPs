The Impact of AI Literacy on Academic Misconduct: What Works in the AI World to Ensure Learning and Integrity

Abstract
Keywords: AI Literacy; Academic Misconduct; Conceptions of Assessment; Academic Integrity; 

1. Introduction



2. Study 1: The Impact of Generative AI on Academic Misconduct: A Theoretical Model of GAI Academic Misconduct

Abstract

2.1. Introduction
The study of academic misconduct spans generations and has a long history in the psychology literature (McCabe et al., 2001). The prevalence of students engaging in academically dishonest behaviors is alarmingly high (Rettinger et al., 2024), prompting a response from the research com-munity to better understand these behaviors and to reduce their occurrence. Although the total number of students who acknowledge misconduct has remained relatively stable, the amount of misconduct has grown dramatically (Lee et al., 2024; Newton, 2018). This increase in frequency points to a pressing need to understand the personal and contextual factors that underpin academic cheating. A number of theoretical explanations for cheating have been offered, which help to inform efforts to develop programming, interventions, and policies designed to reduce and prevent cheating, especially the most serious offenses.

These theoretical explanations have included examinations of institutional climate (Scanlan, 2006), motivational achievement goal structures (Anderman & Koenka, 2017), peer norms (McCabe et al., 2001), and moral attitudes (Lee et al., 2020). The reason these four theories are of interest is because all share a common assumption that academic dishonesty is inherently wrong and problematic (Murdock & Stephens, 2007), that social context plays a role (Macfarlane et al., 2014), and that students can deliberately choose to engage in academically dishonest behaviors (Kolb et al., 2015; MacGregor & Stuebs, 2012; Putarek & Pavlin-Bernardić, 2020). However, these theories potentially yield differing results because they differ on the psychological mechanisms through which students come to the decision to cheat.

Importantly, little research has been undertaken to examine models based on AI literacy and these differing theoretical explanations in concert. Often, these models are examined in isolation (e.g., Bertram Gallant, 2017; Curtis & Clare, 2024; Macfarlane et al., 2014). However, there is an increasing need to examine academic integrity from a multitheoretical approach. Similar efforts have been proposed in the areas of motivation, personality, and development (Dweck, 2017). Combining approaches and examining academic integrity from multiple perspectives simultaneously allows for a more thorough understanding of the antecedents, mechanisms, and consequences of academic misconduct. Although studies have been conducted on four theoretical models of cheating to predict academic cheating in schools, no adult intelligence has been taken into account. It is not clear what role traditional academic integrity theories play in explaining the use of AI to cheat (Perry et al., 2026).

Therefore, the current study aimed to examine the dynamic interactions between these AI literacy and cheating models in order to better capture why and when college students cheat. We used a psychometric approach to examine this issue by first validating measures related to AI literacy and four selected cheating theory explanations, and then assessing the analytic power of AI literacy and each theoretical approach and its combination separately.


2.2. Literature Review and Hypothesis

2.2.1. AI Literacy (AIL) Analytical Model
The increasing incorporation of AI into teaching and learning environments has sparked interest in AI literacy (Al-Abdullatif & Alsubaie, 2024; B. Wang et al., 2023). One approach to AI education is to improve AI literacy (Casal-Otero et al., 2023; Chung et al., 2025). Nevertheless, the effective classroom integration of AI demands that student be AI-literate, encompassing understanding, utilization, monitoring, and critical reflection on AI applications in teaching (Ding et al., 2024).

AI literacy is a newly emerging concept that seeks to define, teach, and evaluate knowledge and skills related to AI. AI literacy refers to an individual’s ability to comprehend, utilize, monitor, and critically evaluate AI applications, even without the ability to develop AI models. Furthermore, it encompasses the ability to observe, ethically use, and critically evaluate AI applications and AI-generated content and their implications without necessarily having programming knowledge. AI literacy encompasses the competencies that enable individuals to critically evaluate AI technologies and leverage them for effective communication and collaboration (Long & Magerko, 2020; Wu et al., 2024). This concept entails the design and implementation of AI learning activities, tools, applications, and pedagogical models (Wu et al., 2024; Yim & Su, 2024).

The term “AI literacy” was first coined by Burgsteiner et al. (2016) and Kandlhofer et al. (2016), who described the competencies required to understand the basic knowledge and concepts of AI and to use AI technologies. In addition, Long and Magerko (2020) defined it as a set of competencies that enables individuals to critically evaluate, communicate, and collaborate effectively with AI and use it as a tool, online, at home, and in the workplace. Furthermore, Ng et al. (2021b, 2021a) added AI to every student’s AI literacy in work settings and everyday life and proposed it as a fundamental skill for everyone, not just computer scientists. He incorporated the model of Bloom’s Taxonomy, Technological Pedagogical Content Knowledge (TPACK), and AI concepts, practices, and perspectives into the instructional design of AI literacy education (Ng et al., 2021b, 2021a).

Carolus et al. (2023) stated that AI literacy covers “competencies needed to interact with AI technology in a self-determined and rational manner.” Pinski and Benlian (2023) provided a definition of General AI literacy, describing it ashumans “socio-technical competence consisting of knowledge regarding human and AI actors in human-AI interaction, knowledge of the AI process steps, that is, input, processing, and output, and experience in AI interaction.” Laupichler et al. (2023) define the concept of AI literacy as “competencies that include basic knowledge and analytical evaluation of AI, in addition to the critical use of AI applications by ordinary non-expert individuals.” They did not include (programming skills) in their definitions because they considered them a separate set of competencies beyond AI literacy (Laupichler et al., 2023).

Recently, several studies have developed AI literacy frameworks for education (Kong et al., 2023; Ng et al., 2021b; Wong et al., 2020). AI literacy does not require detailed technical expertise in AI applications. However, it spans four dimensions: understanding and comprehension of AI, practical application of AI, assessment of AI applications, and ethical considerations of AI (L. Zhao et al., 2022).

Long and Magerko (2020) provided a conceptual framework of AI literacy composed of five overarching themes derived from the literature (Long & Magerko, 2020).Wang et al. (2023) proposed a theoretical framework to conceptualize AI Literacy, comprising four constructs: Usage, defined as the ability to exploit AI technology to proficiently perform tasks; Awareness, the ability to identify and comprehend AI systems during interactions; Evaluation, the capacity to critically analyze and assess AI applications and their outcomes; and Ethics, which involves recognizing responsibilities and risks associated with using AI systems. 

Despite the growing body of research on AI Literacy, significant gaps remain (Lu et al., 2024). Most studies have relied on qualitative methodologies or small-scale quantitative analyses (Ng et al., 2021b, 2023; Schiavo et al., 2024; Su et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2024), limiting our understanding of the broader implementation and effectiveness of AI education (Ng et al., 2021b, 2023; Schiavo et al., 2024; Su et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2024). A significant majority (75 %) of the papers pertaining to AI literacy have focused on students (Al-Abdullatif, 2024; Almatrafi et al., 2024; Ng et al., 2023), while few have focused on student (An et al., 2023; Liang et al., 2023).

Therefore, student must understand what AI is and what it can do inside and outside the classroom, and engage with AI technologies in a self-determined manner. These sets of skills, including understanding, utilizing, and integrating various AI technologies to enhance teaching methods, facilitate student learning, and improve overall educational outcomes, are commonly referred to as AI literacy (Carolus et al., 2023).

This study's theoretical framework is rooted in Ng et al.'s (2021b) AI Literacy TPACK Framework. Ng et al.'s (2021b) framework conceptualizes AI literacy across four dimensions—knowing and understanding AI, using and applying AI, evaluating and creating AI, and AI ethics—providing the theoretical basis for operationalizing AI Literacy. This study adopts the AI Literacy of Ng et al.'s (2021b) framework as the conceptual link between AI literacy and academic misconduct: students who are more AI-literate are hypothesized to more accurately recognize the boundaries of AI-assisted academic work, and therefore report both higher AI literacy and stronger academic misconduct against misconduct, net of institutional climate, achievement goal structures, and peer norms.

2.2.2. Academic Misconduct (AM)
Academic misconduct refers to behavior that violates the standards of honesty, fairness, and responsibility expected in academic work (Stephens, 2019). It has been operationalized in the literature as a multidimensional behavioral construct spanning collusion (e.g., unpermitted collaboration on individual assignments), misuse of resources (e.g., plagiarism, unauthorized use of materials), and fraud and contract cheating (Perry et al., 2026; Rettinger et al., 2024; Stephens et al., 2024). The emergence of GenAI tools has introduced new and complex forms of misconduct—including submitting AI-generated content as one's own work and using AI paraphrasing tools to disguise plagiarism—that blur the lines between legitimate AI assistance and academic fraud (Gruenhagen et al., 2024; Xia et al., 2024).

Scholars have approached academic misconduct from multiple perspectives. Stephens (2019) characterized the problem as epidemic in scale, noting that the majority of tertiary students globally report having cheated at least once during their studies, with this rate remaining remarkably stable across five decades and numerous countries. From a moral-psychological perspective, Stephens (2018) demonstrated that misconduct is driven not primarily by moral ignorance but by moral disengagement—students who cheat frequently possess accurate moral knowledge but use self-exculpatory rationalizations, such as blaming the instructor or normalizing the behavior among peers, to neutralize their ethical standards in specific situations. At a multi-level theoretical level, Perry et al. (2026) found that moral attitudes, institutional climate, and peer norms each contribute unique variance to misconduct, and that no single explanatory factor is sufficient on its own, confirming that academic misconduct is a multiply determined behavioral outcome.

Prevalence data underscore the scale of the problem. A nationwide New Zealand survey of 4,493 undergraduates found that approximately two-thirds (64.8%) reported at least one act of academic misconduct in the preceding year, with unauthorized collaboration being the most common behavior (Stephens et al., 2024). That survey incorporated the first items capturing AI-facilitated misconduct and found an initial 14.8% self-reported prevalence rate—likely a significant underestimate given that data collection concluded just two months before ChatGPT's public release (Stephens et al., 2024). Subsequent studies have confirmed that AI-facilitated misconduct is rapidly increasing and that many students do not recognize unauthorized AI assistance as a breach of academic integrity, highlighting the urgent need for research on the individual factors that shape these misconduct decisions (Gruenhagen et al., 2024; Lund et al., 2025).

Despite extensive research, significant gaps remain. Most existing studies predate the widespread availability of GenAI tools and therefore do not capture its most emergent forms of dishonest behavior (Lund et al., 2025; Perkins et al., 2024). Moreover, while moral attitudes and peer norms have been identified as predictors, the role of AI literacy—specifically whether higher AI literacy leads to more ethical decision-making around GenAI use—has not been empirically tested (Ng et al., 2021b; Stephens et al., 2024). Research from Chinese higher education contexts is particularly sparse, leaving open how cultural assessment norms and institutional factors moderate the AI literacy–misconduct relationship (Brown & Wang, 2016; Zhao et al., 2020). The most psychometrically validated instrument for measuring academic misconduct is the McCabe-ICAI Academic Misconduct Instrument (MIAMI; Rettinger et al., 2024), which assesses three behavioral factors—Collusion, Misuse of Resources, and Fraud/Contract Cheating—validated in large-scale multinational samples and supplemented with AI-specific misconduct items in recent adaptations (Perry et al., 2026; Stephens et al., 2024).

2.2.3. Institutional Climate (IC) Analytical Model
An institution with a strong climate of academic integrity has a culture that supports academic integrity. Academic integrity climate manifests through both formal policies, such as an honor code (Tatum, 2022), attitudes toward academic integrity, student knowledge and acceptance of academic integrity policies (Jordan, 2001; Young et al., 2018), as well as faculty knowledge and support of academic integrity policies (McCabe & Treviño, 1993). Students’ understanding and acceptance of an academic integrity policy is strongly and negatively associated with academic misconduct (Jordan, 2001), and students who attend schools with an honor code report more knowledge and a better understanding of the academic integrity policy and perceive less cheating overall than students at institutions without an honor code (McCabe et al., 2012).

Faculty play an integral role in creating and supporting the academic climate surrounding academic integrity (McCabe et al., 2012). Students report fewer academic misconduct behaviors when they believe they will face consequences and that the consequences will be significant (Miller et al., 2011). Students are less likely to cheat on homework when they are reminded of an institution-wide honor code and even less so when that reminder is combined with a warning about specific consequences of cheating (Bing et al., 2012). The academic integrity climate at schools with an honor code may explain why there is generally less self-reported cheating at honor code schools when compared to those without an honor code (McCabe & Treviño, 1993).

Campus climate also encompasses students’ beliefs about the prevalence of misconduct at their institution. Climate is distinct from peer norms, because climate refers to a nebulous belief about the behaviors of others, rather than specific, experience-based perceptions. Students who believe there is more misconduct are more likely to engage in it themselves (O’Rourke et al., 2010). The role of the academic integrity climate in determining students’ attitudes toward academic misconduct and misconduct behavior in combination with other factors, such as peer attitudes and behaviors, requires further investigation.

2.2.4. Motivational Achievement Goal Structures (MAGS) Analytical Model
Achievement goal theory is an empirically supported framework for examining motivation to engage in various tasks (Kaplan & Maehr, 2007; Urdan & Kaplan, 2020). The theory has been applied widely in many areas, including social psychology (e.g., Chu & Zhang, 2018), sports psychology (e.g., Cury et al., 2006), and education (e.g., Anderman & Wolters, 2006). The theory posits that when an individual is engaged with a task (e.g., an academic assignment), engagement is guided by the goal(s) that the individual is pursuing.

Achievement goals have been operationalized differently by various researchers, but the most widely accepted model includes four primary goals, including two types of mastery goals and two types of performance goals (Elliot & McGregor, 2001). A student who holds a mastery-approach goal when engaging with a task has the goal of truly mastering that task, whereas a student who holds a mastery-avoidance goal is focused on avoiding misunderstanding the task. In contrast, a student who holds a performance-approach goal is focused on demonstrating their ability at a task in comparison to others, whereas a student who holds a performance-avoidance goal is focused on not appearing “dumb” or incompetent when engaged with a task.

Achievement goals have been investigated with regard to how they are reflected in an educator’s instructional practices. Students’ perceptions of instructional practices from a goal theory perspective have been conceptualized as goal structures (Kaplan et al., 2002). Goal structures have been conceptualized in a variety of ways. Students are more likely to report perceiving a mastery goal structure in a class when they perceive the instructor as emphasizing mastery of content such as emphasizing the importance of effort, taking the time needed to deeply understand the class material, and giving permission to embrace making mistakes during learning as an important part of the learning process (Meece et al., 2006). Students are likely to report perceiving a performance goal structure when their instructor is perceived as emphasizing differences in students’ abilities (e.g., by publicly sharing exam scores), and making it obvious when students are not doing well in class. Some researchers have more specifically operationalized perceived performance goal structures in terms of extrinsic outcomes (i.e., an extrinsic goal structure); students report perceiving an extrinsic goal structure in a class if they perceive the instructor as emphasizing the importance of tests and grades, and spending a lot of time talking about tests and assessments (e.g., Won et al., 2020).

There is now a fairly extensive body of research that indicates that perceived goal structures are related in predictable ways to academic misconduct (Murdock & Anderman, 2006). This research indicates that students’ perceptions of an instructor’s focus on mastery of content and/or on performance and grades are related to academic cheating. In particular, research indicates that a perceived mastery goal structure is negatively correlated with academic cheating (e.g., Anderman & Midgley, 2004; Anderman et al., 1998; Krou et al., 2021; Murdock et al., 2004). Most research on the relations between achievement goal structures and academic integrity has focused on perceptions of an extrinsic goal structure (as opposed to a performance goal structure), with results indicating a greater likelihood of cheating occurring in classrooms perceived as emphasizing extrinsic outcomes (e.g., Anderman & Midgley, 2004; Anderman et al., 1998; Murdock et al., 2001).

2.2.5. Peer Norms (PN) Analytical Model
Peer norms and behaviors, as well as perceptions of peer behaviors and attitudes, are consistently correlated with academic misconduct. Students use examples of their peers’ behaviors to regulate their own actions, and when they observe their peers engaging in academic misconduct, they are more likely to engage in similar behavior (O’Rourke et al., 2010). Researchers have utilized several theories to explain the influence of peers on academic cheating including social learning theory and neutralization theory. Regardless of the theoretical explanations, students’ perceptions of their peers’ behaviors are one of the most influential factors related to academic cheating. In a recent meta-analysis, Zhao and colleagues (2022) demonstrated that the perception that one’s peers are cheating is one of the strongest predictors of academic cheating among students. Their analysis of the perceived peer cheating effect included 38 studies conducted over an 80-year span and included multiple countries and over 24,000 students. They found the effect size to be moderate, which is stronger than most other predictors of academic cheating. The perceived peer cheating effect is stronger in collectivist cultures, which may be due to how strongly students value their peers’ opinions, fear disagreement, experience pressure to conform, and demonstrate respect for authority.

Peer behavior can also have an exponential effect on cheating. In a retrospective study of students from three U.S. military academies, Carrell et al. (2008) established strong effects of peer influence on self-reported cheating. They used four-year cohorts rather than graduating year data to help reduce response bias among participants. Individuals’ probability of cheating significantly increased as the number of peer cheaters increased even when they corrected for selection bias, school, and time.

The role of peers is central to the study of academic integrity. When students believe that their peers would disapprove of academic misconduct, they are less likely to engage in cheating behaviors (McCabe et al., 2012). At some institutions, students are encouraged or even required to report instances of academic misconduct. Carrell et al. (2008) found that the military academies with the highest levels of peer reporting of academic misconduct had the lowest levels of self-reported cheating. Although their sample was derived from military academies which may have a peer culture that deviates radically from other types of colleges and universities, their findings are consistent with previous studies including McCabe and Treviño’s (1993) seminal work that established that peers’ behaviors, and the certainty of being reported by a peer for cheating, are strong predictors of academic misconduct.

2.2.6. Moral Attitudes (MA) Analytical Model
Students’ moral attitudes have long been theorized to be important factors in explaining academic misconduct. Students’ beliefs about the morality or ethical acceptability of cheating behavior and their “disengagement” or “neutralization” of responsibility for engagement in it have been two of the most widely studied types of moral attitudes. The former is theorized to oblige a course of action (e.g., if one believes cheating to be morally wrong, then one should not cheat), while the latter serves to reduce or negate culpability for engagement in behavior one believes to be morally wrong (e.g., Ajzen, 1991; Bandura, 1990; Stephens, 2017; Sykes & Matza, 1957).

Research on these moral attitudes has yielded a consistent pattern of results: Students’ beliefs about the immorality of cheating have been negatively associated with self-reported cheating (e.g., Anderman et al., 1998; McCabe et al., 2012; Stephens et al., 2007) while their tendency for moral disengagement a positive predictor of it (Farnese et al., 2011; Haines et al., 1986; O’Rourke et al., 2010; Stephens, 2018). Put another way, the more that students believe that cheating is morally unacceptable, the less likely they are to engage in academic misconduct (Stephens et al., 2007). However, the more that students morally disengage or have neutralizing attitudes about cheating, the more likely they are to engage in those behaviors (O’Rourke et al., 2010). These remain powerful indicators of students’ tendencies to cheat in college.

2.2.7. The Relationship Between AI Literacy and Academic Misconduct
The purpose of the current study is to examine the role of AI Literacy and four analytical models of academic misconduct based on the four theories in predicting academic misconduct among undergraduate college students. Importantly, each theory was examined separately and then all together. This was to ascertain the unique contribution of each analytical model, and then the predictive utility of all models being analyzed together simultaneously. The findings of the present study will help to further explain and hopefully reduce academic misconduct in higher education. Specifically, we hypothesized the following:

H1. AI Literacy has a significant negative effect on Academic Misconduct.
H2. Institutional Climate has a significant negative effect on Academic Misconduct.
H3. Motivational Achievement Goal Structures has a significant negative effect on Academic Misconduct.
H4. Peer Norms has a significant negative effect on Academic Misconduct.
H5. Moral Attitudes has a significant negative effect on Academic Misconduct.

2.3. Methodology

2.3.1 Sample
Hancock and Feng’s (2026) nmax calculator was applied to evaluate whether the sample size was sufficient for SEM. Assuming a minimum standardized path coefficient of γ = 0.20 and moderate multicollinearity among predictors (ρc = 0.30), the required minimum sample size is 213. Considering an anticipated longitudinal attrition rate of 20% across the three waves of data collection, consistent with prior three-wave AI literacy research (Liu et al., 2026), the final required sample size at T1 will be at least 267 participants to ensure a fully matched, valid sample of no fewer than 213 by T3.

2.3.2. Demographics
A total of X university students from various universities in China willingly participated in this study.

2.3.3. Measures
Student AI literacy will be measured using Zhao et al.'s (2022) scale, which includes 20 items assessing four dimensions of student AI literacy: Knowing and Understanding AI (KUAI), Applying AI (AAI), Evaluating AI Application (EAIA), and AI Ethics (AIE). To capture both permitted and prohibited forms of student AI use, this measure will be supplemented with the Academic AI Usage Inventory, retaining the Constructive usage and Irresponsible usage subscales and the Overreliant usage subscale as indicators of ethical versus unethical academic AI use (Elhami Athar, 2025). The AAUI is a 17-item self-report measure specifically developed for this study to assess three distinct profiles of academic AI usage: Constructive, Overreliant, and Irresponsible. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 ("Strongly Disagree") to 5 ("Strongly Agree") and load onto three subscales of Constructive (4 items), Overreliant (7 items), and Irresponsible (6 items).

Academic misconduct will be measured using the RAINZ-S scale (Stephens et al., 2024), developed from the nationwide Research on Academic Integrity in New Zealand (RAINZ) survey of 4,493 undergraduates. Participants will be asked to indicate how often, in the past 12 months, they engaged in a variety of academically dishonest behaviors, supplemented with AI-facilitated misconduct items (e.g., submitting AI-generated content as one's own work) consistent with the original instrument's later adaptations.

Academic integrity climate will assess via a measure adapted from previous literature (McCabe et al., 2012; Rettinger et al., 2024) that measured students’ knowledge of and attitudes toward the academic integrity policy at their institution. The original measure included Likert type response scales as well as dichotomous options (i.e. yes/no). We will utilize 22 self-report items that assessed college students’ perceptions of the academic integrity climate at their institution on a five-point Likert scale from (0) Strongly Disagree to (4) Strongly Agree. 

Perceptions of achievement goal structures were measured via items adapted from Midgley et al. (1998). Participants will rate their perceptions of instructors’ emphasis on mastery of content/mastery goal structure on a five-point Likert type scale from (0) not at all true to (4) very true (4 items; e.g., “My professors believe that it’s important to understand the work, not just to memorize it”). Using the same scale, they also will rate their perception of instructors’ emphasis on the importance of grades and test scores/extrinsic goal structure (3 items; e.g., “My professors emphasize the importance of test scores”). 

Two aspects of peer norms related to academic misconduct – attitudes and behaviors – will assess with measures adapted from McCabe and Treviño (1993) and Stephens et al. (2007). Perceptions of peer disapproval of misconduct will assess using a five-point Likert type scale from (0) Strongly Disagree to (4) Strongly Agree, with five items (e.g., “If I cheated on a test or exam, my friends would be really disappointed in me”). Perceptions of peers’ academic misconduct will assess by asking participants to indicate how often they had “observed or had direct knowledge of students at [their] school/college/university” engaging in nine behaviors using five items (
e.g., “Using unauthorized notes or sources during a test or exam”) with a five-point scale ranging from (0) never to (4) 11 or more times. Both aspects of peer norms (attitudes and behaviors) will enter into a two-factor CFA.

Students’ beliefs about the moral unacceptability of academic misconduct will assess with an adapted version of the McCabe et al. (2012) measure. Replacing the original rating of “seriousness,” our participants will ask to use a five-point scale to rate each behavior from (0) not at all morally/ ethically wrong to (4) completely morally/ethically wrong. The behaviors included eight items yolked to the MIAMI described above (e.g., “Getting questions or answers from someone who has already taken a test or exam”).

2.3.4. Data collection
Data for this study were collected using a three-wave longitudinal tracking design. A self-reported method was employed to survey students' AI literacy, academic misconduct, conceptions of assessment, and academic integrity at three time points. The research team implemented strategies such as securing college support, providing guidance, and offering appropriate incentives to effectively control attrition rates. To reduce common method bias associated with self-reported data, no identifying information will be collected across the three waves. Participants are provided with private spaces to complete the questionnaires, sufficient time for completion, and assurance that the data would be used solely for research purposes, thereby ensuring anonymity, confidentiality, and data validity. Sample attrition in this study was primarily due to invalid responses. Invalid questionnaires were excluded based on criteria such as substantial missing data, patterned responses, abnormal completion time, or illogical answers.

This study employs a time-lagged research design for data collection and conducted three waves of questionnaire surveys to reduce the potential influence of common method bias to some extent (Yu et al., 2021). The interval between each wave of data collection is two months. Each questionnaires will be distributed and collected on-site through an online survey platform. To ensure longitudinal matching across the three waves of data collection, participants will be assigned a unique anonymous identification number (ID), which was retained in T2 and T3 to ensure rigor and traceability.

During data processing, only questionnaires that are successfully matched across all three waves and fully complete are retained for analysis. Responses that could not be matched longitudinally or contained missing data were excluded. Based on the anticipated attrition rate, the final valid, fully matched sample retained across all three waves is expected to meet or exceed the required minimum sample size of 213 participants determined by the nmax approach. Finally, we use a three-wave time-lagged design to keep a temporal separation between predictors and outcomes (Podsakoff et al., 2012).

2.3.5. Data Analysis Procedure
The study employee a structural equation modelling (SEM) approach using RStudio (version 2026.07.1). Data are collected via an online survey and analyzed using RStudio software. RStudio will be used to analyze the data through a Structural Equation Modelling (SEM) approach, including the measurement model, structural model, and Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA). 

First, the variables are examined for outliers using z-values. To describe the data, we utilize the “psych” and “dplyr” packages (Revelle, 2026; Wickham et al., 2014). For the main analysis, which involves Structural Equation Modeling (SEM), we will employ the “lavaan” package and the “semPlot” package (Epskamp, 2015; Revelle, 2026). The Lavaan package will be used to define the measurement model and to conduct multigroup CFA and main effects SEM models, while the Modsem package will be used to test the multigroup interaction SEM model (Rosseel, 2012; Slupphaug et al., 2025). Model fits are evaluated with the robust comparative fit index (CFI), robust root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR).

CFA will be conducted with initial data collected from students to assess the structural or factorial validity of the Questionnaire. CFA is chosen because of its unique ability to explore the causal relationships among latent and observed variables in pre-defined, theory-derived models (Tavakol & Wetzel, 2020). CFA was conducted from the lavaan package in R (Rosseel, 2012). Before conducting CFA, data screening procedures will be conducted, including the examination of missing data, outliers, and univariate and multivariate normality.

SEM will be conducted through the following steps: model specification, model identification, model estimation, model testing, and model modification (Schumacker & Lomax, 2015). The model is specified and identified based on the conceptual framework outlined in the literature section. A bootstrapping method with robust maximum likelihood estimation will be used for model estimation. Robust maximum likelihood estimation with bootstrapped standard errors was selected, rather than PLS-SEM, because this combination maintains stable and unbiased parameter estimates even at moderate sample sizes, without the interpretive limitations associated with PLS-based composite modeling (Schumacker & Lomax, 2015). Initially, the measurement model estimations will confirm that all factor loadings are significant. Subsequently, model fit indices will be examined. We use Chi-square, CFI, TLI, RMSEA, and SRMR as fit indices. For a good model fit, CFI and TLI values should be 0.90 or above, while RMSEA and SRMR values should be below 0.08 (Schumacker & Lomax, 2015). The mediation analysis using structural equation modeling (SEM) with the lavaan package in RStudio is utilized to examine the direct, indirect, and total effects. After evaluating the path coefficients, the results will be reported.


3. Study 2: The Impact of AI Literacy on Academic Integrity: The Mediating Role of Conceptions of Assessment and Feedback Literacy

Abstract
As generative AI (GenAI) tools become increasingly embedded in higher education, concerns about AI-facilitated academic misconduct have escalated rapidly. This study addresses a significant gap by investigating the impact of AI Literacy (AIL) on Academic Integrity (AI) among Chinese university students, with Conceptions of Assessment (CoA) and Feedback Literacy (FL) as serial mediating variables. Grounded in Brown's (2011) Self-Regulation Model of Conceptions of Assessment and Rest's (1999) Four-Component Model of Moral Functioning, the proposed model posits that AI-literate students develop more adaptive beliefs about the genuine purpose of assessment, which in turn strengthen their commitment to academic integrity and ultimately reduce engagement in academic misconduct, including AI-facilitated forms. A quantitative cross-sectional survey design is employed, with data to be collected from Chinese university students and analyzed using RStudio. Findings are expected to illuminate the cognitive and moral pathways through which AI literacy shapes ethical academic behavior, providing empirically grounded guidance for universities seeking to foster cultures of integrity in the GenAI era.

3.1. Introduction
As an emerging field of computer science, artificial intelligence has profoundly impacted education (Celik, 2023; Montebello, 2018). Recently, there has been growing interest in exploring the interactions between people and AI and AI education systems. However, much of this interest has concentrated on AI's instructional and technical affordances, with comparatively little attention paid to the integrity- and assessment-related consequences of students' growing access to generative AI (GenAI) tools such as ChatGPT (Gruenhagen et al., 2024; Perkins et al., 2024). The rapid diffusion of GenAI into higher education has unsettled the traditional assumptions underlying assessment design, evaluation, and academic honesty (Gruenhagen et al., 2024; Xia et al., 2024). Indeed, more than a third of university students surveyed in Australia reported having used a chatbot for assistance with an assessment, and many of them did not regard this as a breach of academic integrity (Gruenhagen et al., 2024). This finding suggests that students' understanding of what counts as legitimate versus dishonest AI use—that is, their AI literacy—may be a critical yet underexamined antecedent of academic misconduct in the GenAI era.

The AI shift has generated challenges and opportunities for teaching and learning (Ding et al., 2024; Pan & Wang, 2025; Wijaya et al., 2024). The expansion of higher education and advanced technologies has intensified concerns about academic integrity (Shomotova et al., 2025). Although AI has great potential to facilitate learning, its integration into higher education settings generally requires students to navigate multilayered ethical decisions about legitimate and illegitimate AI use (Al-Abdullatif & Alsubaie, 2024; An et al., 2023). Academic misconduct—broadly defined as behavior that violates the standards of honesty and fairness expected in academic work—has long been recognized as a pervasive problem in higher education, with the majority of students in most countries admitting to having cheated at least once during their studies (Stephens, 2019; Stephens et al., 2024). A nationwide survey of 4,493 undergraduates in New Zealand found that approximately two-thirds reported engaging in at least one form of academic misconduct in the preceding year (Stephens et al., 2024). At the same time, assessment scholars have long argued that how students conceive of the purposes of assessment shapes their self-regulatory responses and, by extension, their academic behavior (Brown, 2011, 2021; Brown & Wang, 2016; Zhao et al., 2020).

First, although the potential for AI-enhanced learning has grown rapidly, there is a notable lack of both theoretical and empirical research on AI Literacy in the context of academic integrity in higher education (Lu et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2024). While AI literacy has been theorized as encompassing an explicit ethics dimension (Long & Magerko, 2020; Ng et al., 2021b), empirical research has rarely tested whether this ethical-evaluative competence actually translates into more honest academic conduct when students use AI tools for their academic work (Lund et al., 2025; Perkins et al., 2024). Few studies have systematically examined how AI literacy shapes students' assessment beliefs and moral attitudes toward academic misconduct (Nabhan & Habók, 2026).

Second, although interest in AI Literacy is increasing, the precise relationship between AI literacy and academic integrity remains largely unexplored. While conceptions of assessment have been extensively studied as antecedents of self-regulated learning and academic performance (Brown, 2011, 2021; Brown & Wang, 2016), few studies have integrated these two well-established but largely separate literatures with the emerging literature on AI literacy into a single research framework capable of explaining the conceptions of assessment and feedback literacy pathways through which AI literacy shapes academic integrity behavior (Wu et al., 2024).

Although China has actively integrated AI tools into its higher education landscape, systematic empirical research on how Chinese university students' AI literacy relates to their academic integrity remains scarce (A. Zhao et al., 2020). Most existing studies have focused on students' technical AI competencies or pedagogical outcomes, leaving a notable gap regarding how AI literacy shapes assessment beliefs and integrity behaviors in a population where Confucian-heritage assessment norms and high-stakes institutional cultures further complicate the dynamics of academic misconduct (Brown & Wang, 2016; Zhao et al., 2020).

To address these identified research gaps, this study investigates the impact of AI Literacy (Independent Variable) on Academic Integrity (Dependent Variable) through the mediating effects of Conceptions of Assessment and Feedback Literacy (Mediator Variables) in a serial mediation research model. Based on Brown's (2011) Self-Regulation Model of Conceptions of Assessment, this study aims to bridge these gaps by investigating the interrelationships between these constructs among Chinese university students.

3.2. Literature Review and Hypothesis

3.2.1. AI Literacy (AIL)
The increasing incorporation of AI into teaching and learning environments has sparked interest in AI literacy (Al-Abdullatif & Alsubaie, 2024; B. Wang et al., 2023). One approach to AI education is to improve AI literacy (Casal-Otero et al., 2023; Chung et al., 2025). Nevertheless, the effective classroom integration of AI demands that student be AI-literate, encompassing understanding, utilization, monitoring, and critical reflection on AI applications in teaching (Ding et al., 2024).

AI literacy is a newly emerging concept that seeks to define, teach, and evaluate knowledge and skills related to AI. AI literacy refers to an individual’s ability to comprehend, utilize, monitor, and critically evaluate AI applications, even without the ability to develop AI models. Furthermore, it encompasses the ability to observe, ethically use, and critically evaluate AI applications and AI-generated content and their implications without necessarily having programming knowledge. AI literacy encompasses the competencies that enable individuals to critically evaluate AI technologies and leverage them for effective communication and collaboration (Long & Magerko, 2020; Wu et al., 2024). This concept entails the design and implementation of AI learning activities, tools, applications, and pedagogical models (Wu et al., 2024; Yim & Su, 2024).

The term “AI literacy” was first coined by Burgsteiner et al. (2016) and Kandlhofer et al. (2016), who described the competencies required to understand the basic knowledge and concepts of AI and to use AI technologies. In addition, Long and Magerko (2020) defined it as a set of competencies that enables individuals to critically evaluate, communicate, and collaborate effectively with AI and use it as a tool, online, at home, and in the workplace. Furthermore, Ng et al. (2021b, 2021a) added AI to every student’s AI literacy in study settings and everyday life and proposed it as a fundamental skill for everyone, not just computer scientists. He incorporated the model of Bloom’s Taxonomy, Technological Pedagogical Content Knowledge (TPACK), and AI concepts, practices, and perspectives into the instructional design of AI literacy education (Ng et al., 2021b, 2021a). Carolus et al. (2023) stated that AI literacy covers “competencies needed to interact with AI technology in a self-determined and rational manner.” Pinski and Benlian (2023) provided a definition of General AI literacy, describing it ashumans “socio-technical competence consisting of knowledge regarding human and AI actors in human-AI interaction, knowledge of the AI process steps, that is, input, processing, and output, and experience in AI interaction.” 

Recently, several studies have developed AI literacy frameworks for education (Kong et al., 2023; Ng et al., 2021b; Wong et al., 2020). AI literacy does not require detailed technical expertise in AI applications. However, it spans four dimensions: understanding and comprehension of AI, practical application of AI, assessment of AI applications, and ethical considerations of AI (L. Zhao et al., 2022).

Long and Magerko (2020) provided a conceptual framework of AI literacy composed of five overarching themes derived from the literature (Long & Magerko, 2020).Wang et al. (2023) proposed a theoretical framework to conceptualize AI Literacy, comprising four constructs: Usage, defined as the ability to exploit AI technology to proficiently perform tasks; Awareness, the ability to identify and comprehend AI systems during interactions; Evaluation, the capacity to critically analyze and assess AI applications and their outcomes; and Ethics, which involves recognizing responsibilities and risks associated with using AI systems. 

Despite the growing body of research on AI Literacy, significant gaps remain (Lu et al., 2024). Most studies have relied on qualitative methodologies or small-scale quantitative analyses (Ng et al., 2021b, 2023; Schiavo et al., 2024; Su et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2024), limiting our understanding of the broader implementation and effectiveness of AI education (Ng et al., 2021b, 2023; Schiavo et al., 2024; Su et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2024). A significant majority (75 %) of the papers pertaining to AI literacy have focused on students (Al-Abdullatif, 2024; Almatrafi et al., 2024; Ng et al., 2023), while few have focused on student (An et al., 2023; Liang et al., 2023).

Therefore, student must understand what AI is and what it can do inside and outside the classroom, and engage with AI technologies in a self-determined manner. These sets of skills, including understanding, utilizing, and integrating various AI technologies to enhance teaching methods, facilitate student learning, and improve overall educational outcomes, are commonly referred to as AI literacy (Carolus et al., 2023).

3.2.2. Academic Integrity (AI)
Academic integrity refers to students' commitment to fundamental values of honesty, trust, fairness, respect, and responsibility in their academic work (Stephens, 2019). It is most usefully understood not as the simple absence of misconduct but as a positive motivational orientation—a set of moral beliefs and self-regulatory dispositions that determine whether a student chooses to engage authentically with academic demands, including decisions about legitimate versus unauthorized AI use (Perry et al., 2026; Stephens, 2018).

Scholars have approached academic integrity from complementary theoretical perspectives. Rest's (1999) Four-Component Model of Moral Functioning is the most influential framework, decomposing ethical academic action into four components: moral sensitivity (recognizing that a situation has a moral dimension), moral judgment (determining the right course of action), moral motivation (prioritizing integrity values over competing self-interests such as grade pressure), and moral character (the self-regulatory capacity to act honestly despite obstacles). Critically, the model predicts that knowing the right thing to do is necessary but not sufficient to produce ethical behavior. Stephens (2018) confirmed this empirically, finding that moral judgment had no significant direct effect on academic misconduct; instead, the effect operated entirely through moral disengagement—self-exculpatory rationalizations that allow students to cheat while privately acknowledging that doing so is wrong. Perry et al. (2026) replicated this pattern across 2,329 North American undergraduates, finding that moral attitudes—combining moral unacceptability judgments with resistance to moral disengagement—were the most robust predictors of academic misconduct across five competing theoretical models.

Significant gaps remain. The relationship between AI literacy and academic integrity has not been empirically examined; specifically, whether AI literacy's ethics dimension (Long & Magerko, 2020; Ng et al., 2021b) functions analogously to moral judgment in Rest's (1999) model—necessary but insufficient on its own to prevent dishonest behavior. Additionally, no study has tested whether conceptions of assessment predict academic integrity values, despite the theoretical link between ego-protective assessment beliefs and moral disengagement (Brown, 2021; Stephens, 2018). Existing moral attitude measures have also not been widely adapted for AI-specific misconduct scenarios, limiting their applicability to the GenAI context (Stephens et al., 2024). The Moral Attitudes scale validated by Perry et al. (2026) and Stephens et al. (2024) provides the most psychometrically rigorous operationalization of academic integrity currently available, combining a Moral Unacceptability subscale (5 items) and a Moral Disengagement subscale (7 items, reverse-scored). In the proposed model, academic integrity functions as the second mediating variable, positioned downstream of conceptions of assessment, through which adaptive assessment beliefs translate into reduced academic misconduct.

3.2.3. Conceptions of Assessment (CoA)
Conceptions of assessment (CoA) refer to the beliefs students hold about the purposes, nature, and consequences of educational evaluation practices they encounter during their studies (Brown, 2011, 2021). These beliefs function as cognitive-motivational filters that mediate between the objective features of an assessment task and a student's behavioral response: the same assignment can elicit very different reactions depending on whether the student views it as a genuine opportunity to develop and demonstrate authentic learning or as an arbitrary, irrelevant, or threatening hurdle to be circumvented by any available means (Brown, 2021; Brown & Wang, 2016).

Scholars have approached conceptions of assessment from multiple perspectives. Brown's (2011, 2021) Self-Regulation Model draws on Boekaerts' (1996) dual-pathway framework to identify two broad self-regulatory orientations: an adaptive growth pathway, in which assessment is treated as a resource for learning improvement, and a maladaptive ego-protective pathway, in which assessment is viewed as irrelevant or externally imposed to shield self-worth from the threat of poor performance. Students who endorse growth-oriented conceptions exhibit greater metacognitive engagement, higher self-efficacy, and better academic performance, while those who endorse ego-protective conceptions are more prone to disengagement and avoidance (Brown, 2021; Zhao et al., 2020). A systematic review of 22 higher education studies spanning 11 jurisdictions confirmed this pattern consistently (Brown, 2021). In the Chinese higher education context, Brown and Wang (2016) developed the culturally validated Chinese Student Conceptions of Assessment (C-SCoA) inventory, identifying eight culturally grounded belief factors—including Societal Uses, Class Benefits, Accuracy, Teacher Use, Family Effects, and Competition—and demonstrating that jurisdictional and institutional factors, rather than shared cultural values alone, explained the most variation in how students conceptualized the purpose of assessment.

Despite strong evidence linking assessment conceptions to effort and motivation outcomes, significant gaps remain. First, their relationship to integrity-specific behavior has not been empirically tested; specifically, whether maladaptive assessment conceptions supply the motivational premise from which students construct moral disengagement reasoning about unauthorized AI use (Brown, 2022). Second, no study has examined whether AI literacy shapes how students conceive of assessment in an AI-enriched environment, or whether such understandings promote more adaptive beliefs. Third, the role of assessment conceptions as a mediating variable between individual AI-related competencies and ethical academic behavior remains entirely untested.

Brown's (2011, 2021) SCoA inventory provides the most extensively validated framework for operationalizing this construct, distinguishing four superordinate beliefs: assessment improves teaching and learning (adaptive), assessment has socio-emotional benefits, assessment evaluates schools and individuals (external accountability), and assessment is irrelevant (maladaptive). In the proposed model, conceptions of assessment function as the first mediating variable: AI-literate students who better understand what assessment genuinely evaluates may develop more adaptive beliefs that reduce their motivation to engage in academic misconduct (Brown & Wang, 2016; Ng et al., 2021b).

3.2.4. Feedback Literacy (FL)
Feedback literacy is widely regarded as a crucial competence that enables students to benefit from feedback practices, encompassing the knowledge, skills, and dispositions required to interpret and use feedback effectively (e.g., Carless & Boud, 2018). Yu et al. (2022) conceptualised feedback literacy as students’ capacities to appreciate, evaluate, and utilise feedback while managing their emotions during the process. Their framework includes five dimensions: appreciating feedback (valuing its importance), acknowledging different feedback sources (recognising diverse providers), making judgments (evaluating feedback quality and relevance), managing affect (regulating emotional responses), and taking action (applying feedback for improvement).

Initially outlined by Sutton (2012) and further developed by Carless and Boud (2018), the concept of feedback literacy encompasses students’ dispositions and abilities to interpret and utilize feedback to advance their learning. It frames students as proactive learners, varying in their capacity and willingness to use feedback for academic improvement. Feedback literacy can be viewed through two lenses: the sociocultural perspective, which emphasizes development through academic socialization, and the skills perspective, which sees it as a psychological trait and trainable competence (Nieminen & Carless, 2023; Little et al., 2024). Our study adopted the skills perspective, as this positions feedback literacy as a construct amenable to psychometric measurement and, thus, quantitative analysis (Winstone et al., 2019; Woitt et al., 2025).

As a construct coined to capture how students differentially utilize and profit from feedback, feedback literacy can contribute to opening the lamented black box of feedback processing (Winstone & Nash, 2023). Core to the concept of feedback literacy is the assumption that feedback-literate students are able and willing to profit more deeply from feedback than their less feedback-literate peers; as Carless and Boud (2018) stated: “One of the main barriers to effective feedback is generally low levels of student feedback literacy” (p. 2). 

Although many researchers (e.g., Carless & Boud, 2018; Tsai, 2022; Winstone et al., 2021) have emphasized the importance of feedback literacy, its role has yet to be empirically quantified. That is, the extent to which feedback literacy in fact moderates student perceptions is still unclear. Recent efforts to develop feedback literacy scales (Dawson et al., 2024; Song, 2022; Weidlich et al., 2025; Woitt et al., 2025; Yildiz, 2022; Zhan, 2022) now make quantitative investigation possible, particularly as a moderator of feedback perceptions. However, previous studies have not explored the mechanisms by which feedback literacy is utilized in technological environments such as human intelligence (Cui et al., 2025).

3.2.5. AI Literacy TPACK Framework and Brown's Self-Regulation Model
This study's theory is rooted in two fundamental frameworks that jointly explain the cognitive pathways linking AI literacy to academic misconduct: Brown's (2011, 2021) Self-Regulation Model of Conceptions of Assessment (SCoA Model) and Ng et al.'s (2021b) AI Literacy TPACK Framework. Together, these provide the theoretical architecture for the proposed serial mediation model.

The SCoA Model has been used to explain how students' assessment beliefs shape their self-regulatory responses to evaluative demands across diverse cultural and institutional contexts (Brown, 2011, 2021; Brown & Wang, 2016). In contrast, the AI Literacy TPACK Framework conceptualizes AI literacy across four competency dimensions—knowing and understanding AI, using and applying AI, evaluating and creating AI, and AI ethics (Ng et al., 2021b)—and has been widely adopted as the theoretical basis for operationalizing student AI literacy in higher education research (Su & Yang, 2024). The SCoA Model accounts for the motivational and cognitive factors pertaining to assessment from students' perspectives, whereas the AI Literacy TPACK Framework accounts for the competency dimensions—particularly the ethics dimension—through which students recognize and evaluate the boundaries of legitimate AI use. The SCoA Model has been widely validated in higher education research because it explains substantial variance in students' academic self-regulation across more than 22 studies and 11 jurisdictions (Brown, 2021), and Ng et al.'s (2021b) framework provides the conceptual basis for Zhao et al.'s (2022) AI literacy scale, ensuring theoretical-operational consistency in the present study.

The SCoA Model emphasizes two key pathways: (1) the growth pathway (adaptive Conceptions of Assessment), which in the present study corresponds to the first mediating mechanism that channels AI literacy's influence toward honest academic engagement; and (2) the ego-protective pathway (maladaptive Conceptions of Assessment), which corresponds to the mechanism through which students rationalize dishonest AI use when they view assessment as irrelevant or externally imposed (Brown, 2021). The AI Literacy TPACK Framework emphasizes the ethics dimension in particular—students' capacity to recognize and evaluate the ethical implications of AI use—which corresponds to the competency-based antecedent of Academic Integrity and Feedback Literacy in the present model (Ng et al., 2021b). This study adopted the SCoA Model as the theoretical basis for the first mediating pathway (AI Literacy → Conceptions of Assessment → Academic Integrity) and the AI Literacy TPACK Framework as the theoretical basis for conceptualizing AI Literacy as the study's independent variable. The SCoA Model provides the motivational rationale for why AI literacy should shape how students interpret the meaning and purpose of their assessments, while the AI Literacy TPACK Framework explains why students' competency in evaluating and ethically applying AI shapes their subsequent assessment beliefs and feedback engagement. According to these two integrated frameworks, AI Literacy has a significant impact on Academic Integrity through the sequential mediation of Conceptions of Assessment and Feedback Literacy (Brown, 2011, 2021; Ng et al., 2021b).

3.2.6. The Relationship Between AI Literacy and Academic Integrity: The Mediating Role of Conceptions of Assessment and Feedback Literacy
First, AI literacy is a crucial factor affecting students' academic integrity in AI-enriched environments (Gruenhagen et al., 2024; Lund et al., 2025). Recent studies have highlighted the significant role of AI literacy in shaping responsible AI use in educational settings (Long & Magerko, 2020; Ng et al., 2021b; Schiavo et al., 2024). Students with higher AI literacy possess a more accurate understanding of the capabilities and ethical boundaries of GenAI tools, making them better equipped to distinguish legitimate AI assistance from unauthorized substitution of their own academic work (Lund et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2023). Gruenhagen et al. (2024) found that students who did not understand the implications of AI use for assessment were substantially more likely to report using AI tools dishonestly. This reflects the significant influence of AI literacy levels on academic misconduct engagement (Perry et al., 2026; Stephens et al., 2024).

Second, AI literacy significantly shapes students' conceptions of assessment and their academic integrity. Higher levels of AI literacy facilitate a greater understanding of what assessment genuinely measures—authentic knowledge and competence rather than AI-assisted performance—which in turn promotes improvement-oriented rather than irrelevance-oriented conceptions of assessment (Brown, 2021; Ng et al., 2021b; Wang et al., 2023). Similarly, AI literacy is expected to promote academic integrity directly (Ng et al., 2021b; Perry et al., 2026). 

Third, conceptions of assessment directly influence academic integrity. Students who view assessment as a meaningful improvement opportunity have greater intrinsic motivation to engage honestly with academic work and less motivational basis for constructing disengagement rationalizations about AI use (Brown, 2021; Rest, 1999). Previous research confirms that conceptions of assessment and feedback literacy play complementary and sequential mediating roles between individual-difference antecedents and academic misconduct (Brown, 2021; Rest, 1999; Stephens, 2018). Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H1. AI Literacy has a significant positive effect on Academic Integrity.
H2. AI Literacy has a significant positive effect on Conceptions of Assessment.
H3. AI Literacy has a significant positive effect on Feedback Literacy.
H4. Conceptions of Assessment has a significant positive effect on Academic Integrity.
H5. Feedback Literacy has a significant negative effect on Academic Integrity.
H6. Conceptions of Assessment has a significant positive effect on Feedback Literacy.
H7. Conceptions of Assessment mediates the effect of AI Literacy on Feedback Literacy.
H8. Conceptions of Assessment mediates the effect of AI Literacy on Academic Misconduct.
H9. Feedback Literacy mediates the effect of AI Literacy on Academic Integrity.

Based on Brown's (2011) Self-Regulation Model of Conceptions of Assessment and Rest's (1999) Four-Component Model of Moral Functioning, this study introduces the independent variable (AI Literacy), mediating variables (Conceptions of Assessment and Academic Integrity), and dependent variable (Academic Misconduct) to propose a serial mediation research model. 3.3. Methodology

3.3.1 Sample
Hancock and Feng’s (2026) nmax calculator was applied to evaluate whether the sample size was sufficient for SEM. Assuming a minimum standardized path coefficient of γ = 0.20 and moderate multicollinearity among predictors (ρc = 0.30), the required minimum sample size is 213. Considering an anticipated longitudinal attrition rate of 20% across the three waves of data collection, consistent with prior three-wave AI literacy research (Liu et al., 2026), the final required sample size at T1 will be at least 267 participants to ensure a fully matched, valid sample of no fewer than 213 by T3.

3.3.2. Demographics
A total of X university students from various universities in China willingly participated in this study.

3.3.3. Measures
Student AI literacy will be measured using Zhao et al.'s (2022) scale, which includes 20 items assessing four dimensions of student AI literacy: Knowing and Understanding AI (KUAI), Applying AI (AAI), Evaluating AI Application (EAIA), and AI Ethics (AIE). To capture both permitted and prohibited forms of student AI use, this measure will be supplemented with the Academic AI Usage Inventory, retaining the Constructive usage and Irresponsible usage subscales and the Overreliant usage subscale as indicators of ethical versus unethical academic AI use (Elhami Athar, 2025). The AAUI is a 17-item self-report measure specifically developed for this study to assess three distinct profiles of academic AI usage: Constructive, Overreliant, and Irresponsible. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 ("Strongly Disagree") to 5 ("Strongly Agree") and load onto three subscales of Constructive (4 items), Overreliant (7 items), and Irresponsible (6 items).

Academic integrity will be measured using the academic integrity perceptions scale developed by Li, Brown and Hawe (2023) in the context of Chinese university students' Integrated Quality Assessment. Items were adapted to include AI-specific academic scenarios reflecting current GenAI use contexts. Higher composite scores indicate stronger academic integrity perceptions.

To measure Conceptions of Assessment, this study adopted the Chinese Student Conceptions of Assessment (C-SCoA) inventory developed and validated by Brown and Wang (2025) specifically for Chinese university students. The inventory comprises 33 items assessing eight culturally grounded assessment belief factors using a six-point positively packed agreement scale (1 = Strongly Disagree to 6 = Strongly Agree). The improvement-oriented subscales (Class Benefits, Teacher Use, and Accuracy) serve as the primary indicators of adaptive conceptions of assessment in the structural equation model.

To measure Feedback Literacy, this study adopted the college student Generative AI Feedback Literacy Scale (GenAI-SFLS) developed by Cui, Meng, Qian and Tang (2025). The original instrument comprises four dimensions and nine factors: a cognitive dimension (feedback knowledge and appreciation), a behavioral dimension (feedback prompting skills, feedback judgment, and enaction of feedback), an affective dimension (emotional recognition, readiness to engage, and commitment to change), and an ethical dimension (feedback ethics). 

3.3.4. Data collection
Data for this study were collected using a three-wave longitudinal tracking design. A self-reported method was employed to survey students' AI literacy, academic misconduct, conceptions of assessment, and academic integrity at three time points. The research team implemented strategies such as securing college support, providing guidance, and offering appropriate incentives to effectively control attrition rates. To reduce common method bias associated with self-reported data, no identifying information will be collected across the three waves. Participants are provided with private spaces to complete the questionnaires, sufficient time for completion, and assurance that the data would be used solely for research purposes, thereby ensuring anonymity, confidentiality, and data validity. Sample attrition in this study was primarily due to invalid responses. Invalid questionnaires were excluded based on criteria such as substantial missing data, patterned responses, abnormal completion time, or illogical answers.

This study employs a time-lagged research design for data collection and conducted three waves of questionnaire surveys to reduce the potential influence of common method bias to some extent (Yu et al., 2021). The interval between each wave of data collection is two months. Each questionnaires will be distributed and collected on-site through an online survey platform. To ensure longitudinal matching across the three waves of data collection, participants will be assigned a unique anonymous identification number (ID), which was retained in T2 and T3 to ensure rigor and traceability.

During data processing, only questionnaires that are successfully matched across all three waves and fully complete are retained for analysis. Responses that could not be matched longitudinally or contained missing data were excluded. Based on the anticipated attrition rate, the final valid, fully matched sample retained across all three waves is expected to meet or exceed the required minimum sample size of 213 participants determined by the nmax approach. Finally, we use a three-wave time-lagged design to keep a temporal separation between predictors and outcomes (Podsakoff et al., 2012).

3.3.5. Data Analysis Procedure
The study employee a structural equation modelling (SEM) approach using RStudio (version 2026.07.1). Data are collected via an online survey and analyzed using RStudio software. RStudio will be used to analyze the data through a Structural Equation Modelling (SEM) approach, including the measurement model, structural model, and Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA). 

First, the variables are examined for outliers using z-values. To describe the data, we utilize the “psych” and “dplyr” packages (Revelle, 2026; Wickham et al., 2014). For the main analysis, which involves Structural Equation Modeling (SEM), we will employ the “lavaan” package and the “semPlot” package (Epskamp, 2015; Revelle, 2026). The Lavaan package will be used to define the measurement model and to conduct multigroup CFA and main effects SEM models, while the Modsem package will be used to test the multigroup interaction SEM model (Rosseel, 2012; Slupphaug et al., 2025). Model fits are evaluated with the robust comparative fit index (CFI), robust root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR).

CFA will be conducted with initial data collected from students to assess the structural or factorial validity of the Questionnaire. CFA is chosen because of its unique ability to explore the causal relationships among latent and observed variables in pre-defined, theory-derived models (Tavakol & Wetzel, 2020). CFA was conducted from the lavaan package in R (Rosseel, 2012). Before conducting CFA, data screening procedures will be conducted, including the examination of missing data, outliers, and univariate and multivariate normality.

SEM will be conducted through the following steps: model specification, model identification, model estimation, model testing, and model modification (Schumacker & Lomax, 2015). The model is specified and identified based on the conceptual framework outlined in the literature section. A bootstrapping method with robust maximum likelihood estimation will be used for model estimation. Robust maximum likelihood estimation with bootstrapped standard errors was selected, rather than PLS-SEM, because this combination maintains stable and unbiased parameter estimates even at moderate sample sizes, without the interpretive limitations associated with PLS-based composite modeling (Schumacker & Lomax, 2015). Initially, the measurement model estimations will confirm that all factor loadings are significant. Subsequently, model fit indices will be examined. We use Chi-square, CFI, TLI, RMSEA, and SRMR as fit indices. For a good model fit, CFI and TLI values should be 0.90 or above, while RMSEA and SRMR values should be below 0.08 (Schumacker & Lomax, 2015). The mediation analysis using structural equation modeling (SEM) with the lavaan package in RStudio is utilized to examine the direct, indirect, and total effects. After evaluating the path coefficients, the results will be reported.

4. Study 3: The Impact of AI Literacy on Academic Misconduct: The Mediating Role of Academic Motivation and Self-Regulation Learning
Abstract

4.1. Introduction
Artificial intelligence has profoundly impacted education (Celik, 2023; Montebello, 2018). Recently, there has been growing interest in exploring the interactions between people and AI and AI education systems. However, much of this interest has concentrated on AI's instructional and technical affordances, with comparatively little attention paid to the integrity-related consequences of students' growing access to generative AI (GenAI) tools such as ChatGPT (Gruenhagen et al., 2024; Perkins et al., 2024). The rapid diffusion of GenAI into higher education has unsettled the traditional assumptions underlying assessment design, evaluation, and academic honesty (Gruenhagen et al., 2024; Xia et al., 2024). Indeed, more than a third of university students surveyed in Australia reported having used a chatbot for assistance with an assessment, and many of them did not regard this as a breach of academic integrity (Gruenhagen et al., 2024). This finding suggests that students' understanding of what counts as legitimate versus dishonest AI use—that is, their AI literacy—may be a critical yet underexamined antecedent of academic misconduct in the GenAI era.

The AI shift has generated challenges and opportunities for teaching and learning (Ding et al., 2024; Pan & Wang, 2025; Wijaya et al., 2024). The expansion of higher education and advanced technologies has intensified concerns about academic dishonesty (Shomotova et al., 2025). Although AI has great potential to facilitate learning, its integration into higher education settings generally requires students to navigate multilayered ethical decisions about legitimate and illegitimate AI use (Al-Abdullatif & Alsubaie, 2024; An et al., 2023). Academic misconduct—broadly defined as behavior that violates the standards of honesty and fairness expected in academic work—has long been recognized as a pervasive problem in higher education, with the majority of students in most countries admitting to having cheated at least once during their studies (Stephens, 2019; Stephens et al., 2024). A nationwide survey of 4,493 undergraduates in New Zealand found that approximately two-thirds reported engaging in at least one form of academic misconduct in the preceding year, and that students' moral attitudes toward cheating were significantly associated with their behavior (Stephens et al., 2024). 




First, although the potential for AI-enhanced learning has grown rapidly, there is a notable lack of both theoretical and empirical research on AI Literacy in the context of academic integrity in higher education (Lu et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2024). While AI literacy has been theorized as encompassing an explicit ethics dimension (Long & Magerko, 2020; Ng et al., 2021b), empirical research has rarely tested whether this ethical-evaluative competence actually translates into more honest academic conduct when students use AI tools for their academic work (Lund et al., 2025; Perkins et al., 2024). Few studies have systematically examined how the ethics dimension of AI literacy shapes students' academic motivation and self-regulation learning (Nabhan & Habók, 2026).

Second, although interest in AI Literacy is increasing, the precise relationship between AI literacy and academic misconduct remains largely unexplored. While academic motivation and self-regulation learning has been studied as a predictor of academic misconduct (Perry et al., 2026; Stephens, 2018; Stephens et al., 2024), few studies have integrated these two well-established but largely separate literatures with the emerging literature on AI literacy into a single research framework capable of explaining the academic motivation and self-regulation learning pathways through which AI literacy shapes academic integrity (Wu et al., 2024).

Although China has actively integrated AI tools into its higher education landscape, systematic empirical research on how Chinese university students' AI literacy relates to their academic integrity remains scarce (A. Zhao et al., 2020). Most existing studies have focused on students' technical AI competencies or pedagogical outcomes, leaving a notable gap regarding how AI literacy shapes academic motivation and self-regulation learning in a population where Confucian-heritage assessment norms and high-stakes institutional cultures further complicate the dynamics of academic misconduct (Brown & Wang, 2016; Zhao et al., 2020).

Derived from autonomous learning or learner autonomy, self-regulated learning (SRL) has garnered extensive attention among educational scholars. It enables people to decide what, when, and how to conduct learning, as well as the necessity to resort to others for support, resulting in lifelong learning (Huang & Derakhshan, 2025). Unfortunately, relatively few studies have been conducted in the learning setting to understand how learners self-regulate their learning with AI (Huang & Derakhshan, 2025). While SRL have been extensively studied within the context of AI education, few studies have investigated their intersection with AI literacy (K. Wang et al., 2025). The intersection of self-regulated learning, and AI literacy has also received limited attention (Bećirović et al., 2026). To date, the interrelationships among AI literacy, academic misconduct, academic motivation, and self-regulated learning have not been systematically examined (Shi et al., 2025).

Artiﬁcial intelligence literacy has become increasingly critical as AI integrates into high education, yet understanding of the factors inﬂuencing students’ AI literacy and its relationship with self-regulated learning remains limited (D. Wang et al., 2026). Recent studies have also examined the potential synergy between SRL theory and AI (Jin et al., 2023; Molenaar, 2022; Molenaar et al., 2023). However, the current body of research on the relationship between AI and SRL has primarily focused on how AI supports and enhances SRL. For example, Lim et al. (2023) explored the application of rule-based AI systems for real-time measurement and support of students’ SRL. Their findings revealed that personalized scaffolding, facilitated by real-time analysis, significantly enhances students’ SRL. Similarly, several studies have investigated the role of SRLSs in AIED. Despite these contributions, a significant gap remains in the literature regarding how SRLSs specifically influence AI literacy. Furthermore, although SRL has been extensively applied in educational contexts to enhance motivation and engagement in learning, and SRL has been shown to improve students’ AI literacy competencies (Anthonysamy et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2021; Z. Zhang et al., 2023), few studies have investigated the intersection of SRL, and AI literacy.

To address these identified research gaps, this study investigates the impact of AI Literacy (Independent Variable) on Academic Misconduct (Dependent Variable) through the mediating effects of Academic Motivation and Self-Regulation Learning (Mediator Variables) in a serial mediation research model. This study aims to bridge these gaps by investigating the interrelationships between these constructs among Chinese university students.


4.2. Literature Review and Hypothesis

4.2.1. AI Literacy (AIL)
The increasing incorporation of AI into teaching and learning environments has sparked interest in AI literacy (Al-Abdullatif & Alsubaie, 2024; B. Wang et al., 2023). One approach to AI education is to improve AI literacy (Casal-Otero et al., 2023; Chung et al., 2025). Nevertheless, the effective classroom integration of AI demands that student be AI-literate, encompassing understanding, utilization, monitoring, and critical reflection on AI applications in teaching (Ding et al., 2024).

AI literacy is a newly emerging concept that seeks to define, teach, and evaluate knowledge and skills related to AI. AI literacy refers to an individual’s ability to comprehend, utilize, monitor, and critically evaluate AI applications, even without the ability to develop AI models. Furthermore, it encompasses the ability to observe, ethically use, and critically evaluate AI applications and AI-generated content and their implications without necessarily having programming knowledge. AI literacy encompasses the competencies that enable individuals to critically evaluate AI technologies and leverage them for effective communication and collaboration (Long & Magerko, 2020; Wu et al., 2024). This concept entails the design and implementation of AI learning activities, tools, applications, and pedagogical models (Wu et al., 2024; Yim & Su, 2024).

The term “AI literacy” was first coined by Burgsteiner et al. (2016) and Kandlhofer et al. (2016), who described the competencies required to understand the basic knowledge and concepts of AI and to use AI technologies. In addition, Long and Magerko (2020) defined it as a set of competencies that enables individuals to critically evaluate, communicate, and collaborate effectively with AI and use it as a tool, online, at home, and in the workplace. Furthermore, Ng et al. (2021b, 2021a) added AI to every student’s AI literacy in work settings and everyday life and proposed it as a fundamental skill for everyone, not just computer scientists. He incorporated the model of Bloom’s Taxonomy, Technological Pedagogical Content Knowledge (TPACK), and AI concepts, practices, and perspectives into the instructional design of AI literacy education (Ng et al., 2021b, 2021a).

Carolus et al. (2023) stated that AI literacy covers “competencies needed to interact with AI technology in a self-determined and rational manner.” Pinski and Benlian (2023) provided a definition of General AI literacy, describing it ashumans “socio-technical competence consisting of knowledge regarding human and AI actors in human-AI interaction, knowledge of the AI process steps, that is, input, processing, and output, and experience in AI interaction.” Laupichler et al. (2023) define the concept of AI literacy as “competencies that include basic knowledge and analytical evaluation of AI, in addition to the critical use of AI applications by ordinary non-expert individuals.” They did not include (programming skills) in their definitions because they considered them a separate set of competencies beyond AI literacy (Laupichler et al., 2023).

Recently, several studies have developed AI literacy frameworks for education (Kong et al., 2023; Ng et al., 2021b; Wong et al., 2020). AI literacy does not require detailed technical expertise in AI applications. However, it spans four dimensions: understanding and comprehension of AI, practical application of AI, assessment of AI applications, and ethical considerations of AI (L. Zhao et al., 2022).

Long and Magerko (2020) provided a conceptual framework of AI literacy composed of five overarching themes derived from the literature (Long & Magerko, 2020).Wang et al. (2023) proposed a theoretical framework to conceptualize AI Literacy, comprising four constructs: Usage, defined as the ability to exploit AI technology to proficiently perform tasks; Awareness, the ability to identify and comprehend AI systems during interactions; Evaluation, the capacity to critically analyze and assess AI applications and their outcomes; and Ethics, which involves recognizing responsibilities and risks associated with using AI systems. 

Despite the growing body of research on AI Literacy, significant gaps remain (Lu et al., 2024). Most studies have relied on qualitative methodologies or small-scale quantitative analyses (Ng et al., 2021b, 2023; Schiavo et al., 2024; Su et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2024), limiting our understanding of the broader implementation and effectiveness of AI education (Ng et al., 2021b, 2023; Schiavo et al., 2024; Su et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2024). A significant majority (75 %) of the papers pertaining to AI literacy have focused on students (Al-Abdullatif, 2024; Almatrafi et al., 2024; Ng et al., 2023), while few have focused on student (An et al., 2023; Liang et al., 2023).

Therefore, student must understand what AI is and what it can do inside and outside the classroom, and engage with AI technologies in a self-determined manner. These sets of skills, including understanding, utilizing, and integrating various AI technologies to enhance teaching methods, facilitate student learning, and improve overall educational outcomes, are commonly referred to as AI literacy (Carolus et al., 2023).

4.2.2. Academic Misconduct (AM)
Academic misconduct refers to behavior that violates the standards of honesty, fairness, and responsibility expected in academic work (Stephens, 2019). It has been operationalized in the literature as a multidimensional behavioral construct spanning collusion (e.g., unpermitted collaboration on individual assignments), misuse of resources (e.g., plagiarism, unauthorized use of materials), and fraud and contract cheating (Perry et al., 2026; Rettinger et al., 2024; Stephens et al., 2024). The emergence of GenAI tools has introduced new and complex forms of misconduct—including submitting AI-generated content as one's own work and using AI paraphrasing tools to disguise plagiarism—that blur the lines between legitimate AI assistance and academic fraud (Gruenhagen et al., 2024; Xia et al., 2024).

Scholars have approached academic misconduct from multiple perspectives. Stephens (2019) characterized the problem as epidemic in scale, noting that the majority of tertiary students globally report having cheated at least once during their studies, with this rate remaining remarkably stable across five decades and numerous countries. From a moral-psychological perspective, Stephens (2018) demonstrated that misconduct is driven not primarily by moral ignorance but by moral disengagement—students who cheat frequently possess accurate moral knowledge but use self-exculpatory rationalizations, such as blaming the instructor or normalizing the behavior among peers, to neutralize their ethical standards in specific situations. At a multi-level theoretical level, Perry et al. (2026) found that moral attitudes, institutional climate, and peer norms each contribute unique variance to misconduct, and that no single explanatory factor is sufficient on its own, confirming that academic misconduct is a multiply determined behavioral outcome.

Prevalence data underscore the scale of the problem. A nationwide New Zealand survey of 4,493 undergraduates found that approximately two-thirds (64.8%) reported at least one act of academic misconduct in the preceding year, with unauthorized collaboration being the most common behavior (Stephens et al., 2024). That survey incorporated the first items capturing AI-facilitated misconduct and found an initial 14.8% self-reported prevalence rate—likely a significant underestimate given that data collection concluded just two months before ChatGPT's public release (Stephens et al., 2024). Subsequent studies have confirmed that AI-facilitated misconduct is rapidly increasing and that many students do not recognize unauthorized AI assistance as a breach of academic integrity, highlighting the urgent need for research on the individual factors that shape these misconduct decisions (Gruenhagen et al., 2024; Lund et al., 2025).

Despite extensive research, significant gaps remain. Most existing studies predate the widespread availability of GenAI tools and therefore do not capture its most emergent forms of dishonest behavior (Lund et al., 2025; Perkins et al., 2024). Moreover, while moral attitudes and peer norms have been identified as predictors, the role of AI literacy—specifically whether higher AI literacy leads to more ethical decision-making around GenAI use—has not been empirically tested (Ng et al., 2021b; Stephens et al., 2024). Research from Chinese higher education contexts is particularly sparse, leaving open how cultural assessment norms and institutional factors moderate the AI literacy–misconduct relationship (Brown & Wang, 2016; Zhao et al., 2020). The most psychometrically validated instrument for measuring academic misconduct is the McCabe-ICAI Academic Misconduct Instrument (MIAMI; Rettinger et al., 2024), which assesses three behavioral factors—Collusion, Misuse of Resources, and Fraud/Contract Cheating—validated in large-scale multinational samples and supplemented with AI-specific misconduct items in recent adaptations (Perry et al., 2026; Stephens et al., 2024).

4.2.3. Academic Learning Motivation (ALM)
Academic motivation refers to the internal and external factors that drive students to learn and perform academically (Ryan & Deci, 2020). Academic motivation—whether intrinsic, driven by internal satisfaction and interest, or extrinsic, driven by external rewards like grades and recognition—also significantly influences how engaged students are in their academic work (Ryan & Deci, 2020; Shin & Bolkan, 2021).

Suggesting one's tendency to put effort into achieving goals, people's ALM determines the choices they make, strategies they adopt, and commitment they devote (Dörnyei 1998; Ryan and Deci 2000; Zhao and Huang 2024). In an EFL setting, learners who are more motivated or inspired towards a goal tend to be more engaged in learning, and vice versa (Li et al. 2022). LM helps people to initiate and maintain the learning process, which involves devotion, aspiration, and engagement to achieve goals; it also facilitates the formation of favourable attitudes towards learning (Dörnyei 1998; Li et al. 2022).

To categorise motivation, Gardner and Lambert (1972) proposed the well-known dichotomy of integrative and instrumental motivations. Integrative motivation indicates one's impetus to learn a target language for social–emotional purposes, such as communication with members of the target language community; whereas instrumental motivation indicates one's learning for pragmatic purposes (e.g., examination, job opportunities, better education). Later, motivation was categorised into intrinsic and extrinsic types, garnering widespread consensus among scholars. Learners who expect to derive satisfaction, enjoyment, and self-determination are typically intrinsically motivated; those who attempt to obtain extrinsic rewards (such as good grades) or avoid punishment through learning are extrinsically motivated. In addition, intrinsically motivated people may perform a task driven by external pressures or promises of reward (Dörnyei 1998).

4.2.4. Self-Regulation Learning (SRL)
Self-Regulation Learning refers to learners’ ability to actively set goals, monitor progress, and adjust strategies to achieve desired learning outcomes (Zimmerman, 2002). Pintrich (2000) articulated SRL as “an active, constructive process whereby learners set goals for their learning and then attempt to monitor, regulate, and control their cognition, motivation, and behavior, guided and constrained by their goals and the contextual features in the environment” (p. 453). GenAI-SRL describes how learners use GenAI systems to plan, monitor, and evaluate their learning while also managing their interactions with these intelligent tools (Banihashem et al., 2025; Chang & Sun, 2024).

SRL, involving the strategies students use to plan, monitor, and evaluate their learning, further enhances academic engagement by empowering students to take control of their educational journeys (Schunk & Zimmerman, 2012; Teng & Zhang, 2020). Students with strong SRL skills not only demonstrate greater persistence and stress management in learning (Namaziandost et al., 2023) but also eﬀectively leverage external support—such as teachers, peers, or technological tools—when dealing with complex or unfamiliar learning tasks (Xia et al., 2023). 

With the rapid proliferation of AI, particularly generative AI, AI tools are increasingly used to support students’ SRL processes. Empirical studies show that AI applications such as chatbots, adaptive feedback systems, and intelligent tutoring systems can provide personalized support during the forethought, performance, and reﬂection phases of SRL (Ng et al., 2024c; Wang and Lin, 2023) For example, the SRLbot developed by Ng et al. (2024b) oﬀers dynamic learning suggestions based on students’ real-time learning states, while study demonstrates that ChatGPT can signiﬁcantly enhance learners’ planning and self-monitoring during the reﬂection stage (Fan et al., 2025).

A small but growing body of research has begun to examine the direct relationship between AI literacy and SRL. Ng et al. (2021) emphasized in their review that AI literacy involves not only technical operation skills but also the abilities to critically evaluate and ethically use AI—dimensions closely associated with SRL sub-processes such as goal setting, monitoring, and strategy adjustment. Furthermore, Zhang and Chen (2025) identiﬁed four learner proﬁles with diﬀerent patterns in a survey of 1,704 Chinese university students. Their ﬁndings revealed that “AI-Inclined” learners, despite having relatively high AI literacy, tended to over-rely on AI due to weaker SRL, whereas “Potential” learners displayed a mutually reinforcing developmental pattern between AI literacy and SRL. These results highlight that diﬀerences in AI literacy are crucial for understanding students’ SRL performance.

Recent studies have also examined the potential synergy between SRL theory and AI (Jin et al., 2023; Molenaar, 2022; Molenaar et al., 2023). However, the current body of research on the relationship between AI and SRL has primarily focused on how AI supports and enhances SRL. For example, Lim et al. (2023) explored the application of rule-based AI systems for real-time measurement and support of students’ SRL. Their findings revealed that personalized scaffolding, facilitated by real-time analysis, significantly enhances students’ SRL. Similarly, several studies have investigated the role of SRLSs in AIED. Despite these contributions, a significant gap remains in the literature regarding how SRLSs specifically influence AI literacy. Furthermore, although SRL has been shown to improve students’ literacy competencies (Anthonysamy et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2021; Z. Zhang et al., 2023), few studies have investigated the intersection of SRL and AI literacy. 


4.2.5. AI Literacy TPACK Framework and Molenaar's Hybrid Human-AI Regulation Model
This study's theory is rooted in two frameworks that jointly explain the pathways linking AI literacy to academic misconduct: Ng et al.'s (2021b) AI Literacy TPACK Framework and Molenaar's (2022a, 2022b) Hybrid Human-AI Regulation Model. Together, these provide the theoretical architecture for the proposed serial mediation model without reference to moral-psychological mechanisms, consistent with this study's focus on the motivational and self-regulatory pathways of AI-related academic conduct.

Molenaar's (2022a, 2022b) model conceptualizes learning with AI as a process of shared, fluctuating regulatory control between the learner and the AI system, in which the degree of control transferred to the learner depends on the learner's own regulatory skill and understanding of the AI tool. This framework accounts for why students with differing levels of AI literacy engage in fundamentally different patterns of self-regulated learning when using GenAI tools: students with higher AI literacy are better positioned to retain regulatory control—setting goals, monitoring progress, and evaluating AI-generated output—rather than ceding this control to the AI system, a pattern associated with reduced academic motivation and eroded self-regulation.

This study adopts the AI Ethics and Evaluating and Creating AI dimensions of Ng et al.'s (2021b) framework as the conceptual link to Molenaar's hybrid regulation model: students with greater competency in evaluating and ethically applying AI are hypothesized to retain greater regulatory control over their learning process, thereby sustaining stronger academic motivation and more effective self-regulated learning, and consequently engaging in less academic misconduct. According to these two integrated frameworks, AI Literacy has a significant impact on Academic Misconduct through the sequential mediation of Academic Motivation and Self-Regulation Learning (Ng et al., 2021b; Molenaar, 2022a, 2022b).

4.2.6. The Relationship Between AI Literacy and Academic Misconduct: The Mediating Role of Academic Motivation and Self-Regulation Learning
First, AI literacy is a crucial factor affecting students' academic integrity in AI-enriched environments (Gruenhagen et al., 2024; Lund et al., 2025). Recent studies have highlighted the significant role of AI literacy in shaping responsible AI use in educational settings (Long & Magerko, 2020; Ng et al., 2021b; Schiavo et al., 2024). Students with higher AI literacy possess a more accurate understanding of the capabilities and ethical boundaries of GenAI tools, making them better equipped to distinguish legitimate AI assistance from unauthorized substitution of their own academic work (Lund et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2023). Gruenhagen et al. (2024) found that students who did not understand the implications of AI use for assessment were substantially more likely to report using AI tools dishonestly. This reflects the significant influence of AI literacy levels on academic misconduct engagement (Perry et al., 2026; Stephens et al., 2024).

Second, Stephens's (2018) study emphasized the causal effect of motivation and self-regulated learning on academic misconduct and the need to investigate the mediating role of motivation and self-regulated learning in AI literacy and academic misconduct. Self-regulated learning and learning motivation are interdependent and cannot be understood separately (Huang & Derakhshan, 2025). There is a significant difference in self-regulated learning power between different types of students, with those with higher levels of intellectual literacy showing greater self-regulated learning ability (D. Wang et al., 2026). Moreover, learning motivation significantly influences college learners' use of AI in self-directed learning (Huang & Derakhshan, 2025).

Third, the results of Wang et al. (2025) study confirmed the positive correlation between SRL and college students' AI literacy predicted by previous studies. Consequently, recent research has indicated a positive impact of AI literacy on self-regulated learning (Bećirović et al., 2026; He & Wang, 2026; X. Wang & Zhang, 2026). Empirical research shows that AI literacy can significantly predict self-regulated learning (He & Wang, 2026). Students with more advanced AI literacy profiles consistently demonstrated stronger SRL competencies, indicating a clear positive association between AI literacy level and SRL (D. Wang et al., 2026).

Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H1. AI Literacy has a significant negative effect on Academic Misconduct.
H2. AI Literacy has a significant positive effect on Academic Motivation.
H3. AI Literacy has a significant positive effect on Self-Regulation Learning.
H4. Academic Motivation has a significant positive effect on Academic Misconduct.
H5. Self-Regulation Learning has a significant negative effect on Academic Misconduct.
H6. Academic Motivation has a significant negative effect on Self-Regulation Learning.
H7. Academic Motivation mediates the effect of AI Literacy on Self-Regulation Learning.
H8. Academic Motivation mediates the effect of AI Literacy on Academic Misconduct.
H9. Self-Regulation Learning mediates the effect of AI Literacy on Academic Misconduct.

4.3. Methodology

4.3.1. Sample
Hancock and Feng’s (2026) nmax calculator was applied to evaluate whether the sample size was sufficient for SEM. Assuming a minimum standardized path coefficient of γ = 0.20 and moderate multicollinearity among predictors (ρc = 0.30), the required minimum sample size is 213. Considering an anticipated longitudinal attrition rate of 20% across the three waves of data collection, consistent with prior three-wave AI literacy research (Liu et al., 2026), the final required sample size at T1 will be at least 267 participants to ensure a fully matched, valid sample of no fewer than 213 by T3.

4.3.2. Demographics
A total of X university students from various universities in China willingly participated in this study.

4.3.3. Measures
Student AI literacy will be measured using Zhao et al.'s (2022) scale, which includes 20 items assessing four dimensions of student AI literacy: Knowing and Understanding AI (KUAI), Applying AI (AAI), Evaluating AI Application (EAIA), and AI Ethics (AIE). To capture both permitted and prohibited forms of student AI use, this measure will be supplemented with the Academic AI Usage Inventory, retaining the Constructive usage and Irresponsible usage subscales and the Overreliant usage subscale as indicators of ethical versus unethical academic AI use (Elhami Athar, 2025). The AAUI is a 17-item self-report measure specifically developed for this study to assess three distinct profiles of academic AI usage: Constructive, Overreliant, and Irresponsible. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 ("Strongly Disagree") to 5 ("Strongly Agree") and load onto three subscales of Constructive (4 items), Overreliant (7 items), and Irresponsible (6 items).

Academic misconduct will be measured using the RAINZ-S scale (Stephens et al., 2024), developed from the nationwide Research on Academic Integrity in New Zealand (RAINZ) survey of 4,493 undergraduates. Participants will be asked to indicate how often, in the past 12 months, they engaged in a variety of academically dishonest behaviors, supplemented with AI-facilitated misconduct items (e.g., submitting AI-generated content as one's own work) consistent with the original instrument's later adaptations.

Academic motivation will be measured using the Academic Motivation Scale (AMS; Vallerand et al., 1992), which assesses intrinsic motivation, extrinsic motivation, and amotivation toward academic work. The AMS has been widely validated across diverse student populations with adequate reliability and construct validity.

Self-regulated learning will be measured using GenAI-SRL, adapted from Lai and Gu (2011) and Lai et al. (2024) to reflect how learners engage in self-regulated learning with GenAI tools. Six items will be used to measure this construct (e.g., "When using GenAI tools for academic work, I constantly monitored my learning progress").

4.3.4. Data collection
Data for this study were collected using a three-wave longitudinal tracking design. A self-reported method was employed to survey students' AI literacy, academic misconduct, conceptions of assessment, and academic integrity at three time points. The research team implemented strategies such as securing college support, providing guidance, and offering appropriate incentives to effectively control attrition rates. To reduce common method bias associated with self-reported data, no identifying information will be collected across the three waves. Participants are provided with private spaces to complete the questionnaires, sufficient time for completion, and assurance that the data would be used solely for research purposes, thereby ensuring anonymity, confidentiality, and data validity. Sample attrition in this study was primarily due to invalid responses. Invalid questionnaires were excluded based on criteria such as substantial missing data, patterned responses, abnormal completion time, or illogical answers.

This study employs a time-lagged research design for data collection and conducted three waves of questionnaire surveys to reduce the potential influence of common method bias to some extent (Yu et al., 2021). The interval between each wave of data collection is two months. Each questionnaires will be distributed and collected on-site through an online survey platform. To ensure longitudinal matching across the three waves of data collection, participants will be assigned a unique anonymous identification number (ID), which was retained in T2 and T3 to ensure rigor and traceability.

During data processing, only questionnaires that are successfully matched across all three waves and fully complete are retained for analysis. Responses that could not be matched longitudinally or contained missing data were excluded. Based on the anticipated attrition rate, the final valid, fully matched sample retained across all three waves is expected to meet or exceed the required minimum sample size of 213 participants determined by the nmax approach. Finally, we use a three-wave time-lagged design to keep a temporal separation between predictors and outcomes (Podsakoff et al., 2012).

4.3.5. Data Analysis Procedure
The study employee a structural equation modelling (SEM) approach using RStudio (version 2026.07.1). Data are collected via an online survey and analyzed using RStudio software. RStudio will be used to analyze the data through a Structural Equation Modelling (SEM) approach, including the measurement model, structural model, and Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA). 

First, the variables are examined for outliers using z-values. To describe the data, we utilize the “psych” and “dplyr” packages (Revelle, 2026; Wickham et al., 2014). For the main analysis, which involves Structural Equation Modeling (SEM), we will employ the “lavaan” package and the “semPlot” package (Epskamp, 2015; Revelle, 2026). The Lavaan package will be used to define the measurement model and to conduct multigroup CFA and main effects SEM models, while the Modsem package will be used to test the multigroup interaction SEM model (Rosseel, 2012; Slupphaug et al., 2025). Model fits are evaluated with the robust comparative fit index (CFI), robust root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR).

CFA will be conducted with initial data collected from students to assess the structural or factorial validity of the Questionnaire. CFA is chosen because of its unique ability to explore the causal relationships among latent and observed variables in pre-defined, theory-derived models (Tavakol & Wetzel, 2020). CFA was conducted from the lavaan package in R (Rosseel, 2012). Before conducting CFA, data screening procedures will be conducted, including the examination of missing data, outliers, and univariate and multivariate normality.

SEM will be conducted through the following steps: model specification, model identification, model estimation, model testing, and model modification (Schumacker & Lomax, 2015). The model is specified and identified based on the conceptual framework outlined in the literature section. A bootstrapping method with robust maximum likelihood estimation will be used for model estimation. Robust maximum likelihood estimation with bootstrapped standard errors was selected, rather than PLS-SEM, because this combination maintains stable and unbiased parameter estimates even at moderate sample sizes, without the interpretive limitations associated with PLS-based composite modeling (Schumacker & Lomax, 2015). Initially, the measurement model estimations will confirm that all factor loadings are significant. Subsequently, model fit indices will be examined. We use Chi-square, CFI, TLI, RMSEA, and SRMR as fit indices. For a good model fit, CFI and TLI values should be 0.90 or above, while RMSEA and SRMR values should be below 0.08 (Schumacker & Lomax, 2015). The mediation analysis using structural equation modeling (SEM) with the lavaan package in RStudio is utilized to examine the direct, indirect, and total effects. After evaluating the path coefficients, the results will be reported.
