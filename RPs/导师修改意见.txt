第三封信：
Hi David, 
Perhaps most importantly, thesis projects under my supervision must be consists of three or four distinct (but related) studies.  Collectively, they should tell a story, each building on what has come before. 
At present, you have proposed a single study, using cross-sectional data to test for mediation effects. This used to be good enough, but not anymore. If you want to test for mediation, you should be plan to collect at least three waves of data. 
Conceptually, it's not clear to me how you plan to create the  composite variable, "academic integrity", from moral unacceptability and moral disengagement, nor do I think you've suggested a very nuanced measure of AI use. I think you should assess both ethical (permitted) AI use and unethical (prohibited) AI use.  And perhaps not concern yourself with other forms of academic misconduct.
Finally, please revise this proposal as a proposed thesis project. At present, you use the past tense as though the study was already been completed.

Further to Prof Jason’s comments, you need to estimate a required sample size in any cross-sectional study. There are a number of ways to do this but the simple approach is to use Greg Hancock’s nmax approach.
Hancock, G. R., & Feng, Y. (2025). nmax and the quest to restore caution, integrity, and practicality to the sample size planning process. Psychological Methods. https://doi.org/10.1037/met0000776
Second, I accept that PLS-SEM is useful when N is low. But there is no need to rely on PLS when maximum likelihood works very well once N>400. So I would ask you to remove that approach.

Advancing assessment and feedback: What works in the AI world to ensure learning and integrity.
Enrollment information: NZ Citizens, NZ Permanent Residents, International
Introduction
AI tools have become prevalent in all levels of education. But unchecked use threatens the integrity of qualifications and the morality of learners, let alone the environmental impact. How do students use AI, how do we ensure integrity and morality, how can society have confidence that our graduates can do what we say, and does this matter?
This opportunity builds on our previous work, in which we have found that students cannot tell if technology sourced feedback is accurate, but they sure don't like negative wording. We have found that more academically able students tend to get weaker feedback from tutors, which they tend to ignore. 
In this opportunity students will be expected to investigate what interventions can create integrity and morality in the AI-learner relationship.
